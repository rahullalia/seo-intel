# Technical SEO Calls (Denroy & Elliot) — Synthesis

Source: 15 HeyTony Insiders live Q&A / site-audit calls, Feb–Jul 2026, hosted by Denroy (technical SEO lead, ~6 years with Matt) and Elliot (technical SEO, ~2 years, local-SEO/GBP specialist, covered weekly calls during Denroy's paternity leave). Format throughout: raise-hand queue, live screen-share audits on attendees' real sites. Content below is deduplicated across all 15 calls — the same rules (one-keyword-per-page, H1 hierarchy, GSC-first diagnosis, etc.) recurred constantly; each is stated once here in its strongest/most complete form, with every number and tool name preserved.

---

## Cross-Cutting Rules

- **One keyword per page, site-wide, no exceptions.** Stated as the general architecture rule on nearly every call: "It all goes back to one keyword per page." Trying to rank one page for several keywords means it won't rank for any of them, or ranks lower for all of them. Applies to a single page's heading structure AND to how a whole site's pages/nav are organized. The single service that makes the most revenue becomes the homepage's target keyword; every other service gets its own dedicated page.
- **Content on the page is the #1 ranking factor** — ranked above backend items like schema markup, meta description, and title tags (which are still required, just secondary/lower priority). "In the past schema was much more important. Nowadays, not as much." What matters more than raw word count is whether content adds real depth/value to the topic.
- **Google Search Console is "the number one most important tool for SEO"** — ranked above keyword-research tools and even above Google Analytics, because it's the only concrete, real (non-estimated) traffic/ranking data source, direct from Google. Google Analytics pairs with it (GSC = how people find you; GA = what they do once there). Set up GSC *before* making any keyword-targeting decision — "without Search Console, you really are just shooting in the dark."
- **Always internally link, across the board, continuously** — not as an afterthought. This is the single most-repeated fix across every call, for orphan pages, stuck indexing, cannibalization risk, and general authority-building alike.
- **Heading styling never matters for SEO.** H1 does not have to be the visually largest text on the page. Only two things matter: (1) the text must be visible, not hidden, and (2) headings must appear in correct sequential order (H1 first, then H2s, etc.) as the literal first tag structurally, not just visually.
- **Audit-tool warnings are frequently false positives or cache lag, not real ranking problems.** Always cross-check a live/non-caching source (GSC, or a non-caching extension like Detailed SEO) before spending time "fixing" something that already works. Recurring lag windows quoted across calls: 5–12 hours, 1–24 hours, "a week or two" for Google-side report lag after a real fix (redirects, sitemaps). A persisting GSC report entry after a real-world fix is not a penalty signal.
- **Never self-host video, on any platform, ever** — always embed via YouTube, Vimeo, Daily Motion, etc., even with a strong dedicated server: "even then YouTube is just better."
- **Never link out to a direct competitor, for any reason.** Mentioning them by name or comparing them in a table is fine; hyperlinking to them is not.
- **AI (Claude/ChatGPT/Gemini) is good for orientation, drafting, and sanity-checks — never the final decision-maker on keyword/architecture calls.** Repeated explicitly: "make the decision not necessarily based on what Claude's saying, but on just what the actual data points are." ChatGPT specifically "does make a lot of mistakes" on SEO heading/content architecture (produced a keyword-stuffed H1 for one attendee) — fine for implementation tasks (writing missing code, generating schema) but not strategy.
- **Every business tool/team member repeatedly flagged uncertainty rather than guessing live** — several diagnoses were explicitly left open with a research-and-follow-up-in-the-community commitment rather than an improvised answer (canonical-tag edge cases, Showit sitemap failures, Cloudflare's SEO impact, GSC's "alternate page with proper canonical tag" category).

## Heading Tags (H1/H2/H3)

### Core Rules
- **Exactly one H1 per page** (two H1s allowed only if a site has genuinely separate mobile/desktop layouts). H2 can be used as many times as needed, including back-to-back with no text between.
- **H1 must literally be the first tag on the page**, not just visually prominent — an audit found a Shopify H1 sitting on a "limited time offer" element far down the page; it had to move to the top. Google's crawler reads top-down, so H1 position signals what the page is about before anything else.
- **H1 must contain the exact target keyword, no exceptions.** H2s should contain the keyword in "all" of them or at least a "healthy majority" — don't force it into literally every H2 if there are many (e.g. 10 H2s), that reads as stuffing. H3 is optional/flexible but still encouraged.
- **Never put the business/brand name in the H1** — remove it; the brand name is already visible in the `<title>` tag and the URL, so the H1 should be pure keyword/topic.
- **Never skip heading levels** (e.g. H2 straight to H4 with no H3) — always promote the skipped-level headings up to the correct sequential position.
- **Keywords are weighted heavier in heading tags than in regular paragraph copy** — always work the target keyword (or a variation, or a local modifier) into headings; avoid generic non-descriptive headings like "Learn More."
- **Heading hierarchy logic:** H1 = the single broadest topic the page is about (e.g. "Chicago newborn photographer"). H2 = supporting sub-topics adding context/depth under the H1 (e.g. family, maternity). H3 = more specific detail under the H2 above it (e.g. "affordable newborn"). Each level should add "more depth, more context, more specification."
- **Keyword-form consistency:** if a keyword has two commonly-searched forms (e.g. "Med Spa" vs. "Medspa"), pick ONE and use it consistently across H1, title, meta description, and H2s — use the other variant only in body copy, never in structural tags.

### Duplicate H1s and Mis-tagged Elements
- **Diagnosing duplicate H1s** (e.g. Shopify/page-builder auto-generating a second H1 alongside an authored one): check Search Console to see which of the two headings' keyword variant is already ranking higher; keep that one as the true H1 (usually the shorter/more abbreviated version). **Do NOT delete the other text/content** — demote its tag instead (H1 → H2 or plain text), preserving the copy while resolving the technical duplicate. On Shopify: edit the theme's `product.liquid` file (or `theme.liquid`, Online Store → Edit Code) and change the second `<h1>` down a level directly in code.
- **H1 hard-wired to the site logo (Shopify theme, no developer on staff):** if editing/removing the existing H1 risks the theme's universal styling overriding it, add a *second* custom H1 tag via the code editor containing the target keyword — a redundant but functional stopgap. In parallel, escalate to Shopify support to ask why the H1 is tied to the logo and whether that connection can be removed (the real fix). Shopify's built-in AI assistant, **Sidekick**, can also be asked to help set/guess a code-level fix when no developer is available.
- **Live heading-cleanup pass:** go through a page and reclassify anything styled as a heading tag (H2/H3) or a button/title-tag that isn't really a heading — stat callouts, trust badges, list items — down to plain paragraph text with a larger font size instead. Use a page-audit browser extension to see the real tag structure.
- **Scrambled heading order** (H2/H3/H4 out of sequence, cart/footer elements incorrectly tagged as headings) on an old, many-times-updated theme: go through headings one by one and correct each tag; on a sufficiently old/patched theme it may be faster to rebuild the page than to patch it.

### Rewriting Headings for Keyword Fit
- **Fold the keyword more directly into an existing heading:** e.g. "You're trusted Med Spa experts in Scottsdale" → "You're trusted Med Spa Scottsdale experts."
- **H1 rewrite process (live demo):** if the current H1 is a long stuffed phrase repeating the keyword (e.g. "Beaverton Kids Art Camp Creative..." repeating "art camp" twice — produced by following ChatGPT's suggested heading breakdown), keep only the target keyword phrase as the H1 and move the rest of that sentence down into an H2 or plain paragraph. Restyle the new shorter H1 with CSS if you liked how the longer version looked visually.
- **Subheading keyword pass:** scan every subheading for a natural spot to reinsert the local/target keyword — e.g. "Camp Schedule and Camp Themes" → "Beaverton Camp Schedule and Camp Themes"; for a multi-location pest-control page: "Why choose [Brand]" → "Why choose us for pest control in [Location]"; "Kind words from our customers" → "Kind words from our customers in [Location]"; "Learn more about spider control" → "Learn more about spider control in [Location]."
- **Fixing an over-optimized/redundant headline:** e.g. "Metal roofing is the last roof you'll ever need" (flagged as awkward repetition of "roof/roofing") → reword naturally while still containing location + service keyword: "Why a metal roof is important in Vancouver." Google treats close semantic variants (e.g. "metal roofing in Vancouver" vs. "Vancouver metal roofing contractor") as equivalent — natural phrasing beats forced, literal-repeat wording, which reads as over-optimization.
- **Cannibalization diagnosis:** two pages are cannibalizing each other if both are fundamentally about the same core keyword/topic even in slightly different phrasing (e.g. "Chicago Newborn Photography" homepage vs. a second page "Newborn Photography Chicago" — same premise, different word order = cannibalizing). Location-specific pages are NOT cannibalizing as long as the location term clearly differentiates them and is folded directly into the H1 (e.g. "Skokie newborn," not just "newborn photography").
- **Audience/keyword mismatch fix** (nanny agency case: site historically attracted job applicants, not paying families): go back to keyword research to confirm the correct target keyword for the *paying* audience (e.g. test "hire a nanny in San Francisco" instead of "find a trusted nanny"), re-optimize the money page around it, then build supporting blog posts answering that audience's real questions, each linking back to the money page with multiple CTAs.

## Title Tags & Meta Descriptions

- **Every page meant to rank needs a title tag + a meta description + exactly one H1.** Leaving title/meta blank tells Google to auto-generate an excerpt and signals the page isn't focused around a keyword — "don't push it as high."
- **Keyword prominence rule:** the sooner a keyword appears in the title tag, meta description, and body content as a whole, the more weight Google/crawlers assign it. Applies to meta descriptions specifically too: keyword position (earlier = more weight) matters more than mere presence, for any search query, not just a specific niche.
- **Meta description exact-match rule:** keep the target keyword phrase as close to exact-match as possible — don't insert extra connecting words (e.g. "in") even if it reads more naturally, because that changes the keyword string. (Title/meta description are explicitly allowed to "push the boundaries" of natural phrasing more than body copy for this reason.)
- **Every page/product needs a unique meta description — never duplicate one across the site,** even across near-identical product variants (e.g. 18-karat vs. 14-karat vs. 24-karat gold engagement rings, each its own long-tail keyword). At catalog scale (e.g. 100 product designs), track keyword-to-page assignments in a spreadsheet to avoid accidental duplication; achieve variation by reordering emphasis (materials-first vs. design-name-first) as long as no two are identical.
- **Yoast overriding page-builder meta descriptions:** if Yoast is set to pull an excerpt from page content, it silently overrides whatever meta description you set in Elementor/any builder. Fix: open Yoast's own meta description box on that specific page and set it there directly — that's what search engines actually read. Must be repeated per page.
- **A missing period after an abbreviation** (e.g. "St" vs. "St." in a title/meta, caused by an AI-generated description running out of characters) does NOT affect actual Google search results — treat both forms as the same keyword. Note: some tools (Semrush specifically) treat them as different strings for keyword-density reporting, so you may need to run two separate reports in that tool.
- **Squarespace auto-appending business name to titles:** even if the title looks correct in settings (e.g. 41 characters), the business name can still get appended in previews/audit tools via a "dynamic text" option inside the title tag's HTML/theme code. Treat as optional/informational rather than a hard fix — Google typically strips the appended name in actual SERPs anyway.
- **Shopify auto-appending store name to titles despite manual edits:** check Store Settings (Marketplace / Store / SEO — one of the three) for an auto-append-store-name toggle, or look for a `[store name]` shortcode directly inside the theme's title tag field and delete it. Known behavior on certain **Shopify Dawn theme** versions.

## Schema Markup

- **Priority:** lower than content — "if you write good content, then that matters a lot more than just having a schema markup on the page." Was more important in the past, less so now.
- **Yoast's schema toggle** auto-generates standard/generic schema across every page type (blog posts, pages, service pages, homepage) with zero extra setup — turn it on regardless of niche, it's essentially free. For anything more specific (reviews, prices, FAQ blocks) you need a third-party plugin/tool on top.
- **Manual schema markup, step by step:**
  1. Search "schema markup generator" (any generator works — pick by UI friendliness; Google's own older built-in tool is less user-friendly than third-party generators).
  2. Pick the content category (Product, Article/Blog, etc.).
  3. **Product:** fill name, image URL, brand, product description (include the target keyword here), price, price currency, lowest value.
  4. **Article/Blog:** fill type, URL, headline, image URL, short description (reuse the meta description or a variant, include the keyword).
  5. Copy the auto-generated JSON-LD script from the tool.
  6. Paste it into the page's **header** — in Rank Math this is a dropdown menu in the blog/page editor; if the theme has no native header field, use Yoast's header field or a "header extension" plugin.
  7. **Header placement is non-negotiable, same rule for blog posts and web pages:** the header loads before the rest of the page, so Google's crawler reads the script first — same mechanism as an analytics tracking script.
- If Rank Math is supposedly generating schema but a site-analysis tool still reports "no schema markup," a manual JSON-LD paste is a legitimate workaround/supplement — don't assume the plugin alone guarantees detection.
- **FAQ schema generation via AI:** describe your on-page question + answer text to Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini and ask it to generate the schema markup, then paste that markup into the page's backend code-injection block. Confirmed as the correct method on a live call.
- **Schema is only a real risk when the data is wrong or misleading** — that's a genuine Google penalty (example: wrong business hours listed = "slight penalty"; schema claiming "restaurant" when the business is actually digital marketing/PR = "major penalty"). Missing/incomplete but accurate schema fields are NOT penalized. FAQ schema specifically: skipping it carries no penalty, only worth the manual dev effort for high-competition keywords.
- **Diagnosing schema errors flagged by Google's Rich Results Test:** open the tool, check each flagged item against what's actually rendered in the schema block, not just the error label — several "critical" flags can be non-issues if the real data (name, image, etc.) is present elsewhere in the schema; often a platform rendering artifact (Squarespace's schema-rendering quirks reportedly unfixed for "five, six years" specifically because real-world impact nets to zero) rather than a real gap.
- **Hand-coded FAQ schema drawing a bot swarm:** if a legitimate FAQ schema is winning AI-search visibility but also attracting heavy AI/training-bot crawl traffic, the fix is NOT to remove the schema — take the code snippet to Claude, explain that bots are combing the site, and ask it to add a rule that blocks the bad-bot crawling while preserving the FAQ schema. (Real case: bot traffic spiked to 150,000 visits/day on a site that normally gets ~40/day; required a cybersecurity consultant, a full hosting-IP reset, and adopting Cloudflare — but "Google's AI is now recommending me because of those [FAQs].")
- **Local business schema for a multi-city service-area business:** put the main schema (matching the Google Business Profile) on the homepage, listing every service-area city. Add a separate, page-specific schema on each individual city/location page mentioning only that one city. Multiple separate physical locations (multiple GBPs) require a full separate schema per profile/location.

## Keyword Density, Word Count & On-Page Benchmarking

- **No fixed keyword-density number — always benchmark against what's currently ranking.** Google the target keyword, note usage counts on the top 3 ranking pages, then use it once or twice more than the top count (they mention it once → you mention it twice; they mention it 20 times → you mention it ~21). **Don't wildly exceed competitors** (e.g. stuffing 20 extra instances beyond the top competitor) — that reads to Google as manipulation, not helpfulness.
- **Low-difficulty/local keywords often need only 2–3 mentions**, not 10–12, to hit the top spot — one worked case ranked top 3 for "mechanic" using only 3 mentions vs. 0 for all three competitors.
- **Word count benchmarking:** pull the top 3 Google results for the target keyword, note their word counts, and target roughly 10% more than whichever has the highest count (competitor at 1,000 words → write ~1,100; or "if they're at 2,000 words, you can put your word count at 2,005" — just one-upping slightly). **Don't overshoot into 2–3x territory** — that risks reading as over-optimized even though you're still ahead of the top ranker on the metric.
- **When a page significantly overshoots competitors in aggregate keyword count**, don't judge the aggregate — break it down **per individual keyword variation** (e.g. "auto repair" vs. "automotive repair" as separate metrics) and trim/add per-variation rather than uniformly.
- **One target keyword per blog post is sufficient** — it must appear in the title, the URL slug, and early in the body copy (second sentence, in one worked example) — no need to run the whole title/content through a keyword tool.
- **Density cap alternative framing:** check how many times competitors use the exact keyword; if they use it ~3 times, cap yourself around 4–5 uses at most — don't go overboard chasing volume.
- **Exact-match placement rule:** put the exact keyword (no added words) in H1, H2, and a couple of times through the body copy; variations of the keyword can be used as many times as feels natural elsewhere in body copy.
- **After word count and density are dialed in, benchmark internal links and backlinks the same way:** if a competing page has 5 backlinks, aim for 6; if it has 3 internal links, aim for 4 (use a tool like Semrush to see competitor backlink/internal-link counts).
- **Anchor text audit:** generic anchor text ("click here," "learn more") is unhelpful and non-descriptive; rewrite to descriptive, keyword + location-included anchor text (e.g. "learn more about auto repair in [city]"; "Contact a Vancouver roofer" instead of "Contact Us").
- **CTA keyword rewrite:** upgrade generic CTAs to keyword-bearing versions — "Shop Now" → "Shop Healthy Dog Treats" / "Shop Organic Dog Treats"; "Learn More" → "Learn more about Design Art Camp"; "Contact Us" → "Contact a professional roofing company." Generic CTAs carry no keyword and give crawlers/AI no context on what "more" means.
- **Padding thin sections to rank for more variants:** double or triple the length of short paragraphs while working in more location+service keyword variants; add a toggleable FAQ/accordion section (confirmed fine for indexing, no special handling needed — used "on over 100 sites," "they all rank") to increase keyword density and topical coverage.
- **Comparison tables "always work fantastic for Google"** — build a table comparing yourself vs. a generic competitor (e.g. cost, features) without linking to the competitor; especially useful against big-name incumbents on a competitive head term.
- **When a page starts drifting off-topic** (e.g. a service homepage accumulating unrelated content), don't dilute the core page — add a button/link out to a dedicated page for that subtopic instead.
- **On-page optimization pass order** (used live, repeatable checklist): title tag → meta description (keyword position) → H1/H2 → body headline phrasing → CTA text → FAQ/accordion → comparison tables → internal-link section.
- **Building a single-intent "money page":** make the exact target keyword the H1 and the title, cover it again in the first H2, then build the body out with lists, tables, and explicit numbered steps.
- **Niching down an overly competitive seed keyword:** in Semrush, type the seed keyword, open "Keyword Ideas," click "View all," sort/scan by search volume while checking keyword difficulty for named-entity variants (pairing the seed term with a specific competitor/brand name) that carry real volume but much lower difficulty; build one dedicated page per such variant, with each named entity as its own nav item/dropdown funneling into the core conversion action.
- **When the real top-3 SERP holders for a head term are massive non-competitor authorities** (e.g. airlines outranking a flight-delay-compensation startup) rather than similarly-sized rivals, the standard competitor-research homework still applies unchanged — you're competing for the ranking position, not with the entity currently holding it.

## Content Strategy: Blog vs. FAQ vs. Service Page, Pruning, Cadence

- **FAQ vs. blog post decision rule:** can the question be answered in one paragraph, or does it need more information/visuals/longer-form treatment? Yes, needs depth → turn it into a blog post. Simple answer → keep it as an FAQ on the homepage, service page, or contact page.
- **Service pages vs. blog pages:** service pages = the specific things the business sells, where visitors convert (examples: MRI scans, retina scans, lip injections). Blog pages = supporting context/value content answering a question about the service topic (example: "are lip injections safe for X"). A small business doesn't need many service pages — 3–4 is fine; keep linking blogs to those few pages, and blogs can also link to other blogs.
- **Category hub-page structure:** create one category per core service/offering; each becomes a "hub" page listing/linking every blog under that topic. When topics overlap and categorization feels ambiguous, resolve it by the keyword literally in the blog post's title. Build a hub page linking out to every service page — service pages don't all need to launch at once, build over time.
- **Never create a blog post for every YouTube video** — redundant. Embed/link the video from within blog posts instead. Never paste a YouTube transcript directly as the blog post — you can reuse the topic and even the chapter/heading structure, but the written content under those headings must differ, and headings should still be optimized around one target keyword (what worked as a video chapter heading may not be the right on-page heading).
- **New-site content cadence:** publish at least 2 blog posts/month to build backlinks, topical authority, and traffic.
- **Working blog recipe (confirmed effective, not just proposed):** topic = "common issues with [a specific year/make/model]" + 3 internal service links + 1 authoritative external link (a manufacturer TSB/service bulletin confirming the issue and fix). One real case moved average GSC position from ~12–14 to ~6 using this recipe, and hit #1 for a specific long-tail query.
- **Front-load structure for long, research-heavy posts:** move "key takeaways" and the table of contents to the very top of the post, before the deep content — add a TL;DR block up front rather than after paragraphs of research. Google effectively evaluates roughly the **first ~500 words** of a post to judge relevance/indexing worth, so front-loading matters more than total depth (a real case used the target keyword 91 times with genuine research depth, but its key-takeaways block sat after the dense research and the article stayed stuck "crawled — not indexed").
- **AI-era TL;DR summary:** consider a ~100-word summary at the top of blog posts for AI-crawler/LLM consumption — tested with "mixed but leaning positive" results across clients; more compelling specifically as a competitive differentiator when rivals aren't doing it.
- **Blog archive crawl depth:** pagination (page 2, page 3, etc.) counts as a click away from the main menu and adds to crawl depth — don't dump hundreds of posts onto a single archive/homepage. Expose blog **categories** (footer or dedicated section) as hub pages instead (e.g. a "marketing" category page showing all 100 marketing posts directly). A mega menu is also a good tool to surface pages that would otherwise sit 2–3 clicks deep.
- **Cleaning up mass low-quality/AI blog content:** identify what's most relevant to the business among indexed pages and check performance. Indexed + relevant + getting traffic → re-optimize or consolidate (merge into a service page or a single blog) rather than delete. Indexed but low-traffic (including keyword-swapped duplicate/competitor pages) → cut immediately, no need to preserve. Look-back window: 3 months (up to 6). Low-traffic cutoff: **under 1% of total site impressions** in that window (calibrate against your own site's per-page average). Rationale: irrelevant blog sprawl signals Google an unclear topical direction and "diminishes the authority you have in the specific field."
- **Legitimate link-building via guest content:** find companies that advertise as press-release distributors/connectors to large publications, ask if they also do guest blogging, then write and pitch a full blog post optimized around one keyword with a link back to your site, and give it to them for free — they get free optimized content, you get a natural backlink. **Cross-border variant:** write one English post on your own site, offer it to a partner/sister company's site to translate and publish in their language with a backlink back — no volume cap, and it's a net positive for domain authority regardless of how many you do.
- **~90% of paid link-selling services are low-quality spam** and bad for a site; only the remaining ~10% (legit blogger/publication partners) are worth pursuing.
- **Removing an outdated pricing/package list** from a page (e.g. switching pricing systems, no longer wanting exact numbers public) is safe and won't hurt rankings.
- **Deciding whether to touch an already-ranking page:** for a full content overhaul, do partial variations only — don't rewrite all the content, that risks the ranking. Purely additive changes (e.g. adding client testimonials) are always safe.
- **Multilingual (EN/FR) setup:** it's fine if the French page's URL literally keeps the English slug text with "fr" inserted mid-URL — Google treats it as a distinct French page as long as the on-page content is genuinely in French. Check GSC before touching an already-ranking translated URL; if you do change it to the French keyword, ALWAYS create a redirect from the old URL (Shopify has a checkbox for this on the URL-edit screen).

## Indexing & Google Search Console Workflows

- **Diagnosing "discovered / crawled — currently not indexed" pages, in order:** (1) check internal links — confirm the page isn't orphaned; (2) check the keyword competition level the page targets — high competition can stall indexing priority; (3) paste the URL into GSC's top inspect bar; if the gray "not indexed" box appears, click **Request Indexing**; (4) check the XML sitemap itself — confirm the specific page is actually listed (sitemap updates can lag behind publish depending on plugin/generation method).
- **Ignore any GSC URL containing `?` or `=`** — dynamic/template URLs generated by front-end builders (Bakery, Elementor, Astra) that will never rank regardless. Confirm these are NOT listed in the sitemap, and confirm the sitemap itself is actually submitted in GSC.
- **Request Indexing workflow:** URL Inspection search bar (top of GSC) → paste URL → if it shows "Page is not on Google" / gray "?" → click **Request Indexing**. Not a ranking guarantee, but signals Google to recrawl, raising the odds. Not instant — "Google does like to take its sweet time." Use it only on priority/key pages; don't repeatedly mash "Start Validation"/"Validate Fix" on the sitemap itself, no benefit.
- **Indexing timeline expectations:** "three months is a safe bet in most cases" for stuck blog posts, varying with keyword difficulty, search volume, internal link count, and backlinks to that specific page — one alternate framing gave **2–3 months** as the patience window before troubleshooting further. New/brand-new sites: Google can take **2–3 weeks, sometimes up to ~2 months**, to send a crawl bot at all — don't panic before that window passes. Large sites index **"very, very slowly"** — a rising indexed-page count over time is itself the success signal, no fixed timeline.
- **404/redirect validation:** after making redirects/fixes, click **Validate Changes** in GSC for that specific issue. Typically resolves in ~3 months, but not guaranteed on a fixed schedule — a lingering GSC entry after the real-world fix is a slow report, not a live problem or penalty signal.
- **Reviving an old blog post stuck as "not indexed":** change something so it reads as fresh to Google — update the copy, tie it to a newer related post, or add a new backlink — otherwise Google keeps deeming it uninteresting and it stays unindexed.
- **Verifying a ranking-position jump isn't noise:** in GSC Performance → Queries, scan clicks/impressions/position columns; Cmd+F a suspected irrelevant keyword to jump to its row and see its actual click count. Zero-click, low-impression rankings on off-topic queries (e.g. #3 position, 3 impressions, 0 clicks) do not damage overall site metrics — "I would not worry about that at all."
- **Diagnosing a mystery/unexpected keyword ranking** (e.g. ranking for a service you don't emphasize, like "headshots" for a family photographer): run the page through GSC or Semrush to find the exact page + query driving it, then decide whether to consolidate related content onto one dedicated page or just re-optimize whichever page is already converting — don't suppress it outright, that risks losing a working (if accidental) lead source.
- **GSC "Page with redirect" entries showing old URLs:** if those are old permalinks with 301s already in place, this is not an error — GSC is just cataloging pages it knows have redirects; no action needed.
- **Domain property vs. URL-prefix property:** always use the **Domain property** for reporting — it aggregates every URL variant under the domain (all subdomains, http/https, www/non-www). The separate URL-prefix property that shows up alongside it usually stems from a past protocol/URL change; don't bother trying to delete or merge it ("a lot of hoops") — just ignore it and read the Domain property. Confirm the live site is actually served over **HTTPS**.
- **Sitemap totals are NOT the same as the true indexable-page count** — sitemaps still list redirected pages and 404 pages. To verify what's really indexed, grab a clean (non-404) live URL and run it through URL Inspection.
- **GSC → Performance → Search Results → "+ Add Filter" → Page → paste the exact URL → Apply:** filters all queries down to just that page, showing what people actually search to land on it. (Discover tab = vague "opportunities"; Performance → Search Results = the actual Queries/Page/Countries/Devices breakdown you use to diagnose one page.)
- **GSC sitemap-lag check:** Indexing → Sitemaps tab shows the date Google last crawled the sitemap. If a redirect tests fine but GSC still shows "not found," check this date first — if scanned same-day, treat it as Google-side lag, not a broken redirect; wait about a week or two.
- **Semrush — find what keyword a page ranks for:** paste the page/domain URL into the top search bar → view Authority Score + historical traffic graph → scroll to Organic Research → **Top organic keyword** field shows the primary term driving that page.
- **Bing Webmaster Tools** reports completely separate data from GSC (different search engine, different clicks/impressions/CTR/rankings) — but the underlying ranking *process* is essentially the same on both. Importing a property from GSC into Bing Webmaster Tools is a one-click process. **Always use IndexNow**, especially right after publishing/updating a page — faster indexing means faster traffic and faster natural backlink accumulation.
- **Real-estate/listings indexing triage:** check GSC's "crawled — currently not indexed" list and cross-reference against any recent site rebuild/migration date (look for a drop-off/dead zone in the timeline around the switch). Rank un-indexed pages by importance (money pages like bio/area-served vs. lower-priority blog/sold listings) before spending optimization time. Once a listing sells, **archive it** (keep it on the CMS backend, pull it from public/Google visibility) rather than leaving a dead page live — dead pages drag down overall site health. To retain marketing value from past sold work, build dedicated case-study/service pages instead of leaving individual sold-listing pages indexed.
- **A page with nothing but a bare contact form** is a common non-indexing cause — add even a short paragraph of real, keyword-relevant copy underneath it.
- **Cannibalization-tool false positives:** if a tool flags multiple URLs with identical/near-identical H1s/titles as cannibalizing, check whether those extra URLs actually 301-redirect to the canonical page — if they do, they aren't live and aren't really cannibalizing; the tool likely isn't detecting the redirect. Report the gap rather than "fixing" pages that already redirect.

## Sitemaps & Robots.txt

- **View any sitemap directly** by appending `/sitemap.xml` to the root domain.
- **Editing an auto-generated Yoast sitemap:** with Yoast Premium, edit directly in the plugin's settings. Without Premium: host's file manager → `public_html` → `wp-content` → `plugins` → the Yoast folder → find `sitemap.xml` → edit manually; if not there, check the server root instead. Standard Yoast sitemap structure = 4 sub-sitemaps: posts, pages, categories, authors (useful reference when troubleshooting a broken sitemap on another plugin).
- **One sitemap per site is the standard rule of thumb**, especially for new WordPress sites — some complex/legacy sites legitimately run 4–5 split by content type as the exception, not the norm. **Segmented sitemaps must each be submitted individually** in GSC — it won't automatically pull in all of them.
- **Fixing a broken/plugin-generated sitemap:** use a standalone sitemap-generator tool as fallback — input the site URL, it crawls and lists pages as "added" or "skipped" (skipped = e.g. taken-down/archived pages still technically discoverable, don't worry about these), click Download for the XML file, submit that file to GSC → Sitemaps.
- **Sitemap "can't fetch" despite a verified, correct URL:** suspect a platform/CMS-level limitation (seen on Showit) rather than a sitemap-content error.
- **Checking robots.txt directly:** append `/robots.txt` to the homepage URL. WordPress + Yoast: Tools → File Editor → open `robots.txt`. A default/untouched Yoast or WordPress robots.txt blocks nothing — if a ranking or audit-tool problem exists, it's coming from somewhere else (a plugin, JS, or the tool itself). Only add `disallow` lines for things deliberately hidden (admin dashboard, throwaway pages); if Search Console already shows traffic, leave it alone.
- **Fixing Shopify robots.txt over-blocking:** open the Shopify robots.txt, remove "disallow" lines for blog/collections/products — these are commonly injected by a third-party app rather than done manually; disable the suspect plugin, wait a few minutes for cache to clear, then recheck.
- **Diagnosing a bot blocked on a full-site crawl but not single-page checks:** check both `.htaccess` and `robots.txt` for user-agent blocks — specifically whether "Python" is blocked, since many SEO audit tools (including Hey Tony's own) run their crawler on Python.
- **Diagnosing a CDN blocking a legitimate audit-tool crawler:** identify the CDN via the **BuiltWith** browser extension, then get the crawler's whitelist (IP/user-agent list) added to that CDN's/firewall's allowlist. **Cloudflare** is flagged as a recurring culprit for this.
- **Diagnosing a redirect/sitemap 404 root cause:** check `.htaccess` or any redirect-managing WordPress plugin; if unfamiliar with `.htaccess`, paste its contents into ChatGPT and ask if anything in it is causing a redirect.
- **Diagnosing "page with redirect" / not-indexed errors:** open the flagged URL and check whether it silently appends a parameter or redirects (e.g. a location name appended to the end) — the parameterized/redirected version won't rank, but the clean target URL will (check separately via URL Inspection).
- **www/https mismatches post-migration:** compare protocol (http vs. https) and www vs. non-www format of each flagged URL against the site's actual live/canonical format — mismatches from a redesign are common; ignore ones that are old snapshot noise, fix real ones.
- **Post-relaunch #1 check:** did any URLs change, including www/non-www? If yes, set up 301 redirects from every old URL to its new counterpart immediately — described as "the number one most important thing" to verify after any redesign/relaunch.

## Canonical Tags, Duplicate Content & Audit-Tool False Positives

- **Canonical/permalink mismatch fix** (e.g. a renamed WP taxonomy — "Portfolio" renamed to "Work" — leaving the canonical tag pointing to a now-404ing old URL): confirm via an SEO inspector that "canonical tag does not match sitemap," then fix per page via WordPress → Yoast → Advanced tab → **Canonical URL** field → paste the correct URL → publish. If it persists on one specific page after that, suspect caching, a theme setting silently overriding it, or duplicate logic — next step: Appearance → Theme File Editor → `functions.php` (or theme equivalent) to find/edit the code generating the canonical tag directly. Don't attempt this fix through permalink structure settings.
- **"Canonical URL not self-referencing" is very often just a trailing-slash mismatch** between the live URL and the canonical tag (tool flags it even though the page is fine) — fix by locating the canonical field in the CMS (Squarespace: page/site settings) and adding the missing slash so it exactly matches the live URL.
- **"Never have duplicate content on your site — that's the number one thing" to avoid.**
- **Bilingual URL structure is "good enough, not ideal":** Google can correctly treat an English-slug-plus-"fr" URL as a distinct French page based on on-page language — not urgent to fix, but if the page isn't ranking well, changing the URL to the French keyword (with a redirect) can help.

## Redirects, URL Changes & Site Migrations

- **301 redirects specifically pass link authority to the destination page** — but authority transfer is never fully free: "a little bit less because that will still be a new page." Changing a page's URL/parent path always warrants a 301; visitor click-through still works fine via the chain.
- **Before changing any URL, check GSC traffic/impressions on that page first.** No traffic/impressions → redirecting is low-risk. Meaningful traffic (e.g. ~100/day) → redirecting carries real risk of losing traffic — weigh it before acting.
- **Never straight-404 a seasonal/out-of-stock product page** if there's any chance of restocking — hide the "Add to Cart" button or set inventory to zero, leave the page itself live; taking it down (404 + redirect) causes Google to devalue the page, and bringing it back later means lost traffic history plus Google having to recrawl/rediscover it from scratch. Only fully remove (zero stock + 301 redirect) products that are permanently discontinued ("one and done").
- **One-time campaign/webinar landing pages** (registration, confirmation pages): set to **Draft** once the campaign is done, so Google doesn't see them at all. **Evergreen landing pages** (e.g. an always-live PDF-download page promoted repeatedly): keep live, but internally link them (nav, or from a related service page/blog post) so they're never orphaned.
- **Never blindly re-redirect back and forth.** Reverting a slug/URL change: check GSC to see which URL (old vs. new) is actually getting traffic; whichever has the traffic is the one to keep. Delete the current redirect, create a fresh one pointing the new URL back to the old. Don't delete pages yet — test the revert first, monitor for about a month, and only remove the abandoned duplicate once its metrics fully drop off.
- **Changing a URL/slug — even on what feels like "the same page" — creates a brand-new page in Google's eyes.** Some CMS/plugin combos (e.g. certain WordPress SEO plugin setups) auto-generate a redirect on slug change automatically; verify one was actually created.
- **Never change an established domain/brand name just because a bigger, better-funded, name-confusable competitor is winning the "did you mean...?" SERP prompt.** Preserve accumulated domain history/authority (a real case had 7 years of history) instead of resetting to zero — compete via local, high-authority backlinks (city hall, chamber of commerce) and deeper, niche-specific blog content instead.
- **Never move acquisition-facing content (a blog) to a brand-new domain** — Google treats a new URL as an entirely new domain, resetting accumulated posting history and indexed traffic. Use a **subdomain** instead if a split is technically unavoidable. Purely internal/customer-support content (login help, billing, ticket knowledge base) doesn't need to be indexed at all and isn't worth protecting.
- **Real domain-change failure case:** a business expanded from photography into video, slightly changed its domain name to reflect the new offering, and rebuilt on a new website builder with no redirect set up — traffic and inbound calls dropped to zero. Root cause: the domain change itself, compounded by no redirect, forfeiting accumulated domain authority. Lesson: never change the domain further; build separate new pages for a new service line instead of another rename.
- **CMS/platform migration does NOT reset SEO from scratch as long as the domain stays the same** — backlinks and authority persist, only the platform/design changes. Treat a full platform migration as a "longer down the line" move if the business is thriving on its current limited platform now.
- **Migration checklist:** keep every URL exactly the same (including www vs. non-www — Google treats that as a different URL even with an identical slug), keep the same meta titles/descriptions, carry over H1s/H2s. **Prioritize internal linking over immediate H1 cleanup post-migration** — internal linking protects long-term indexability once Google recrawls; scrambled/keyword-stuffed H1s can be cleaned up afterward without hurting the transition.
- **Before agreeing to migrate a ranking hand-coded/legacy site to WordPress:** ask about the current editing process, then check existing SEO tools (Ubersuggest/Semrush) plus Search Console/Analytics/Tag Manager to see if pages actually rank and get traffic. Not ranking → an easy full move is fine. Ranking and getting traffic → be very careful, focus effort on the pages that actually rank, ignore the likely-large number of zero-traffic pages.
- **Identify an unknown site's CMS/platform** by pasting the URL into **whatcms.org**.

## Orphan Pages & Internal Linking

- **Orphan page definition:** not reachable by any click path from the homepage. Every page on a site needs to be reachable somehow (footer-only placement is fine — it doesn't need to be in the top nav, it just needs *a* click path).
- **Nofollow as a hidden cause of orphan flags:** a page can register as "orphan" in audit tools even with a couple of inbound links if those links are set to `nofollow` (nofollow tells crawlers not to pass through/follow the link). Diagnose via View Page Source + Cmd+F/Ctrl+F search for `nofollow`; cross-check the platform's link editor for a hyperlink nofollow toggle — check footer links, collection-page links, and brand-page links specifically, not just product links. Products linked only from a collection/category page normally do NOT throw nofollow/orphan flags by default.
- **Triaging a large orphan-page count instead of fixing all of them:** pick the ~10 most important pages, add 1–2 extra internal links to each (from a blog post or elsewhere), monitor how those specific pages perform, then decide whether to dig further into the rest.
- **Intentional orphans are fine:** if a page is only meant for conversions and is linked from outside the site (e.g. a YouTube video description) and not meant to rank, leave it un-linked internally — do nothing.
- **Internal linking is "the best, most tangible/practical" fix** for stuck ("crawled but not indexed") pages, cannibalization risk, and general authority — repeated across nearly every call as the default remedy.
- **Placement matters more than volume:** prefer inline contextual links within body copy, placed **higher up** in the content, over a "suggested/recommended posts" block dumped at the bottom — Google values in-body links placed higher more than three links stacked at the bottom. Best practice is a mix of both, weighted toward inline/higher.
- **Every blog post should link to at least one location or service page**, not exclusively to other blog posts.
- **Anchor text specificity pass:** expand a vague single-word anchor (e.g. just "most" inside a sentence) to describe the destination page (e.g. link the full phrase "most common questions about pest control"); conversely, shorten an anchor that's an entire long sentence down to a short, destination-specific phrase.
- **"You may also like" / related-product blocks count as internal links.**
- **Opening an internal link in a new browser tab has zero effect on indexing** — pure UX/navigation choice, not an SEO issue.
- **A bottom-of-page "related links"/"other services" block is acceptable only as a fallback** when no natural in-body placement exists — naturally integrated links throughout the content are the preferred default.

## Site Speed & Image/Video Optimization

- **WebP conversion cleanup:** after converting images to WebP and re-uploading, physically delete the old PNG/JPEG originals from the server — don't just "replace" via a tool; the old files keep dragging speed down if left in place. (Real case: a 5.4MB PNG converted to WebP was estimated to drop to ~300KB, under 10% of original size.) Recommended plugin: **WebP Converter for Media** (preferred over **Imagify**, called "a little bit too heavy"). On platforms without server access (e.g. Wix): use any online JPEG/PNG-to-WebP converter, or in Photoshop, File → Save As → change the file-type dropdown to WebP, re-upload.
- **PHP + server-level speed fixes (WordPress, cPanel access):** update PHP to **8.2 or higher**; verify all plugins are compatible first (so you know what to roll back to); in cPanel enable **OPcache, Redis, and Memcached**; re-test after each change.
- **Shopify speed checklist (check these four before anything else):** CDN set up; images served as WebP; image lazy loading enabled; CSS/JS deferred (non-critical). If all four are in place, overall site speed "should be very, very good."
- **WP Rocket File Optimization** (Minify JavaScript files, Load JavaScript deferred, JS execution-delay setting) and **Media → Lazy Loading** are the most common causes of broken assets/images after a speed-optimization pass, especially images with effects/animations attached — test changes and be ready to roll back; Hey Tony's own production site runs with lazy loading fully disabled. **SiteGround Speed Optimizer** is a comparable alternative caching plugin.
- **Hosting memory/execution time limit:** most hosts default this to 30 seconds; if plugins/scripts need more, triple it to **90 seconds** and re-test.
- **Delaying JavaScript for speed:** from a PageSpeed Insights report, identify the specific long-rendering scripts, enable "delay JavaScript" for those specifically, then re-test both the speed gain AND full page functionality — load order can matter (something delayed may be a dependency another script needs first).
- **Self-host Google Fonts** via the **OMGF** WordPress plugin instead of loading them from Google on every page load.
- **Image format preference:** WebP or AVIF; fall back to compressed JPEG only where the platform doesn't support the newer formats. Squarespace does not accept AVIF at all; WebP support is version/builder-dependent on Squarespace (older builders may reject it). Wix accepts WebP without issue.
- **Photo resolution:** keep uploads under 2K; downsize large raw/DSLR captures before upload (e.g. 5000×2000 shrunk to ~1000×1500) rather than letting the platform serve the full-size file. General rule: images under **1000px wide**, except banner images.
- **Don't trust an SEO tool's automated word count at face value**, especially against a competitor's — verify manually by copy-pasting all visible on-page content into a separate word/character counter. Known inflation sources: pop-ups, review-widget text, hidden/background code, mobile-version content counted twice, currency/country selector text (one real case: 536 product reviews alone added a couple thousand phantom words to the tool's count).
- **Speed-triage decision when choosing between fixing a current template vs. switching to a new one:** run a speed test (PageSpeed Insights or an audit tool) on BOTH the current template and the candidate new template before deciding where to spend effort. Priority order after testing: CDN → file optimization (compression, file type) → hosting review.
- **Live site-speed audit checklist:** confirm a caching plugin is active → confirm PHP version is current (8.2.x = fine) → confirm images are WebP → confirm whether JavaScript is delayed → pull a PageSpeed Insights report and identify which scripts are slow/badly ordered.

## Local SEO: Location Page Architecture

- **Deciding broad-region vs. single-city targeting:** check keyword difficulty/volume for the broad regional term (Semrush) before assuming it's unwinnable — a niche business can still be viable for a broad term even against a large population base. Viable → keep the homepage optimized around the broad term. Too difficult → pick one specific city/localized area (typically the biggest-population city in the region) and optimize the homepage around that instead. Either way, expand outward with dedicated location pages for every other town/neighborhood in the target area.
- **Building the location-page keyword list:** ask an AI directly — *"what are all the towns or neighborhoods in [County/region]?"* — to generate the full list, then append each town name to the core service term.
- **Page-building sequence for a new/small local-service site, in order:** (1) optimize the homepage first (single primary keyword/city); (2) build service pages right away; (3) expand to location pages "as much as possible"; (4) long-term: cross services with locations so every service has its own page for every target city (full service × location matrix), all internally linked. **Don't try to build everything at once** — work backward from cities with the highest population, or better, the cities generating the most actual conversions/customers; build low-population/low-conversion cities last.
- **Lower-effort alternative to a full service × location matrix:** keep one set of well-optimized service pages (optimized for the area as a whole), and on each location page simply describe/list which services are offered there and link out to the corresponding service pages.
- **Location-page viability threshold:** validate real search volume behind [service + location] before building — check via GSC or a keyword tool. Illustrative ratio given: a county with ~500 population where roughly **85%** of that population's search volume sits behind the target term = viable to build; no meaningful volume = "a waste of space on your site."
- **Don't over-expand blindly into every possible city/county just because you technically can** — Google can read over-broad, unsubstantiated local claims as gaming the system: "if you live in Nashville, you can't say you're a local lender to Chattanooga." Use AI (Claude) as a sanity-check on an expansion plan, but base the final go/no-go on actual keyword-volume data, not the AI's opinion.
- **URL slug structure for a single-location local service business:** use `/service-type/location/` and drop a redundant "services" parent segment entirely (people search "security guard [city]," not "services security guard [city]"). Hyphen vs. slash between service and location scales with site size — hyphen is fine for small page counts; once building dozens/hundreds of location pages, use the slash, it's easier to categorize/tag by location at scale.
- **Multi-location page-building process (a UK-HQ agency expanding into US states):** check Search Console first for what keywords people already use to reach the site; if majority traffic is from one market, optimize the homepage/main service pages around that market and don't touch the other market's optimization yet. Build one entirely separate page per new location targeted, each with title tag + H1 = "[Service] in [Location]," filled with subheadings answering questions like "Why should you hire a [service] in [Location]?" Repeat the exact structure per location. Build a hub page or blog posts linking to every location page, and make sure anyone landing on the hub can immediately tell which market those pages target.
- **Internal linking from location pages to service pages:** only link the services genuinely relevant/tailored to that specific area's content — don't force every service into every location page. It's fine if different location pages link to different subsets of services, as long as there's overall crossover site-wide. Prefer working links naturally into body copy over a generic "related links" block at the end.
- **Local-first strategy for a new/low-authority site in a big-city market:** don't target the broad, maximally competitive city-wide term first. Build a small locational/neighborhood page (or a distinct description page from the homepage) targeting your immediate area, establish authority there, then broaden toward the bigger city term over time. Use a real keyword tool to confirm the smaller local area may show *more* traffic than the broad regional term before committing (don't rely on GSC's own built-in keyword-insight popup, which can surface only a generic, low-value term).
- **Service-area page restructuring:** replace one long flat list (county → town → city, all linking to the same generic service page) with a click-through hierarchy — clicking a Town surfaces the Cities within it. Use the restructure as the opportunity to build dedicated individual pages for the highest-opportunity cities (traction, underserved/easy-to-rank markets), not all cities at once.
- **Multi-location nav/IA:** top-level nav item "Locations" → dropdown lists each city → hovering a city opens a second flyout with that city's service/client-type pages. The homepage itself represents the business's own home city.
- **Simultaneous local + broad approach:** optimize the actual ranking target around the single city you can realistically win (chasing a whole state/country ranking is "extremely difficult," multi-year and costly); within that same content, explicitly *mention* that you also serve the wider state/nationwide, without optimizing to rank for that broader scope. This wins both traditional local rankings and broader AI citations simultaneously.
- **Deciding between two nearby local markets** (e.g. physically in one city, most customers in a neighboring one): don't guess — set up GSC first, then check which term real search traffic is actually using. Rule: if 50% or more of real queries use one location's name over the other, optimize the page around that one.
- **Escaping high competition on a broad head term:** replace broad terms ("mechanic near me" — cited at 2,200,000 searches/month, "probably the most broad SEO keyword on planet Earth," high impressions but essentially no clicks/conversion) with city/neighborhood-modified long-tail versions; for a city with distinct neighborhoods, build a separate location page per served area.
- **Location pages matter far more for geography-tied service businesses than for product-based or nationwide/worldwide businesses** (e.g. web hosting/programming) — skip location pages for the latter unless targeting one specific area.
- **Reselling another company's products at a discount** (e.g. an apparel retailer reselling a European brand): create one separate collection per brand being discounted ("[Collection] Brand Name Sale"), list all discounted products, add 1–2 paragraphs of unique differentiating content (what the brand is, that it's on sale, what differentiates your store from the brand's own site). Accept that the brand itself will always rank #1 for its own name — target the sale/discount modifier instead, aiming to rank directly under the brand's own site. Build internal links, get a press release if possible, and go all-in on whichever discount-adjacent keyword (sale, cheap, discounted) has the highest volume.
- **Ranking for an ultra-competitive head keyword with a new/low-authority site:** fix heading hierarchy first if out of order; do not chase the broad city-wide term — narrow to specific neighborhoods/sub-areas; use a keyword tool to find modifiers with low competition but sufficient volume; build content targeting those first; build backlinks/authority over time before going after the harder, broader keyword.

## Google Business Profile (GBP)

- **GBP post-linking chain:** every new post should carry three links — (1) the share-link of your immediately previous post (three-dot menu on that post → Share → copy link), (2) a website link to a valuable service page, (3) your CID link. Whatever new post you make next, link back to *that* one, continually rolling the chain forward. This signals to Google that the profile is active and its posts are interconnected, building local-profile authority.
- **Getting your CID link:** primary method — copy the profile's Share URL, paste it into a CID finder tool (sometimes fails). **Fallback:** right-click the profile link → View Page Source → Cmd+F search "ludocid" (transcribed variously as "Ludo"/"Ludocid") → the long number next to it is the CID. Easier method: install the **Plerdy** WordPress plugin, which surfaces the CID number/link directly. Final usable link format: Google Maps URL + `?cid=[number]`.
- **CID link use:** reuse the same link inside blog posts and service pages (not just GMB posts) wherever it fits naturally — passes profile authority through the site and increases profile visibility. These links exist **for the search engine, not for human visitors** — don't expect them to drive click-throughs. Make the business address on the site a clickable CID link rather than plain text or the standard GBP share link. Example anchor pattern: "[business] advertising/SEO agency [city]."
- **A GBP is assigned a permanent CID number once it has existed roughly 2+ weeks; that CID stays valid "basically for life,"** regardless of whether the profile's share link ever expires or details change later.
- **Posting cadence:** twice a week (Hey Tony's own standard, applied to clients and themselves); at least once a week as a minimum benchmark. Purpose is primarily to signal to Google/Search Console that the profile is actively maintained — customers seeing the post is a secondary benefit. Posting via a secondary scheduling tool (social cross-poster, BrightLocal's scheduler) counts the same to Google as posting manually and directly.
- **Post content:** doesn't need to be promotional — a simple summary of what's on offer that week works. Can be AI-drafted, roughly tweet-length. Include a link at the bottom (book now/learn more) plus the CID link. Start every post description with the exact keyword you want the profile to rank for (e.g. "SEO agency in Hamilton").
- **Photos:** batch roughly 10 images, then leave a gap of a week to a month before adding another ~10 — steady incremental additions, not one-time dumps. Recommended image size: **1,000 × 1,000** (square). Geotag images to the service location.
- **Recheck the GBP description and full profile info at least once a month** — descriptions can be silently declined, publicly edited by others, or disappear "through no fault of your own."
- **GBP post-core-update priorities, in order:** (1) fully complete profile — every listed service has a description, all info matches the website exactly; (2) consistent updates/posts, daily or every-other-day, to signal active monitoring; (3) reviews, especially for service-based businesses — Google reads reviews as a relevancy/experience signal. Not hitting 100% profile completeness even after following every prompt can be a Google glitch, or the recommendations aren't always relevant — fine regardless of the percentage shown as long as all services have keyword-rich descriptions and no core info is missing.
- **Posts vs. photos:** both matter — posts signal freshness to the algorithm more than to human searchers; photos signal value/trust more to human searchers.
- **If your website ranks for a city, your GBP tends to rank for that city too (and vice versa)** — keep NAP (name/address/phone) and city targeting consistent across both. GSC and GBP are completely separate systems: GSC covers organic website search traffic; GBP covers the Maps listing (calls, direction requests, and — via third-party local tools — searcher distance).
- **Switching a GBP to a new address** (e.g. closing one location, moving to another, but keeping the old market's customers online): you can change the existing profile's address directly — it will likely need re-verification, but functions identically once reverified and **keeps existing reviews**. Decision fork: if the old market's traffic doesn't matter anymore, convert and close it out. If it's still a priority alongside the new location, instead create a brand-new separate profile for the other location after converting the first (a new profile starts with zero reviews).
- **Two businesses with different names/websites CAN share the exact same physical address** and each still get its own separate GBP verification — identical address alone doesn't block a second listing. Managing two GBP listings under one business: BrightLocal or directly inside GBP, either works, preference-based.
- **Squarespace blog location-metadata field:** set it to a specific location only if that blog's content actually targets/is about that location; leave generic content (e.g. "What is a web developer?") with location blank.
- **Escalating a stalled GBP verification:** resubmit once through the standard "verify profile" flow; if it still doesn't resolve, contact Google Business/local-business support directly rather than repeating video verification indefinitely (Google's Business Profile Help Center has a direct escalation path). One real case sat stuck over a month with zero response despite Google's stated ~5-day turnaround.
- **Virtual/no-physical-location businesses don't fit GBP's model well** — some had to "almost add the location page and then eliminate it" just to get Google to notice the business at all. Non-location businesses used to be flatly penalized for lacking an address; a more recent core update loosened that penalty, but full profile completeness, regular updates, and reviews still have to compensate for not having a location.
- **Don't publicly list a home address in schema/GBP for a remote/service-based business** — safety reason (a real anecdote involved unwanted packages showing up after a home address was listed publicly).
- **A brand-new, exact-match-keyword-named GBP can visibly outrank an older, formally-named profile almost immediately** — suggesting exact-match naming can matter as much as, or more than, the full checklist of individual optimization tactics (posting cadence, geotagging, descriptions) applied to a generically-named profile.
- **GBP audit checklist:** confirm regular posting, confirm images are geotagged, confirm service descriptions are present, confirm the whole profile is fully optimized (services, cities, categories) — check all of it monthly.

## Citations: Yext vs. BrightLocal

- **BrightLocal is Hey Tony's preferred citation/local tool** (used ~6 years; tried switching away, came back); praised support response time ("within a day usually"). Whenever the team sees a client on Yext, they'll almost always recommend swapping over.
- **Yext is an API overlay — you never own the citations built through it.** If you stop paying the subscription, those citations disappear (one real case: an annual Yext plan initially built "4–5 pages" of citations, but a year later only 8 individual citations remained). If you manage 3, 4, or fewer than 10 citations through Yext, just take direct control of those yourself instead of paying the monthly subscription — cheaper and easier long-term.
- **Migrating citations off Yext onto BrightLocal, step by step:**
  1. Pull the full list of citations Yext currently manages/previously built.
  2. Compare against BrightLocal's citation list — find duplicates.
  3. For duplicates: let BrightLocal own/manage the citation, remove it from Yext.
  4. For citations only on Yext: check BrightLocal's Citation Builder/Campaign Builder to see if that directory is buildable there. If yes, build it through BrightLocal and let it fully manage it. If no, leave it on Yext, or check if BrightLocal can add it as a custom/manual citation to monitor.
  5. Once BrightLocal covers everything it can, go back to Yext and check remaining outliers for the same custom-citation possibility.
  6. If a citation truly can't move: leave it on Yext, or cancel Yext entirely and manage that handful directly yourself.
  7. **Critical:** on the Yext account, find the sync toggle for business info/citations and turn it OFF for anything BrightLocal also manages — if sync stays on, Yext will compete with/overwrite BrightLocal's data.
- Yext isn't "all bad" — keep it for the small residual set of citations BrightLocal genuinely can't build/manage; the real fix is just disabling the sync toggle so it stops fighting BrightLocal's data.

## Link Building & Backlinks

- **~90% of paid link-selling services are low-quality spam and bad for a site;** the remaining ~10% (legit blogger/publication partners) are worth pursuing.
- **Legitimate process:** find press-release-distribution/publication-connector companies, ask if they also do guest blogging, write and pitch a full keyword-optimized post with a backlink to your site, give it to them free.
- **Cross-border guest posting:** write an English post on your own site, offer it to a sister/partner company abroad to translate and publish with a backlink back — no volume cap, confirmed a pure net positive for domain authority regardless of frequency.
- **Backlink benchmarking:** match or slightly exceed competitor backlink counts on the target page (competitor has 5 → aim for 6).
- **Descriptive, even very low-domain-authority backlinks that specifically describe the product** (what it's made of, how it was printed, print quality) helped more for AI-search visibility than generic high-DA brand mentions, per direct testing on print-on-demand stores.
- **Paid backlinks carry no guaranteed ROI** — explicitly not vouched for either way; framed as the site owner's own budget/risk call.
- **Social-media backlinks carry much lower link authority than a referring website or internal link** — still worth doing, just don't expect it to move rankings much on its own.
- **Local high-authority backlinks (city hall, chamber of commerce/town business center)** are recommended specifically when defending an established domain against a bigger, name-confusable competitor, rather than trying to out-brand them directly.
- A proprietary backlink-recommendation tool can surface whether other sites already consider you a good backlink source even without you having built many backlinks yourself (one real case: after writing a guest article, the site itself started being surfaced as a *recommended* backlink source, with visibility score rising from 17 to 22).

## AI Search, AI Overviews & Zero-Click (GEO)

- **Google is actively prioritizing zero-click search** — moving people away from a list of possible results toward a single direct answer, making its own judgment call and just telling the user "the best thing."
- **What drives AI Overview citation (from an internal test analyzing hundreds of keywords):**
  1. **Broaden mentions across as many different mediums as possible** (press releases, social posts, etc.) rather than chasing fewer, "higher quality" mentions — presence/volume outweighs mention quality for citation.
  2. **Broaden geographic scope in written content** — write around the full state/province rather than city-specific, to maximize AI Overview surface area.
  3. **Bake in time-relevance** — include the current year in content, matching how AI models silently append the current year to their own background searches before answering (e.g. Gemini appending "2026" to a query like "demolition cost").
  4. **Don't prioritize high-domain-authority sources** for these mentions — volume of mentions matters more than the authority of where they appear.
  5. **Build a Reddit presence specifically** — create your own subreddit and accumulate brand mentions there; links back to your site aren't even required, mentions alone reportedly move AI Overview visibility "wonders," in every case seen.
- **Dual local + AI approach:** optimize the actual ranking target around the single city you can realistically win, while explicitly *mentioning* (not optimizing for) the broader state/nationwide service area in the same content — wins both traditional local rankings and broader AI citations simultaneously, pursued at the same time, not as either/or.
- **AI Overview citation weighting (presence/volume over domain authority) is a real departure from classic backlink-quality thinking** — framed internally as something actively being tested/"played around with," not settled doctrine.
- **Broad generic product-term visibility in AI answers (e.g. "beautiful t-shirts" in Gemini/ChatGPT/Anthropic-style results) is genuinely difficult** based on direct hands-on testing running multiple print-on-demand stores — no reliable playbook yet. What did help: sourcing backlinks (even very low-DA ones) from sites that specifically describe the product itself rather than making generic brand mentions.
- **Using AI as an E-A-T self-audit tool:** after editing site content, paste/describe it to Claude or ChatGPT and ask for a grade on each E-A-T category plus specific suggestions to improve it — endorsed as "a healthy way" to prep content for Google's increasingly AI-driven evaluation.
- **Google's direction is shifting more AI-focused** — prioritize very specific, tightly concentrated long-tail keywords (service + niche modifier + specific city/county) over broad head terms going forward.
- **FAQ schema double-edged for AI visibility:** it can drive genuine "Google's AI is now recommending me" outcomes, but the same implementation can also draw heavy AI/training-bot crawl traffic that needs separate mitigation (see Schema section) — the fix is to block the bad bots, never to strip the schema that's earning the AI-search visibility.

## Platform-Specific Gotchas

- **Squarespace:** dynamic-text auto-append of business name to titles (theme code fix; often a non-issue since Google strips it in real SERPs); doesn't accept AVIF at all, WebP support version/builder-dependent; category/tag archive pages often only editable via code/design injection settings, not the normal page list; animated section reveals are a crawl risk — Google's crawl bot effectively "scrolls" through a page at a set pace, and content that hasn't rendered by the time it passes may not register; fix by cutting section-reveal timing roughly in half in CSS/animation settings, or in the page editor drag the small resize handle in a section's corner upward to shrink dead/empty space before content displays.
- **Shopify:** duplicate H1 commonly injected by page-builder apps (e.g. GemPages pulling a collection-page product title as a second H1) — fix by demoting the tag directly in `product.liquid`/`theme.liquid`; H1 sometimes hard-wired to the logo (add a redundant second H1 as stopgap + escalate to Shopify support); certain **Dawn** theme versions auto-append the store name to every title tag regardless of manual edits (check Store Settings for the toggle, or a `[store name]` shortcode in the theme); robots.txt over-blocking blog/collections/products commonly injected by a third-party app; **Sidekick** (Shopify's built-in AI assistant) can help with code-level guesses when no developer is available; the URL-edit screen has a built-in checkbox to auto-create a redirect when a URL changes.
- **WordPress:** some themes (e.g. Cornerstone) lack a parent-page selector in the editor — use Settings → Permalinks instead to prepend a category to blog URLs; Rank Math's built-in redirect manager can silently "accept" a 301 without actually applying it or listing it — switch to a separate dedicated redirect plugin; Rank Math's no-index toggle backdates a page if used long-term, so be careful no-indexing pages you eventually want to rank; Divi's styling controls are notoriously scattered across many settings locations — Hey Tony's standing workaround is inline CSS directly in the content, appending `!important` if Divi still ignores it; Avada, Elementor, and Divi are all in active use at Hey Tony — pick based on personal comfort, not a single "best" answer (Avada has the deepest bench of Hey Tony tutorial content); WordPress is generally recommended over Squarespace/Wix/Shopify for maximum control: unrestricted custom code placement, no plugin/add-on ceiling, ability to set up email lists and push notifications not available on the others.
- **Wix:** accepts WebP without issue; no server/code access, so image-format conversion requires an online converter or Photoshop workaround; template lock-in — switching templates later effectively means starting over.
- **Showit:** sitemap fetch failures suspected as a platform-level limitation rather than a content error; HTML customization "slightly limited," heading-tag order/behavior on-page uncertain; migrating off Showit to WordPress later preserves backlinks/authority as long as the domain stays the same.
- **GoHighLevel:** auto-creates two blog pages by default — remove the unused one once you've settled on which to optimize; repeatedly flagged as the platform the technical team has the least hands-on experience with.
- **BigCommerce → Shopify migrations churn all existing URLs** — expect a broad indexing disruption and a robots.txt/sitemap audit as standard post-migration steps.
- **Elementor/page-builder sites** commonly generate large numbers of harmless auto-generated template/category pages that show as "not indexed" (one real case: as many as 20,000 such pages) — none of them would ever rank anyway and this is not something to spend time "fixing."

## Notable Worked Case Studies

- **Auto repair shop keyword-density benchmark:** top 3 competitors ran 1,500–2,000 words; the operator raised his page from 1,300 to 2,010 words (closest competitor below him sat at 2,002 — confirmed a fine, non-excessive gap). Keyword counts "auto repair" ×26 and "automotive repair" ×17 combined beat competitors' combined total by 12 instances — flagged as an overshoot, advised to trim per keyword variation rather than in aggregate. Same operator ranked top 3 for "mechanic" using only 3 mentions vs. 0 for all three competitors, while the head term "mechanic near me" (2.2M searches/month) produced impressions but no real clicks/conversion. A separate sister towing-company site was unaffected by the same Google core-update rollout that hit this auto-repair site hard — illustrating that one update can devastate one site in a portfolio while leaving a closely related sibling untouched.
- **Ecommerce indexing/robots.txt case:** a BigCommerce → Shopify migration produced roughly 13,000 not-indexed vs. ~1,000 indexed pages, top flagged issue "Blocked by robots.txt" — traced to a third-party SEO app disallowing blog/collections/product paths in the Shopify robots.txt, not anything done manually.
- **AI-generated content sprawl:** a hired SEO freelancer produced roughly 3,000 AI-generated blog posts for an escape-room business, of which at least ~1,000 got indexed (not ranked); one post was duplicated ~5 times with only the focus keyword swapped to competitor names each time; an auto-interlinking plugin (Link Juicer) tied all ~3,000 pages together. Reassessed against the 3-month look-back + <1%-of-impressions pruning rule rather than a longer look-back that made the pages look less wasteful than they were.
- **Real estate indexing triage:** a realtor's sitemap listed 87 pages while GSC showed only 24 indexed against 113+ not indexed; the drop traced to a site rebuild with an indexing dead-zone around the switch date, requiring two sitemap-resubmission iterations to fully resolve. Sold listings were the identified "dead page" drag — the fix was archiving them (not deleting) and building separate case-study pages to retain their marketing value.
- **FAQ schema bot-storm:** adding hand-coded FAQ schema (via a small third-party snippet tool) to win AI-search visibility caused bot traffic to spike to 150,000 visits/day on a site normally getting ~40/day, requiring a cybersecurity consultant, a full hosting-IP reset, and Cloudflare — while simultaneously getting the site recommended by Google's AI specifically because of those FAQs. The fix applied was blocking the bad bots via Claude-assisted code, not removing the schema.
- **Domain-name-change failure:** a photographer expanded into video, slightly changed her domain name to reflect it, and rebuilt on a new website builder with zero redirect set up — traffic and inbound calls collapsed to zero, diagnosed as lost domain authority from the unredirected domain change, not any single on-page issue.
- **AI Overview visibility test:** internal testing across hundreds of keywords found presence/volume of brand mentions (including a self-created subreddit, no backlink required) outweighs source domain authority for AI Overview citation — a genuine departure from classic backlink-quality thinking, explicitly framed as still being tested rather than settled.

## Tradeoffs & Open Questions

- **Audit-tool warnings vs. real ranking problems:** cache lag, trailing-slash canonical mismatches, and "St" vs. "St." period discrepancies are all real tool flags that carry zero actual ranking impact — always verify against GSC or a non-caching tool before spending time on a "fix."
- **Keyword density/word count have no universal number** — both are always relative to the current SERP and must be re-checked per keyword, not treated as a fixed formula; the same keyword can need 2 mentions or 11 depending on what's currently ranking.
- **Redirect authority loss is real but partial** — the decision to change a URL should weigh existing traffic on that page (near-zero = low risk; meaningful daily traffic = real risk), never treated as free.
- **AI as advisor, never decision-maker:** Claude/ChatGPT are used for strategy sanity-checks, self-audits, and drafting, but the final call on keyword targeting, location-page expansion, and heading architecture should rest on real keyword/traffic data, not AI output alone — ChatGPT specifically has produced keyword-stuffed headings when asked to architect content directly.
- **FAQ/hand-coded schema is a genuine double-edged tool** — real AI-citation upside paired with real bot-abuse downside; the resolution is always to patch the bot problem, never to strip the schema earning the visibility.
- **Location-page expansion is data-gated, not ambition-gated** — the identical tactic (a page per county/city) is good practice for one business and "gaming the system" for another; the only legitimate differentiator is verified keyword search volume behind that specific location term.
- **Not every "not indexed" or "404" flag is a real problem** — tag/category/template pages, harmless builder-generated URLs, and stale sitemap caches routinely inflate not-indexed counts without representing any actual SEO issue; the two GSC reasons genuinely worth chasing are "Crawled – currently not indexed" and "Not found (404)" on pages that should rank.
- **Platform choice is a flexibility-vs-simplicity tradeoff**, not a technical absolute — WordPress offers maximum control at the cost of a steeper learning curve and more moving parts to misconfigure; Squarespace/Wix/Shopify trade flexibility for guardrails, and are worth leaving only when a business already plans significant changes or has hit a real platform ceiling.
- **Ranking for an unintended keyword isn't automatically bad** — the default move is to consolidate/optimize toward it if the business genuinely offers that service, not suppress a working, if accidental, lead source.
- **The team is actively moving away from Semrush post-Adobe-acquisition** but hasn't settled on a full replacement — a live, unresolved tooling transition rather than a finished recommendation.
- **Google's AI-driven search shift (zero-click, AI Overviews) is treated as an active experiment internally**, not settled doctrine — presence/volume-over-authority for AI citation, broad-geography content, and Reddit-presence tactics are all framed as "what we're seeing work," with explicit admissions that broad e-commerce/product-term visibility in AI answers remains an unsolved problem.
