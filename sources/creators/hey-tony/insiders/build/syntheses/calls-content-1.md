## 1. Strategic Frameworks (decide priorities before tactics)

- **Human vs. optimized spectrum**: every on-page choice (title tag, keyword placement, tone, hyphenation, grammar) sits on a line between "fully human/natural voice" and "fully optimized/robotic but keyword-perfect." No universal right answer — a deliberate per-business, per-brand call, not an SEO rule. You can knowingly sacrifice a perfect Yoast score for voice, or vice versa. Some businesses (e.g., lawyers) will insist on grammatical/professional correctness over matching sloppy real search phrasing because looking "right" matters more to their brand; others chase conversion and match consumer search behavior even when ungrammatical.
- **Two-question business-priority filter** (use before picking SEO tactics): (1) "What are my priorities for the next 3–6 months?" (revenue/leads vs. authority-building vs. brand) and (2) "Is my business hyperlocal, or do I serve broadly (any state/any English-speaking region)?" The combination determines which levers matter now — there is no single universal rank-order across all businesses.
  - Plenty-of-leads-but-wrong-fit businesses (plastic surgery, law clients getting calls for procedures they don't do): stop chasing more high-traffic/low-competition keywords; narrow/optimize specific service pages that describe exactly what you do and don't do instead.
  - Needs-more-leads, non-hyperlocal businesses: deprioritize Google Business Profile work; prioritize high-traffic/low-competition keyword content + conversion-rate optimization (make "book a call" obvious in layout).
  - Brand-new/near-zero-traffic sites: prioritize low keyword difficulty even at lower volume, to generate visible momentum fast (confidence/motivation play, not just a traffic play).
  - Already-has-traffic-but-wrong-traffic sites: prioritize relevance/qualification over more raw volume.
- **Framework-first, then customize**: when new to a program/strategy, execute every step exactly as taught first to build repetition/muscle memory; only start deviating once you know *why* you'd deviate. "Learn the language before you decide to break it." Bounded by personal/brand values — if a tactic conflicts with what a business is comfortable representing, that's legitimate grounds to deviate.
- **Quantity-then-quality sequencing**: while working through a content backlog, prioritize shipping everything (quantity) over each post's polish. Once caught up and posting on a steady rhythm, shift focus to quality per post. Same logic applies industry-wide: don't create blog categories until you've published ~10–20 posts (categories become obvious in hindsight; boxing yourself in early can itself become a reason not to write).
- **Funnel mapping (TOFU/MOFU/BOFU) for topic ideation**: bottom-funnel = searcher already knows they need your exact service (e.g., "web design" — high intent, lower volume). Middle/top-funnel = searcher doesn't yet realize your service is the fix (e.g., "why aren't I getting business," "why is X not working on Squarespace"). Most available search volume sits in middle/top-funnel, not bottom — don't stay stuck only answering bottom-funnel questions.
  - Client-discovery question to surface middle/top-funnel topics: *"If I could wave a magic wand and give you anything, what problem would I solve?"* The literal answer (e.g., "I want consistent leads") is raw material for topic ideas — write about the underlying problem, not just the service name.
- **Core content litmus test**: "Would my customers actually need this? And read this? If the answer is yes, then publish it."
- **Gut-check qualitative test**: after AI drafts a post, read it back and ask "Am I proud of this?" — use alongside (not instead of) any SEO-plugin score, especially early on.
- **Give yourself grace**: a solo business owner covering SEO + social + everything else is running "multiple full-time jobs" — don't hold yourself to an SEO-team-level cadence or to every best practice simultaneously. Decide priorities deliberately rather than freezing from overwhelm ("if you're not doing these two things for everything, don't waste your time on number 32").

### Positioning & differentiation tactics
- **Repositioning away from head-term competition** (Robert Starling, voiceover artist): stop competing for generic global keywords ("voiceover," "voice actor") against 100,000+ competitors chasing the same big clients. Reposition around a genuine differentiator (his: "48 years of business experience") and target the much larger pool of small/local businesses instead of a few big global ones. Referenced concept: "Acres of Diamonds" — the opportunity is often local/overlooked, not far away.
- **Competitor brand name as a secondary (never primary) keyword**: never target an established competitor's brand name as your primary keyword — you won't outrank them for their own name. Instead work it into body copy as a comparison angle (e.g., "Compared to Suavecito pomade, our pomade is made with 100% organic [ingredient]...") — this can also surface you to people searching the competitor's name directly.
- **Membership/partner program as differentiation** (Glenn): built an unpaid "Preferred Realtor Partner Program" (3 tiers: preferred realtor, interior designer, contractor) as a repositioning strategy ahead of a trade show, instead of directly competing on generic terms.
- **"Found before you're needed" content positioning** (photographer client, pre-dating Kira's SEO era): blog content with nothing to do with the core service, aimed at people before they know they need it (e.g., "how to find the best wedding venue in this area") — builds trust long before the person is shopping for the actual service.
- **Radical-honesty differentiation** ("problems with X" post, core exercise of call 112): write about the downsides/pitfalls of hiring or buying YOUR OWN product/service type. Title pattern: "The problems with hiring/buying [X] that no one talks about." Counterintuitive on purpose — competitors won't write this, so you become the honest/transparent voice. Must be genuinely honest about real pitfalls, never a disguised sales pitch ("we're better because we don't do this").
- **Trademark/USPTO leverage** (David's new trademark): look up your registration number on the USPTO site (a page exists there for it); write a press release framed as "here's what I do," titled with a keyword, linking to the USPTO page and your homepage — helps AI ranking. Also use the USPTO page intermittently as an external link on money/collection/blog pages to signal legitimacy. Must use the **TM** symbol every time the brand name appears, or it can become public property even post-trademark; also register a service mark (**SM**) where available (not in Canada).

## 2. Topic & Keyword Discovery

### Core discovery tools/workflows
- **AnswerThePublic** — type in what you sell, go to **Search Engines → Questions** (skip the "wheel" visualization, scan the plain list). Free tier = **3 free searches/day** even logged in. Explicitly *not* a full keyword-research tool ("it kind of is, but it's not a full thing") — pair with a real keyword tool or GSC for actual volume.
  - **Skip page 1 of results; use page 2 or 3** — these show lower apparent volume, "a lot easier to rank for" (a heuristic/proxy, not a confirmed real-volume number — "we don't actually know the real volume, but it's lower, which... means it's likely easier to rank for").
  - **Narrow/hyperlocal niche fix**: broaden the seed term to the parent category (e.g., search "Pilates" not a hyper-narrow local variant; search "Yellowstone" instead of "Yellowstone tours") rather than appending a city/state — local businesses don't need to geo-modify the seed term. Then contextually tie the offer back in (e.g., "if you're staying at [X RV park], book your tour here").
  - Fixing "only 2 questions returned": try the "all ideas" tab instead of "Questions" only, and test a broader/adjacent middle-funnel phrase, not just the literal bottom-funnel term.
- **findquestions.com** and in-platform **Hey Tony "blog idea finder"** — alternate question-finder tools; type in what you sell.
- **What/How/Where/Who prefix trick**: take your core service term and prefix each way — "what [service]," "how [service]," "where [service]," "who [service]" — good bottom-funnel starting point; pair with funnel-mapping to avoid staying stuck at bottom-funnel only.
- **Long-tail discovery via question stubs in a keyword tool**: type a question stub ("is roll on perfume...", "what roll on perfume...") and read the autosuggest list; add/remove words repeatedly until one shows real volume.
- **AI keyword brainstorm (no paid tool)** — prompt: *"give me some high traffic, low competition keywords [for a web design brand/service] that a [service] can write SEO blog posts about."* Purpose is to get into the customer's headspace for topic ideas — **AI-suggested keyword numbers are NOT accurate for traffic/competition** (that data is gated behind paid tools like SEMrush/Ubersuggest); use AI output for directional/ideation purposes only, never as a substitute for real volume data.

### Competitor sitemap / content-gap method (ChatGPT-based, no paid tool required)
1. Pick a competitor (same niche/city).
2. Go to `[competitor-domain]/sitemap.xml`; if they run Yoast, open the **post sitemap** specifically (isolates blog posts from everything else).
3. Copy the whole sitemap page contents.
4. In ChatGPT: paste "Here's my sitemap first" + your own sitemap, then the competitor's sitemap, then ask: *"Tell me some content that I can write about on my website that likely have an easier keyword difficulty."*
   - Alternate one-shot version: *"Using my competitor['s] sitemap, tell me content gaps that I can write about on my website that likely have an easier difficulty."* (competitor sitemap only, no own-sitemap paste needed)
5. Optional gut-check: run the returned topic through SEMrush/Ubersuggest to confirm low-competition before committing.
6. **Squarespace sitemap-bloat workaround**: if your own sitemap balloons to thousands of per-image lightbox URLs, skip pasting your own sitemap — feed the competitor's sitemap alone.
7. **If competitor sitemap won't load (too long)**: pick a different competitor, manually eyeball their blog, or convert the sitemap to a PDF and upload that instead.
8. **Red flags that mean "pick a different competitor"**: sitemap is a single line with no post links, or no sitemap exists at all — signals they're "probably not doing well on Google."
- Case result: **Jessa** got **24** content-gap ideas this way — "a roadmap for the next six months." **Dr. Karen's** Squarespace sitemap ran to **~5,000 lines** (unusable, all per-image URLs).

### Keyword Gap tool workflow (Semrush / Ubersuggel / Ahrefs — paid tools, more rigorous)
1. Open the competitor-comparison feature: Semrush **"Keyword Gap,"** Ubersuggest **Competitor Analysis → Add Competitor**, or Ahrefs' equivalent.
2. Enter your site + your biggest competitor — **doesn't need to be local/direct**; pick the single biggest player in the niche overall (Matt used neilpatel.com vs. his own SEO agency site — any marketer can benchmark against a big content publisher).
3. Click **View All → filter to "Untapped"** (keywords the competitor ranks for that you don't). Ignore "Shared" keywords (both already rank — targeting again = cannibalization).
4. Sort by keyword difficulty ascending. Start at **0–15** ("very easy"); widen to **0–30** if nothing usable turns up.
5. Pick a keyword that "makes sense" to write about — don't overthink or hand-guess keywords.
6. Click into a candidate to see **variations** if the base term is too generic/head-term to build a title around.
- **Don't pick a same-service/direct competitor for this if they don't blog** — you'll get near-zero usable data (Joyce's Riemann-distributor comparison set yielded nothing; pivot to any publisher/creator in the broader category who does blog and out-write them).
- **Don't try to outrank paid-ad-driven competitors on their branded terms** — e.g., major beauty brands winning via paid traffic, not content; you can't out-content a paid strategy on their exact keyword.
- Results seen live: **47** untapped keywords vs. small local competitor (Socially Infused) vs. **927** untapped vs. neilpatel.com.

### Ignoring/handling Domain Authority
- **Ignore Domain/Authority Score when deciding which difficulty to chase** — Matt calls it "a made up metric," computed differently across Ahrefs/Ubersuggest/Semrush. His own site's Authority Score is **~32–35** and he still only targets easy (0–15) keywords rather than "leveling up." Keyword Difficulty (KD) is better read as roughly the Domain Authority you'd need to realistically rank page 1 for that term (0–100 scale; Semrush color convention: red = harder, green/yellow = easier — always read Volume + KD together, never volume alone).
- Location-page example: "Orlando Web Design" = **1,300/mo volume**, needs **DA ~53** to realistically rank page 1. At DA 4, tested cheaper variants in sequence: "websites Orlando" (irrelevant) → "website agency"/"web design agency" (weaker fit) → landed on **"Orlando web design company"** (lower volume, realistically winnable). Then layered a nearby smaller city (Cocoa Beach, **~20/mo**) as an easy bonus win. Pick the closest big city even if you don't live directly there — footer can still list/link that bigger city name as anchor text without it being a false location claim ("you're not technically saying you're in Orlando, you're just saying web design Orlando") — just never fake a zip code or claim "I live in X."
- **You do not need backlinks/domain authority to rank** for this style of low-competition question content — it accrues naturally with good content over time; don't treat it as a prerequisite for going after a keyword. (Nuance: this applies specifically to sidestepping competitive terms via low-competition questions — Matt is explicit competitive/head terms genuinely need backlinks/authority "yes and no," not that authority is irrelevant everywhere.)

### Word count & keyword-density benchmarking (no fixed formula)
- Don't chase a fixed word count — Google your primary keyword, check the top 2–3 (or top 3) ranking competitors' word count, and use that as the reference. Rule of thumb: **match top 3, then add a bit more** (example: they run ~1,500 words → write ~1,550–1,600). Don't pad with fluff just to match a bigger competitor number if theirs is mostly filler.
- Keyword frequency: same logic, illustrated with the (explicitly "crazy") example — "if they're using the keyword 100 times, you need to use 101 times" — directional principle, not a literal formula.
- Long-tail exact-match phrases (e.g., "best hair pomade for guys") may need the literal phrase repeated a few times to register as the target — balance against natural-sounding copy.

### Misspelled / "wrong" / non-obvious high-volume keywords
- When the top-volume variant for a topic is technically misspelled, backwards, or a layperson term (not the professionally "correct" one): still write the post targeting that exact term/spelling, and use the post itself to explain why it's technically wrong/correct — don't avoid the keyword just because it feels unauthoritative. Recurring tension in reputation-sensitive niches (law, plastic surgery) — some clients insist on correcting rather than adopting the popular-but-"wrong" phrasing, prioritizing professional accuracy over pure keyword-matching; both are legitimate choices.
- Hyphen/spacing/pluralization decisions ("permanent makeup" vs. "permanent-makeup," "ebook" vs. "e-book"): default toward matching real competitor/consumer search behavior over strict grammatical correctness — unless the brand's values call for correctness. "If Google doesn't know the difference between a dash and a space, we've got bigger problems" — don't obsess over the literal form when a tool only surfaces one.
- Multi-language keyword-variant choice has no universal answer (e.g., Dutch has ~4 words for "training"; German market split between hyphenated/non-hyphenated forms of a term) — test different variants across separate posts and compare in Search Console rather than solving it theoretically; fine to vary the word used across posts rather than force one term everywhere.
- Cross-tool disagreement is real and expected (same term can show meaningfully different volume/difficulty across tools) — treat numbers as directional. When two tools sharply disagree on difficulty (example: "dog anxiety at night" — Ubersuggest **44** vs. Ahrefs **1**): refresh metrics in the outlier tool first; if still divergent, **trust Ahrefs over Ubersuggest 100% of the time**.

### Event/service & recurring-vs-one-off content architecture
1. Pick a keyword tool (Semrush or KeySearch); search close variants of event name + city (e.g., "Paint Night Toronto" vs. "Paint and Sip Toronto").
2. Compare volume/difficulty across variants, pick the best tradeoff, check what your current ranking page targets and consider swapping.
3. **Recurring events** (e.g., weekly paint nights): build a dedicated **service page** — keyword worked in repeatedly, embedded signup calendar, experience description — rather than per-instance listings.
4. **One-off events**: keep on Eventbrite, Meetup, Facebook (Events/Pages) instead of building a page per occurrence.
5. **Internal-linking structure**: keep/build a broader umbrella page (e.g., "fun things to do in Toronto") linking to the narrower dedicated service page ("Paint and Sip Toronto"), and link back the other way too; repeat the dedicated-page treatment for each other recurring offering (pottery, team building).
- Live numbers: "Paint Night Toronto" ≈ **20 volume**; "Paint and Sip Toronto" ≈ **50 volume, 21 difficulty** ("pretty easy to rank").

### Secondary keywords
- Prompt: *"Please give me 10 secondary keywords that are SEO optimized"* (paired with the primary keyword). Only use the ones that naturally fit — no obligation to work every suggestion in.
- One practitioner's personal (explicitly not team-confirmed) rule: skip volume/difficulty scoring entirely for secondary keywords, just check topical relevance — "secondary keywords are a dance," and she predicts the technique may matter less within ~2 years as AI gets better at detecting genuine topical coverage without surface keyword-stuffing signals (her own speculation, not settled fact).

### Reverse-engineering AI citations for topic ideas
- When you prompt an AI tool (ChatGPT etc.) about a client and ask it to search the web, it surfaces "bubbles" showing which sources it pulled from and why. Track this over time per client/query to learn which sources a given AI trusts (e.g., Reddit) and why citations shift day to day.
- **Rank-Week method reapplied to AI citations**: treat the AI's cited sources like page-2/3 competitors — compare their word count/approach against your own content to find gaps.
- **Reddit-as-AnswerThePublic**: scrape Reddit threads (via Claude) as an AnswerThePublic-style tool to pull real user questions into blog topics.
- **Genuine Reddit participation** (Tara's approach, results-backed): comment authentically in niche-relevant threads as someone who's actually used the product/service — got "almost immediate sales" recommending a remodeling client's service this way. Contrast: "infiltrating" threads while secretly being the business is common among small businesses but not something Kira does with clients.

## 3. Keyword Cannibalization

- **Definition (the only real definition)**: more than one page or blog post on your own site ranking/competing for the exact same keyword. It is about the page's **topic**, not about link destinations — many different blog posts can all link to the same money page without being cannibalization, as long as each post's own topic differs from the money page's topic and from each other.
- **Different locations = different keywords by definition** — "we buy junk cars, Dallas" and "we buy junk cars, Houston" are not cannibalization no matter how similar the surrounding template copy is, provided the exact-match keyword differs and the location-specific content is genuinely substantive (don't just swap the city name in an identical template — write real copy about why that location needs this).
- **Topical authority via many similar pages is fine** (and is not cannibalization) as long as (a) no two pages exact-match the same keyword and (b) the content is meaningfully different, not re-skinned — "it's a band aid: you use it once, you don't reuse it, you just do one" (re: one keyword per page).
- **Never A/B test two pages/posts for the same keyword** — that IS cannibalization. A/B testing works for Instagram Reels or YouTube titles/thumbnails but structurally doesn't work for blog posts/pages chasing the same keyword. Test variants sequentially over time instead, and note that any comparison is confounded once the pages differ by keyword too.
- **Fix cannibalization in sequence when multiple content types compete**: collection pages first, then product pages, then blogs (one keyword per page).
- **Never rewrite/republish a post to "refresh" it** — edit the existing post in place in the CMS regardless of its original publish date, rather than creating a second entry. ("Just update it. Instead of rewriting it, just update the existing one.") A literal copy-paste repost (vs. a genuinely edited update) is a real cannibalization risk — the line matters and is easy to blur (one member wasn't sure which she'd done).
- **Differentiate near-duplicate topics by content format/angle, not just wording** — e.g., one page as a listicle ("12 ways to use honeysuckle perfume") vs. another as an explainer ("what's in honeysuckle roll-on"); or vary angle across posts about the same product (best features / worst features / pros-and-cons) rather than repeating the same angle.
- **FAQ + a full blog post on the same question can coexist** without cannibalizing, as long as the FAQ only partially/briefly covers it and the full topic can support its own page — treat as a gut check ("if this page literally sounds exactly like this other page, that's a signal to adjust"), not a bright-line rule.
- **Don't put your core service keyword in a blog title if a dedicated money page already targets that exact term** — unless the post's topic is literally about choosing/comparing the service (e.g., "How do you choose a website management service" is fine).
- **Guest posts you publish on someone else's site**: fine even if the topic mildly overlaps your own business, since it still links back to you regardless — but do NOT also publish the same/similar piece on your own site afterward (creates competing near-duplicate content against a page that's already sending you a backlink).

## 4. The AI-Assisted Blog-Writing Workflow (core method + exact prompts)

### End-to-end sequence
1. Pick a topic/question you can **already answer today** — not one requiring research.
2. Sanity-check competition in Semrush/Ubersuggest before committing (see Section 2).
3. Open a blank doc (Google Docs/Word), title it with the keyword/question — same title becomes the blog post's working title.
4. Paste the **interview prompt** into ChatGPT (or Claude) so it interviews you before drafting anything.
5. If it asks too many questions (can default to ~30), cap it: *"ask me only 10 questions."*
6. Answer via **ChatGPT Voice Mode** (desktop or phone) — talk through real answers like explaining to someone with zero background ("this is how I explain SEO to my mom"); rambling/long-winded answers are fine, they add personality. Budget **~20–30 minutes** for 10 questions (Matt logged ~10 minutes per pass, doubled once when a voice transcript failed to save).
7. Let it produce the full draft from your answers.
8. **Never publish the first draft as-is.** Review each section — is it actually helpful or a thin bullet list?
9. For weak/bullet-heavy sections, feed back a **rewrite-to-prose prompt** (see below), section by section if needed; follow up with "elaborate a little bit" if the rewrite becomes too thin.
10. Apply a **word-count ceiling** if the draft runs long (Matt used 250 words in one case; general target is matching top-3 SERP results, see Section 2).
11. Where a paragraph covers several distinct ideas, prompt for **H3 subheadings** to break it up.
12. Strip AI "tells" manually: horizontal divider lines/spacer bars (paste in as stray blocks in WordPress), random/unnecessary **bold** text, **emojis** (considered unprofessional/an AI tell in blog copy — fine in texting/social, not blogs), **checkmark-style bullets**, plain **numbered list markers (1, 2, 3…)**, generic **"Conclusion"/"Final Thoughts"** headers (replace with a specific, content-tied closer), and the AI-tell closing phrase **"and finally..."**.
13. Verify every first-person experience/results claim the AI wrote ("I have actually done this," "I've helped businesses rank for...") against what's actually true for you — cut anything inaccurate.
14. Remove jargon/framework references the reader won't recognize (e.g., cut "Big Five" from a title/body when readers don't know the term, even if you used it as an ideation lens).
15. Add ~1 external link to a credible outside source.
16. Add internal links (Section 5).
17. Add a **"Key Takeaways"** or **"TL;DR"** summary block near the top if desired (prompt below) — Matt calls this an open experiment: "sometimes it really works, sometimes it doesn't."
18. Ask AI to smooth the ending: *"Make this sound better and natural as a finisher for the blog post."*
19. Generate a meta description (prompt below); pick the best of ~5 returned if multiple are generated.
20. Paste into WordPress, run **Clear Formatting**, manually rebuild real bulleted/numbered lists, assign **one category** (never tags — see Section 8), add featured image + alt text, set focus keyphrase, save as draft, **Preview** to confirm internal links rendered, then publish.
21. Post the published link into the community accountability thread; review at least one other member's post (heading tags, links, formatting).

### Exact prompts (verbatim, by function)

**Interview/ideation prompt** (fullest form, combine elements as needed):
> "I'm writing a blog post answering this question: [question]. Ask me any questions you need to know the answer to in order to create a truly unique, helpful piece of content that uses my expertise, experience, opinions, case studies, etc. Ask me one question at a time and wait for me to finish talking / wait for my answer before moving to the next question."
- Cap variant: *"ask me only 10 questions"* / "keep it limited to ten questions."
- Note: the more you've used ChatGPT with a given account over time, the more it "knows you" from prior chats and can pull in your expertise/case studies automatically (it inferred a services-page URL without being told, not even in the sitemap). If new to it (fewer than ~6–8 posts of history), lean harder on the interview prompt; a formal ChatGPT "Project" with saved instructions is a viable alternative to a running conversation but wasn't demoed live. You can also upload a book, white paper, or past posts as source material for case studies/experience (Matt uploaded his entire book).

**Rewrite bullets → prose** (consolidate the core direction; near-verbatim variants actually used):
> "Rewrite the following. I don't want it to be a bunch of bullet points — that's not helpful. Elaborate on it a bit and ensure there's at least one paragraph. It can still include bullets if useful for understanding, just not as the whole section."
- Variant length-control add-on: *"This is a bit long now — shorten it to like three to four paragraphs, and don't end it with 'finally.'"*
- Variant length ceiling: *"Same with this, but keep it under 250 words."*
- Variant subheading breakup: *"Let's do some H3 tags or something to break this up a bit."*

**Content-gap-from-sitemap prompts** — see Section 2.

**Internal-linking prompt** (paste your sitemap + the finished draft):
> "Using my sitemap, give me three internal linking opportunities from this blog post [using existing text]."
- Follow-up for placement: *"Anchor text, where to place it — right after you mention [X]... give me a sentence where I can add this."*
- Money-page variant: *"Give me an internal linking opportunity up to my [service] page from this post"* — it can infer the right page from prior conversation context even without being fed the URL.

**Featured-image prompt** (attach 2–3 existing on-brand images as style references):
> "Using the style of the images attached, create me a featured/cover image for this blog post. Here is [the topic] — you don't need any text on the image (no title, no question). [Optional mood direction, e.g.: it should be someone who is frustrated or has questions and isn't sure what to do.]"
- Don't use the same images as before; regenerate if unwanted text appears or the tool ignores extra reference images (not fully reliable on a single pass).

**Key Takeaways / TL;DR prompt:**
> "Give me a TL;DR at the top of this blog post. Five points max. Call it 'Key Summary.'" (Prefer "Key Takeaways"/"Key Summary" over "TL;DR" — that term reads as Reddit-native/casual; TL;DR is still fine if you prefer it.)

**Meta description prompt:**
> "Write me a killer, compelling, click-worthy meta description for this page." (Generate ~5, pick the best.)

**Infographic prompt:**
> "Make an infographic for this page."

**Big-Five ideation prompt** (Marcus Sheridan's "Big Five" framework, from his book *Endless Customers* — used to avoid just re-summarizing top-ranked competitor lists):
1. First have ChatGPT summarize each of the top-3 ranking articles for your target keyword ("summarize this," one at a time — "training ChatGPT" on what's ranking).
2. Then: *"I only want 20 blog post ideas that work for any business, not for specific industries. The keyword I'm trying to rank for is [X]. I want you to come up with topics based on Marcus Sheridan's Big Five blog posts."*
3. Then: *"Write me a blog post based on these blog post ideas. Knowing that the keyword is [X], it should be helpful, unique, and include my personal expertise, experience, case studies, etc. Use any relevant info from previous chats with you to do that."*
- Only one Big Five category was elaborated on this call — **cost & pricing** — sub-angles: how much does this cost, what factors affect price, is it worth the cost, cheap-vs-expensive breakdown of what you actually get.
- Add one external link to a credible outside source backing the named framework (e.g., linking to Sheridan's own site — credibility citation, not competing for his branded term).

### Editing/humanizing quality bar
- Content does **not** need to be "100% human," but does need to be **"100% helpful"** and use your own personal expertise/experience. "It's not really tricking SEO" to weave your own offer into educational content — the actual violation is a **bait-and-switch** (searcher wants X, content only pitches unrelated Y); a genuine, relevant tie-in is fine.
- AI-generated images are fine for SEO ranking purposes — one practitioner used them across "many, many" ranking articles with no observed penalty (parallel drawn to Google's earlier resistance-then-acceptance of AI-written content: "it just has to be helpful").
- EM dashes / other AI "spacer" tells are, as of the relevant call, **not a confirmed Google ranking/crawling penalty** — a style/authenticity concern, not an algorithmic one, and moot regardless since raw ChatGPT output should never be copy-pasted straight to the site.
- Bilingual/multilingual posts: don't run an entire post through ChatGPT translation in one shot (content gets mixed up/dropped) — translate section by section. Set **hreflang** correctly between language versions.
- Multi-model habit: Claude, Gemini, and ChatGPT kept open simultaneously; run the same prompt through all three and pick the best or mix between them — quality varies day to day between models. No one has definitively proven a single "best" model for this workflow as of these calls (Matt was actively testing Claude for blog-writing at the time of several calls, having previously used ChatGPT primarily); chat commentary at one point: "Claude's great for writing, ChatGPT is great for brainstorming and strategy" — presented as anecdotal, not settled.

## 5. Internal Linking

- **Standard rule**: **3–5 internal links per new post** (most guidance converges on ~3–4; one call's rule: "3 to 5, usually about 4" — older guidance of "do a bunch, like 10" was walked back as too spammy-looking). **At least one of those must point to a money/service page** — if you don't have a dedicated service page, point it at whatever you actually sell (e.g., an affiliate page) or the homepage instead.
- **Reciprocal/inbound linking (separate, equally important step)**: after publishing, go back and edit **2–3 other existing, already-indexed posts** to add a link pointing **into** the brand-new post. This is bidirectional — not just outbound from the new post. Purpose: Google already crawls those older indexed pages regularly, so it discovers the new page fast instead of waiting to stumble onto an orphaned page on its own ("the lazy way to do internal links" = a plain-paragraph, non-heading "Related: [Post Title]" link when no natural anchor phrase exists in the existing text). Skip adding a link to a page that already has "a lot of internal links... almost too many."
  - Practical finder method: Google your own target phrase (or search your own WP admin for a relevant word) to locate your already-ranking related posts, then hyperlink a relevant word/phrase in each to the new post.
  - Choose which existing pages to prioritize by current rank: leave pages already at **#2–3** alone (already getting clicks); focus new internal links on pages at the **bottom of page 1 or lower**.
- **Anchor text rule**: ideally the exact primary keyword you're trying to rank the target page for; if that doesn't fit naturally, use something close/similar (does not need to be the literal post title). A blog's own title already contains its keyword, so linking that title elsewhere in a post counts as a "free" second internal link. Anchor text should be descriptive of the destination page's actual topic (e.g., anchor "cosmetic tattoo" → the page actually about cosmetic tattoo) — never generic phrasing.
- **Volume ceiling**: don't exceed roughly **50–100 internal links** on a single page — unreadable for people and a signal to Google that something's off. "You'll actually get more out of doing less."
- **Linking to not-yet-published/scheduled posts** — no perfectly clean fix, two workarounds: (1) link to the future post's URL slug now and accept a temporary broken link for a few days until it goes live; (2) keep a tracking spreadsheet logging each post's scheduled live date, revisit weekly, and backfill the links once posts go live (can be delegated to an assistant — one real example: checked and executed every Friday).
- **Guest posts are the exception**: only **one** money-keyword link to your own site (not 3 internal links like a normal post), plus one link to an existing relevant piece already on the host site.
- **Consolidating fragmented sub-sites**: rather than splitting topically-related content (activities, rentals, charters) across several separate sister sites that cross-link back and forth, put it all on the one primary site the topic most belongs to — multi-hop cross-site navigation "loses people."

## 6. External Linking

- **1–2 external links per post**, to credible/authoritative sources.
- **Never link out to a page targeting the same keyword you're trying to rank for** — that hands your competitor extra "link juice" toward the exact ranking you want. Workflow: write the post first, then go back and place external links to sources clearly unrelated to your own target keyword (e.g., link to a shoe brand for footwear pairing inside a shirt-focused post).
- Purpose framing: an external link shows "I have an external link that's showing that I'm not just making all this stuff up" — cite Google's own documentation for a concept (example: linking the word "crawl" to Google's "Overview of crawling" page), or a named framework's source site (Marcus Sheridan's "Big Five").
- **Nuance**: this guidance is strongest for blog content specifically — Matt's own core "search engine optimization" service page has **zero** external links and still ranks well, while his web design services page does link out (Chamber of Commerce, Google Reviews, Bark.com). Not a rigid universal rule for every page type — check what competitors do on their equivalent money pages too. Contact/About pages are exempt from linking minimums generally.
- Trademark/USPTO pages and other third-party legitimacy signals (see Section 1) can double as periodic external links on money/collection/blog pages.

## 7. Images

- **Density**: roughly **one image, video, or chart every 200–300 words** — "as often as makes sense," more if the topic is naturally visual (data, cost breakdowns, screenshots of social proof), less if conceptual. Explicitly called an unrealistic target for a solo operator/small team, not a hard requirement. Separate guidance: **at least 4 images per post** minimum, ideally one per H2 section if budget allows. Every post needs at minimum a hero/featured image, even a plain text-heavy one.
- **Sourcing options**:
  1. **Unsplash** — free, royalty-free; search using the literal blog title/question as the search term.
  2. **Own media library** — reuse an existing on-brand style already used elsewhere on the site.
  3. **AI image generation** (ChatGPT/Gemini) — see prompt in Section 4. Cost framing: **~$20/month now** for effectively unlimited images vs. "**2 or 300 bucks**" historically paid per image. Not fully reliable on a single pass (unwanted baked-in text, ignoring extra reference images) — regenerate as needed.
  4. **Adobe Stock** (paid) alongside Pexels (free) — deliberately limit yourself to a small, consistent, provable set of licensed sources so licensing can be proven if challenged (a real copyright claim against one site was closed by producing the Pexels/Adobe Stock license).
  5. Turn the post into an **infographic** (prompt: "make an infographic for this page") — compress and embed, then separately post to Pinterest; infographics are a content type other sites sometimes re-embed with credit, which becomes a backlink.
- **No on-image text** — instruct the AI generator explicitly not to bake in text (title, question) on the image itself.
- **Compression is mandatory**: check file size first (Get Info); if over ~1MB, run through a free PNG→WEBP converter (or Photoshop **Save As WebP at ~70% quality**) before uploading — keeps site speed fast. Real before/after examples: 537 KB PNG → 32 KB WebP; 1.1MB → 27KB; 1.9MB → 102KB ("looks exactly the same"). Rename the file to match the post's exact target keyword; set **alt text = the same title/keyword text**.
- **Canva copyright trap**: never export a bare/unmodified stock element straight from Canva's library — violates Canva's terms; Canva has directly contacted site owners over this, demanding takedown or payment. Workaround: drag a stock element in, then add something on top (title text, an outline, another graphic) **before** exporting — even if partially cropped/hidden, that edit is what keeps the export compliant. Canva Pro ≈ **$20/month**; background remover called out as excellent.
- **Batch images separately from writing** (time-management tactic): publish the post text-only first rather than let missing images block publishing; set aside a dedicated later block (e.g., 30 minutes) to drop images into several already-published posts at once (one practitioner added images ~6 weeks after posting with no penalty).
- **Don't chase people who re-post your images/video without credit** — not worth the hassle; a takedown/credit request is optional, litigation is "a huge, huge hassle," and content volume produced makes it a non-issue. A copyright notice at the bottom of posts is a low-confidence, low-cost habit some practitioners keep without proof it helps ("I don't know that it matters").
- **Logo on featured images**: optional, not required.
- Cross-posting images/infographics to **Pinterest** (and low-effort posting on Tumblr/Medium) can drive traffic, help Pinterest growth, and send weak engagement signals to Google — post "when you can" rather than committing to constant activity.

## 8. Publishing Mechanics & CMS-Specific Fixes

### Universal WordPress rules
- **Categories: exactly one per post** (occasionally "1–2" per other guidance) — never leave "Uncategorized." Keep total sitewide categories to **5–10 max**; don't create categories until you've published ~10–20 posts.
- **Never use tags** — "nobody uses them properly," they "create a bunch of redundant pages that bloat your website."
- Always run **Clear Formatting** after pasting AI content into the WordPress editor — strips inherited bold/font-weight/inline styles while preserving heading tags; then manually rebuild any real bulleted/numbered lists.
- Set a **featured image + alt text** (alt text = the image/post title or exact keyword).
- Set the SEO plugin's **focus keyphrase** field to the exact target keyword/question; make the **URL slug** match the primary keyword too.
- **Disable comments sitewide** — "generally it's just spam."
- Save as draft frequently while working; use **Preview** before publishing to confirm internal links actually rendered/landed.
- **Publish as soon as it's ready** — "the sooner you can get it published, the better because then it'll start indexing." Don't wait for the site/images to be perfect if you have a backlog (SEO's ~3-month lag means early, imperfect publishing costs nothing — "no one's gonna see it" yet anyway).

### Yoast-specific tactics
- **Triage order**: don't chase orange→green first. Go through every page/post still **red** (or blank/no score) and fix those first — older sites built before these plugins existed usually have the most pages missing basics entirely.
- **Meta-description fix**: if the keyword is present but the description still scores yellow, move it into the **first five words** — this alone can flip yellow→green with no other change.
- **Don't blindly trust Yoast Premium's AI-generated title/meta suggestions** — a suggestion can score green while dropping the geo+service keyword that actually drives relevance (example: "photography services that elevate your brand" scored green over "Hamilton wedding photographer"). Always check AI suggestions preserve location + service terms.
- Yoast's per-post internal-link-count view (Posts list) shows inbound/outbound link counts per post — used live to catch posts sitting at only 1 internal link (needs more) vs. 16/9/3 (fine).
- **Contested reliability**: one participant's view, read into the record — Yoast/SEO-plugin scores are "not data driven," often based on title length etc. rather than real ranking data; treat as a directional gut-check and favor real ranking/traffic data when they conflict. Matt's team makes the same point independently: "you can't get 100, and getting 100 doesn't mean it's better optimized than 50" — automated readability/content scores (Flesch reading ease, passive-voice %, transition-word %) can be flat-out wrong even on long, legitimate pages.

### Platform-specific bugs/workarounds
- **Shopify H1/H2 tag mixing** — a theme code issue, cannot be fixed from the standard page editor; requires going into "Edit code" on the theme. Self-fixable with ChatGPT's help or a community dev member's help. Confirmed to persist even on a newer theme (a "Craft" theme still had the bug) — theme age doesn't guarantee it's fixed.
- **Shopify blogging is generally weak** — one practitioner moved her blog off Shopify entirely to a Wix subdomain for this reason.
- **Squarespace** sitemap bloat (per-image lightbox URLs, thousands of lines) breaks the competitor-gap-analysis workflow — use only the competitor's sitemap in that case. Squarespace can still rank fine for SEO even without top site speed, as long as standard on-page SEO is otherwise followed. Fiverr's site-speed specialist recommendation (below) is Shopify-only and does **not** apply to Squarespace.
- **SiteGround** DNS/crawling quirks — a DNS setting removal fixed sitemap recognition for one site; full crawling reliability on SiteGround remains a known limitation the platform side can't fully resolve. Crawler fallback logic observed: "the tool sends a bot... if the site says no bots, it tries headless... then tries impersonating a human" (3–4 redundancy fallbacks). CrockWeb.com offered as a hosting alternative/migration target (no affiliate relationship); can buy a plan then ask them to migrate the WordPress site from SiteGround.
- **Fiverr Shopify site-speed specialist** — the one exception to Matt's general Fiverr skepticism ("the only time... you're going to hear me say, go to Fiverr"). Roughly **$100**, premium tiers available, **Shopify only**.

## 9. Sitemaps, Indexing & Search Console

- **Getting the sitemap**: append `/sitemap.xml` to a domain (may redirect to `sitemap_index.xml`). On Yoast sites, open the specific **post-sitemap.xml** entry (not page-sitemap.xml) to isolate blog posts — select-all/copy for pasting into AI tools.
- **Submitting the correct sub-sitemap in GSC**: don't paste one giant sitemap dump. Open the root sitemap.xml, find the sub-sitemap you actually want (e.g., a "blogs" sitemap listed inside the index), and submit that specific sub-sitemap path (e.g., `sitemap/blogs`) in Search Console.
- **Sitemaps auto-update and get auto-recrawled** by Google on their own — no need to manually resubmit every time you publish new content. You don't technically need an XML sitemap to rank, but having one "helps a lot."
- **Manual GSC indexing request after publishing is optional, not required** — Google typically finds new content on its own; if it hasn't been found after a couple weeks to a month, then request manual indexing. No downside to requesting every time either.
- **Indexing/ranking timeline**: allow **roughly 3 weeks to 1 month** depending on how often Google crawls the site; a "0" in GSC right after publishing is not a failure. Crawl frequency after any update/edit depends entirely on how often Google already crawls that specific site (sites publishing regularly for years get crawled far more often than sites updating once a year) — not a fixed universal number of days.
- **GSC property history only starts at registration/connection date** — a site live since e.g. 2022 but connected to GSC only in November will show data starting in November, not the site's true full history.
- **Checking real ranking**: don't just Google your own query in a normal browser (personalization skews results) — check Google Search Console for that specific URL/query instead. (A member asked about using a VPN to avoid personalized results; not confirmed or denied — GSC + patience was the redirect.)
- **New content traction timeline**: can take **3–6 months** (varies by site) before any real views/traction appear — zero views after a few early posts is not a red flag.

## 10. Cadence, Patience & Timelines

- **Publishing cadence** (multiple data points, no single universal number):
  - Agency/client default cadence at Hey Tony: **2–3 blog posts/week** (also stated elsewhere as "2 blogs/week or 3 blogs/week," i.e., ~8/month or ~12/month packages); Hey Tony's own actual process discloses **two deliverables/week per client**: one new blog post + one existing service/product page rewrite/optimization.
  - Solo-operator realistic cadence: **~1 post/week** is fine — don't hold yourself to more than you can sustain; posting 2x/week produces "a lot more progress" than 1x/week but 1x/week is still genuinely valuable, not an inferior fallback.
  - If posting 2x/week, **never publish both on the same day** — space them out (e.g., Monday/Thursday or Tuesday/Friday); consistency of cadence matters more than which specific days.
  - Historical industry lesson: once AI made mass content production possible, sites that pushed to **~500 pages/week** saw quality (and rankings) drop — described as Google "retaliating" against low-quality mass output, expected not a bug.
- **Refresh cadence**: revisit old posts "every few months or so" — at minimum update the published date to look current; ideally also revise the content. Blog posts are living documents (unlike, e.g., YouTube) — you can go back anytime to add images, edit copy, even swap which keyword a post targets.
- **Check performance ~once a month, not daily** — checking daily "causes disappointment" and "is what causes people to give up with SEO." Want guaranteed instant results? "That's what ads are for, not SEO." SEO is "a marathon, not a sprint."
- **Ship the backlog now**: if you have a stack of written-but-unpublished posts (ranges cited: 12–30), start publishing rather than waiting for the site/images to be perfect — nothing is lost given the ~3-month SEO lag anyway.
- **Client auto-publish trust**: default is client pre-approval; ask around **month 3–4** of the relationship whether they'll allow auto-publish going forward. Clients who allow it "typically get the best results" (an observed pattern, not a rigorously isolated causal claim).
- **Testing a "spammy-feeling" news cadence** (multiple posts/day): publish anyway if each is genuinely non-duplicate/real news, then watch traffic for a couple of months — cut frequency only if traffic plateaus, not preemptively.
- **Experimentation discipline**: don't run a structural/stylistic experiment on every single post — space tests out to roughly every **2nd–4th post** so you can actually attribute what worked (Matt deliberately added only 2 reciprocal links instead of the recommended 3 on one demo post specifically to observe the isolated impact).
- **Content is a volume game as much as a quality game**: "not everything is going to hit, but when things do start hitting, that lifts everything up across the site" — a single strong post can lift the whole site's clicks/impressions, not just its own page.

## 11. AI Overviews / AI Citation Strategy

- **Core research finding** (Matt built a custom AI agent analyzing ~100 search queries comparing who's cited in Google's AI Overviews vs. who ranks page 1 organically): **ranking #1 organically does NOT reliably correlate with being cited in AI Overviews.**
- **Mechanism, from manually inspecting cited source content**: citation isn't about heading tags or formatting. Google's AI generates a claim/statistic first, then looks for content that **supports** that claim.
- **The tactic**: find "Google's claim" by Googling the exact query/question you want to rank for and reading what the AI Overview asserts and cites. Then **state that claim first in your content, and pivot afterward** with your differentiated info — don't contradict the claim outright.
  - Worked example: if the AI Overview states "average kitchen renovation costs $50–75K" and your real price is $25–35K, writing "we do it for $25–35K" alone won't get you cited (you're contradicting the claim). Instead: *"the average kitchen renovation costs between $50 and $75,000 in [state/area]. However, we can do it for $25,000."* This backs up Google's claim (raising citation odds) while still delivering genuinely differentiated info to the reader.
- **Origin story validating the tactic**: Matt's own "how long does it take to rank on Google" Q&A post; 5 days later a competitor copied the exact title/idea but wrote it to align with Google's stated claims, and got cited in AI Overviews despite publishing after Matt.
- **Verification limits**: no reliable automated way exists yet to confirm AI-Overview citation impact — manual Googling of your exact target query is the only check. GSC's AI Overview reporting is an incomplete rollout (visible for only 1 of Matt's clients at time of call) — don't rely on it yet.
- **Competitive/legal exposure**: it's "technically all right" for a competitor to run your original content through AI and reword/paraphrase it to compete for the same citation. The one carve-out: a genuinely original study/proprietary data set can't legitimately be claimed as a competitor's own.
- **Superlative claims without backing** (e.g., a competitor's unsubstantiated "premier photographer in Atlanta"): likely won't get demoted by Google, per Matt's belief, but also won't earn the citation/credibility lift that a reviews-or-evidence-backed claim will — the risk isn't punishment, it's simply no lift.

## 12. Backlinks & PR

### 12a. Press releases (full ABNewswire workflow)
1. Gather **5–6 reviews max** (site won't use more anyway) from Google Business Profile or any source — screenshots fine, non-Google reviews (DMs, texts) acceptable. **No reviews yet** (brand-new business)? Angle as a launch/new-in-market announcement instead (e.g., "[Business] launches as [City]'s newest [service]"). **Nothing to announce at all**? Fall back to a milestone/anniversary angle (e.g., "[Business] celebrates 20 years in business").
2. Paste Matt's Claude press-release prompt (posted in Insiders community as "press release prompt" — not read verbatim on the call; retrieve from the community post) into a new Claude chat (split into two pastes if too long). Drop review screenshots into the same chat.
3. Output includes: "For Immediate Release" line + dateline/city, a headline, a body opening with an aggregate-rating claim (e.g., "holds a 5-star average across 111 Google reviews"), a placeholder owner quote to swap for a real one, quotes pulled from actual reviews, and an "About [Company]" boilerplate + contact info.
4. Optional: paste into a Google Doc/Word to edit — swap in the real owner quote, fix placeholder/location mismatches.
5. Create a free ABNewswire.com account, pick a pricing tier (see Rules), click "Add Press Release."
6. **Title field = headline only** (leave "For Immediate Release" in the body/content box, not the title). Paste full body into the content box. Write/have AI draft a **500-character-or-less summary**.
7. Upload an image: a real GBP/review screenshot, or an AI-generated composite (headshot + 5-star graphic + a real review quote overlaid). Skip the optional image-description/quote field.
8. Add links (**platform allows up to 5, use only 2–3**): e.g., a Google Reviews link + a website/service-page link, using a real keyword phrase as anchor text (e.g., "SEO Agency based in Hamilton"). Get your **CID link** (via whatismycid.com — paste your Google Maps listing link in, get the CID link back) and use it instead of a generic Maps/place link for anything pointing at your GBP — Matt's claim: the CID link "passes authority," the generic place link doesn't (his working theory, not independently verified).
9. Select **~3 relevant categories** (seen: Media and Communications, Website and Blog, Manufacturing, Personal Finance, Marketing and Sales, Books and Literature, regional UK/US options, no Canada option).
10. Fill in company details (address unverified by the platform — any address works; use a real contact email). Set **PR Type to "web only."** Select all available syndication outlets. **Skip the meta tags section entirely.**
11. Set publishing schedule to same-day, preview to confirm links render, submit. Expect **~1 day turnaround**; email confirmation with links to where it was picked up (e.g., USA Today, syndication partners).
12. **If rejected for formatting** (line breaks/page breaks/paragraph length): paste the rejection notice + your release text back into Claude/ChatGPT, ask it to fix per the feedback, resubmit.

**Rules**: one release = **one topic/business/location/keyword** (never combine two businesses, two locations, or multiple service keywords — same discipline as one keyword per page). New company/location: don't publish the "we're open" release until it's actually open. DBA/rename in progress but not yet on Google: fine to publish now, just note "also doing business as [new name]." Relocating: fine to publish an arriving-in-market release now. Multiple services: fine to eventually do one release per service, spaced out. **Frequency: 1–2 press releases/month max** (contrast: directory listings run at a separate, previously-taught **8–10/month** cadence — don't confuse the two). If doing several, wait **at least ~2 weeks** between them. Image must always be a real screenshot, never stock. Don't add a press-release section/link on your own website (self-referential, no SEO value) — instead post about it (with a real link) on LinkedIn/X/Facebook, and on Instagram post a screenshot without the link. Proactively contact local newspapers after it goes live ("we were featured here — want to do a write-up or exclusive interview?"). Ethics: fine to headline "[Business] named best choice for X" when genuinely sourced from real reviews — normal PR practice, not deceptive, as long as something real backs the claim (reviews, an award, a study, even a pay-to-enter contest win). Press-release copy does **not** need a de-AI-ification/humanizing pass the way blog posts do.

**Pricing**: **$80** = web distribution only. **+$30 USA Today add-on = $110 total** (Matt's recommended tier if budget allows). **~$500 bulk-credit tier** ("the 501") — cheaper per unit, meant for people running lots of tests; don't buy upfront unless you already know you'll test repeatedly. Full human-reviewed press-release service (manual submission, human review) costs **~$1,500–$2,000** — this AI+ABNewswire DIY approach is explicitly the cheaper "B tier/C tier" alternative.

**Verification**: no dedicated tracking tool — manually Google your exact target query to check. Not "buying backlinks" in the penalized sense — standard PR practice (new business, new client, award, great reviews), not manipulative.

### 12b. Guest posting (The Plaid Zebra exercise, generalizable process)
1. Find a real user question via a question-finder tool (findquestions.com or Hey Tony's blog idea finder) by typing in what you sell.
2. Use the recurring ChatGPT interview prompt (same one used weekly) to draft the post.
3. Unlike a normal own-site post (3 internal links), a guest post gets only **one link**: a money-keyword anchor to your own site, **plus one link to an existing article already on the host site** (search the host's own search bar for a relevant existing piece to link to).
4. Write as a Google Doc (title = H1/URL slug); set sharing to **"Anyone with the link can view."**
5. Provide featured image (and optionally one in-body image) as a **separate file**, not embedded — resize to **750px wide**, smallest file size possible.
6. Submit via the intake form (name, email, doc link, image); watch for the submission deadline and expected rollout pace (this round: deadline Friday, ~3 posts/day published, rolling out over ~30 days).
7. Posts also get shared on the host's Facebook page after publishing (partly framed as "influence AI" via broader distribution signals).

**Rules**: never write a guest post in a self-promotional tone (no packages/pricing/"hire me") — keep it generic/topical with a CTA-style link at the end. Don't publish the same/similar piece on your own site afterward (competes with yourself as near-duplicate content). If a candidate topic feels too similar to your own site's content, broaden/narrow the angle (e.g., "photographer" instead of "newborn photographer") or skip it for now.

### 12c. Other backlink tactics
- **featured.com** (successor to HARO/"Help A Reporter Out") — browse journalist/blogger questions in your niche, submit a short expert answer with your link; if picked, you get a citation + backlink (sometimes from a major publication). Batch it: put **20 minutes/day** on the calendar; the dashboard shows publish status and the live link once picked.
- **AB vs. ABC backlink exchanges**: direct reciprocal exchanges (I link you, you link me) no longer work well — Google can detect the direct reciprocity and discounts it as favor-trading. **ABC exchanges** (A→B, B→C, C→A — no direct reciprocal link between any two sites) are what backlink agencies run instead, specifically to avoid the detectable AB pattern.
- **CID link** (see PR workflow) — Google Maps listing link → whatismycid.com → CID link; use for any link pointing at your Google Business Profile in place of the generic Maps/place URL.
- Manually vetting/disavowing spammy backlinks is extremely time-intensive ("people literally spending an entire day going through backlinks") — better ROI generally sits in generating new legitimate links (featured.com) than auditing old spammy ones for a solo operator.
- Diagnosing a traffic drop via backlinks/content pause: in Semrush Domain Overview, pull the organic-traffic graph and organic-keywords graph together, line up the date they bend against the site owner's own publishing calendar — check whether they simply stopped writing before blaming the algorithm.

## 13. Blog Template Design (Elementor / WordPress)

- **Platform-agnostic principle**: use whatever CMS you're already comfortable with (WordPress, Squarespace, Wix) — design principles transfer, only the execution UI changes. Consistency on your own platform beats chasing what others use.
- **Blog roll (index) layout**: grid/feed with optional sidebar categories and/or featured-posts-at-top + roll-below + category filter. Reference pattern: 2 featured posts at top, category filter, roll below.
- **Single-post anatomy**: hero/header (title + featured image) then body. Include title, author, date, featured image; show categories for navigation; disable comments sitewide.
- **Body layout options**: sidebar-left + content-right (most common in the examples), single long column, or content-left + sidebar-right. Break body copy into short paragraphs + bullets + H2 breaks — never one dense wall of text.

### Building a custom single-post template in Elementor (step by step)
1. Theme Builder → Single Post → Add New (blank canvas or pre-built template).
2. Add a Container for the hero/header; give it a background color if needed so overlaid nav text stays visible.
3. For a 2-column hero (image + title side by side): add a container, duplicate it, set the parent container's "direction" so columns sit side by side instead of stacking.
4. Drag in the **Title** widget (dynamic, auto-pulls the post title) and **Featured Image** widget.
5. Add author/date/read-time via the **Post Info** widget, or a Text Editor widget + **Dynamic Tags** (same technique works on a Heading widget too).
6. Style the H1 size, fix line-height (start line-height numerically equal to font-size, e.g. 72→72, then adjust until letters stop touching), add top/bottom padding in **em units** (unlink the padding control so top/bottom can differ from left/right — ems scale better for mobile, "like inches vs. centimeters").
7. Resize the featured-image column vs. title column to match a reference site's proportions.
8. Below the hero, add a second container for the body: duplicate → set direction → narrow column = sidebar, wide column = content.
9. Drag the **Post Content** widget into the content column — auto-pulls whatever was written in the WP back-end editor; no manual linking needed.
10. Drag **Table of Contents** widget into the sidebar; anchor only **H2 and H3** (skip H4–H6 — "we generally don't go that deep"); switch numbered list to bullets, or remove bullets for a cleaner look.
11. Sidebar can also hold static content (a Text Editor widget with a bolded "Call me today" + phone number, business hours) — not limited to the TOC.
12. Apply a subtle background color to the whole content+sidebar container to separate it from the hero, then a distinct (e.g., white) background behind the content area specifically; use **margin** (not padding), unlinked to top-only, to push the body section away from the hero.
13. Publish → Display Conditions → Include → "Posts" (all posts) to roll out sitewide. **If another single-post template is already active, Elementor throws a conflict error** — go to the OLD template, remove/X-out its Display Condition, save; only then does the new template take over. New templates apply **retroactively to every existing post**, not just future ones (binary, not a preview-and-approve-per-post flow).
14. To revert: re-enable the old template's condition, disable/delete the new one.
- **Two ways to edit an individual post**: (a) pure WP back-end editor (uses whichever template is assigned, no per-post layout control); (b) open in Elementor for layout freedom on that post's content container only — a separate "Edit single blog post" button jumps into editing the shared TEMPLATE itself (affects every post; easy to blur these two).
- **Authoring workflow**: write in Word → copy/paste into the WP back-end post editor (images uploaded there too) → the template's Post Content widget displays it automatically.
- **Building the archive/blog-roll template**: Elementor → "Edit Post Archives" → drag in **Archive Post** widget (auto-pulls the preview grid). Adjust column count (5 = "very busy"; settled on 4 or 2). Add author name per card via Dynamic Tags → metadata → Author. Reduce default oversized title font, reset line-height ≈ font-size as starting point. Add a background color per card so previews look grouped/separated. Adjust inter-post spacing. Fix blurry thumbnails via the **Image Resolution** setting (Medium was the demo's sweet spot vs. Large's crisp-but-heavy 1000×1000px).
- **Excerpts**: toggle on/off, set a fixed word-count limit (demo used 50 words) in the archive widget; for awkward auto-excerpts, write a bespoke one per post via the "Edit excerpt" field in the WP post editor.
- **Pagination**: turn on once the blog has many posts (~200-post threshold cited) so the roll doesn't load everything at once.

### Sizing/styling rules of thumb
- Featured image aspect ratio: **16:9** rule of thumb (or square — either is fine, just be consistent sitewide).
- Body font size: **~16px baseline**; don't exceed necessary size (oversized reads "childish"); don't go below ~10px (eye strain). Real examples: one site 18px, another 20px.
- Heading scale example: H1 48px / H2 36px / H3 26px (one site); a different reference site used H1 72px / "key summary" H2 48px — copy a specific reference's numbers only when literally cloning that site, not as a universal rule.
- Body fonts: keep simple sans-serif (works for "99% of all websites") — named picks Inter, Open Sans. Headline fonts can be more decorative since text is bigger/shorter (lower readability cost). Serif fonts often need to run bigger than sans-serif (more visual "action" in the letterforms needs more surrounding white space).
- Padding = space inside a container; margin = space pushing the container away from what's outside it. Use ems for both.
- Images per post: **minimum 4**, ideally one per H2 section.
- Stick to a small, consistent set of provable stock sources (Pexels + Adobe Stock) so licensing can be proven if challenged.

### Tools used
- **Elementor** Theme Builder (Container w/ direction control, Title, Featured Image, Post Info, Text Editor + Dynamic Tags, Post Content, Table of Contents, Author Box, Archive Post, Display Conditions).
- **Font Ninja** (browser extension) — identify fonts on any live site.
- **Pexels** (free) / **Adobe Stock** (paid) — the only two stock sources used.
- **Word** — drafting surface before pasting into WP.

## 16. Cross-Cutting Tradeoffs & Nuance

- **Human voice vs. optimization** is a spectrum, not a binary, on nearly every decision in this material (titles, hyphenation, grammar, keyword density) — deliberately choose where a given business sits rather than treating either extreme as "correct."
- **Thin content is an accepted tradeoff specifically for zero-competition questions** — minimal editing is enough to have a shot at ranking when a keyword has no competition; this is not a general endorsement of thin content elsewhere.
- **Broadening topic scope beyond your exact offer** (Aaron's Yellowstone example, Grace's gift-market pivot) trades ICP precision for much greater available search demand and topical authority — treated as worth it because the broader audience is still plausibly your customer.
- **Domain/keyword-difficulty numbers are directionally real but tool-dependent** — never treat any single tool's number as gospel; Google Search Console is the more accurate ground truth for actual organic traffic, vs. Semrush/Ubersuggest/Ahrefs estimates for research/planning.
- **Backlink acquisition trades effort for reliability**: passive/reciprocal (AB exchange) is easy but increasingly detectable/ineffective; engineered reciprocity (ABC exchange via an agency) works better but needs third-party coordination; earned PR (featured.com) is the "credible" route but slower/unpredictable vs. a guaranteed paid placement (press release).
- **Perfect execution (full SEO team dividing images/content/links, curated real photography, zero backlog) is the acknowledged ideal but explicitly not the bar a solo operator should hold themselves to** — choose priorities deliberately per business goals/values rather than attempting every best practice simultaneously.
- **AI drafting speed vs. authenticity risk**: ChatGPT/Claude dramatically accelerate ideation, drafting, and internal linking, but raw output has detectable "tells" (random bolding, ungrounded first-person claims, generic conclusions, emojis, dividers) that must be manually stripped, and any invented experience/case-study claim must be verified against reality before publishing.
- **List-format content still matches user/competitor/SERP expectations** in many niches (top-ranking pages are often literally giant lists) — the guidance is to blend narrative/expertise context into lists, not to reject list formatting outright.
- **Session-density feedback loop**: even Matt has explicitly acknowledged overloading a session with too much new material and losing attendees, committing to simplify pacing (pre-assigned topics, more guided steps) — a reminder that the frameworks themselves are iterated on based on what actually lands with the community.
