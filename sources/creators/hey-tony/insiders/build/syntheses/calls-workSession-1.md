## Quick-Reference Rules & Thresholds

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Cadence (baseline) | **1 blog post/week minimum**; steady-state target **1–2/week**. Brand-new site may launch with a batch of 5–6 at once (exception, not a live-site practice). |
| Internal links per new post | **3 links to older posts + 1 link to a money page = 4 total** (most-repeated version; one session specified 2+1=3, another gave a 3–4 range — treat 3+1 as the standard). |
| Reciprocal linking | After publishing, add a link **from 3 existing older posts pointing to the new post** — required in addition to the outbound links inside the new post. |
| Images | **One every 200–300 words**; ~750px wide × 400–500px tall; real/own photos > relevant AI-generated > stock. |
| Word count | No fixed rule. Typical output from the standard workflow: **~1,000–1,500 words**; sweet spot cited as 750–1,500; "as few words as you can possibly get away with" but no ceiling if the topic needs depth. |
| Categories | **5–10 total** for the whole site; each post in **1–2 max** (usually just one). |
| Tags | **Never use.** Delete existing ones. |
| Headings | **One H1 only** (the post title); H2/H3 only in body, strictly sequential (never H1→H3); no horizontal rules, dividers, tables, or callout boxes. |
| Title tag | Target keyword toward the **front**; aim **≤60 characters** for new posts; don't retroactively mass-edit old longer titles — revisit only if a post is producing zero results after 3–4 months. |
| Meta description | Unique per page, benefit-forward (written to earn the click, not just describe); don't force a secondary keyword in. |
| FAQ blocks | Plain Q&A text directly on the page — **never** behind an accordion/toggle/dropdown. |
| Keyword scope | **One keyword per page**, never reused across pages (Yoast "Focus keyword" logic); Google shows only one page per site per keyword in results. |
| Refresh/patience windows | Leave a new page alone ~6 months, then re-optimize only if not ranking; don't re-touch old posts until ~30–50 posts published; give any geo/keyword experiment 1–2 months before judging; let a core-site-element change (H1, target keyword) simmer 1–2+ weeks before changing again. |
| Interview-prompt question count | Tool default is 5 or 10 depending on version; most members run **5–10**, commonly trimmed to 7–8 or 5. |
| GBP service description | **300 characters**; work the city/keyword in **once** per listing, never repeated per entry; never put a street address in the description text. |

## 1. Session Format, Culture & How the Program Iterates on Itself

- Standard flow: members raise a hand to share a win → host runs a short live teach/diagnostic → **quiet writing block** (camera/mic off, ~30–45 min, sometimes up to ~30 min) where everyone just does the exercise → return for open Q&A/live troubleshooting.
- New-member sessions are sometimes blended with veteran sessions (shared Monday call), then split later (e.g., Friday runs two separate calls on two calendars) so new signups don't flood the existing group's list before merging.
- Matt's own meta-process for improving the program: after each session/Rank Week day, feed **all call transcripts plus all chat comments** into Claude and ask *"what were the pain points, were people struggling today, how did they feel"* — use the read to decide what to change next time (e.g., a member's request for a 4–5 minute mid-session break was estimated to cut newcomer drop-off by roughly 10%).
- Reciprocity is the explicit community norm: "if you need help, try to help somebody else... the more you help, the more other people will help you." Posting your URL for feedback is normal and reciprocated (one member's detailed review of another's site led to real changes).
- Recurring philosophy lines: "we don't care about SEO, we care about getting clients — SEO is the way we're doing that"; "the objective is not to game the algorithm, it's to write a piece of content so unique it talks to somebody with that specific problem"; overwhelm — not the tactics — is "the number one thing that ruins your SEO," because people quit feeling behind, not because the method stops working; "don't overthink anything... don't let that take you off track and go down a rabbit hole."
- Meta-resource: the Insiders member portal has a **transcript search across every past call recording** — type a keyword and it surfaces the exact clip/timestamp where it was said, with a jump-to-moment link.
- Support/bug-report channel: email screenshots/issues to **matt@heytony.ca** or **support@heytony.ca**, include your website URL. Mac screenshot shortcut for bug reports: **Cmd+Shift+3**.

## 2. The Core Content Workflow (the recurring weekly drill)

This exact sequence is taught, re-taught, and lightly varied in nearly every session. Canonical form:

### 2.1 Step by step
1. **Source a real customer question.** Options: findquestions.com, AnswerThePublic (increasingly paywalled, ~$20/month cited by one member), Ask.com, Answer/Ask Socrates, Also Asked, the Insiders "Blog Idea Finder" / "Blog Topic Finder" tool (needs its own separate login — same email as Insiders, new password), the Insiders "Reddit Keywords" tool (searches Reddit for your term), "People Also Ask" section — or simply a question you've personally heard from a customer/lead (at an expo, on a client call, in a niche Facebook group).
2. **Paste the question into the interview prompt** in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Grok (all confirmed interchangeable for this step).
3. **Answer the AI's clarifying questions** — one at a time, via **voice mode** (strongly preferred: "I'll digress and might reveal something else I wouldn't think of if I was typing"; tangents are fine — even long-winded rambling "trains" the AI to sound more like you).
4. **Draft the post** — either the same thread, or relay your answers into a second AI (see model-split notes below).
5. **Copy into a plain text document first**, then paste from there into WordPress/your CMS — this strips a hidden AI-generated tag/div that the CMS backend otherwise stores, which can flag content as AI-generated. (Alternative: WordPress's paste-as-code clipboard-icon button auto-strips it; or have the AI write raw HTML and paste into the CMS's **code tab**, not the text tab.)
6. **Add images** (200–300 words apart) and **internal links** (see Section 4).
7. Write the **title tag + meta description** by directly asking the AI, confirming the target keyword appears in both.
8. **Publish** — fine to publish without images yet and add them later; "get something up rather than not get something up." Caveat: if Google crawls the page before images are added, it will pick up later-added images noticeably slower.
9. Ensure the post's **Google Search Console indexing setting is enabled** (not left disabled).

### 2.2 Exact prompt wording

**Interview / clarifying-questions prompt** (canonical form, consistent across nearly every session; question count varies 5–10 by version):
> "I'm writing a blog post answering this question: [paste the question]. Ask me anything you need to know the answer to in order to write a blog post that is truly helpful and unique. It should include my experience, expertise, case studies, examples, opinions, etc. Keep it limited to 10 questions. Ask me them one at a time. I'll be using voice mode to answer them. Give me time to breathe as well before going to the next question."
- Default in the tool is sometimes capped at **5** questions (Rank Week's original version); Matt's own version bumped to **10** "because we want to be a little more specific." Members commonly run **5–8** in practice (trim to keep posts from running long, or front-load context in the first message to shorten the interview naturally).
- **Nick's variant** (produced an ~11-page post that outperformed anything he'd posted on LinkedIn, "99% me, plus a couple tweaks," undetectable as AI-written): *"Ask me anything. I'm writing this blog post about this question. Ask me anything. Up to 10 questions."*

**Writing / final-draft prompt** (canonical form):
> "Write this as a clean WordPress ready blog post using my expertise, experience, opinion, stories, et cetera. Use H2 and H3 headings only. No horizontal rules, section dividers, tables, call out boxes or other formatting elements. Just headings, paragraphs, and bullet points where appropriate."
- Applies "doesn't matter if you're using WordPress or not — it's just verbiage" to get the AI to strip its own default formatting clutter.
- **Simpler relay version** (used when handing ChatGPT's Q&A off to Claude): *"Write me this blog post using this information."* / *"I just did this with ChatGPT — write me the blog post using my answers."*
- **Carol's fuller relay version**: *"Here's my chat with ChatGPT about writing a blog post. There are questions it asked me and my transcript I did in voice mode to answer. Please write me a blog post using this information plus whatever searches you need to do online to get the most accurate information."*

**Modifying the interview prompt to repurpose a transcript/podcast/video** (append to Prompt 1 — never just hand over a raw transcript/video and ask for "a blog post," it won't do well):
> "Here's a transcript from a conversation I had. Use that information when you can and where you can to pull my expertise, experience, all that kind of stuff."
- Variant for video/podcast material specifically: "here's a transcript from a related video, ask me 10 questions [so I could write this blog post]." Don't assume the video's own topic is a searched keyword — the payoff is supplemental training material, not a standalone keyword play ("nobody's searching for that podcast... but it's really great supplemental information").

**Knowledge-base seeding (carry context from ChatGPT into Claude)**:
> "I want to make a .md file that has everything you know about me and everything that another AI would need to know in order to operate the same way that you do with me. Give me that whole breakdown, make it into this file." — paste the resulting markdown file's contents into Claude as standing context so it doesn't need re-briefing every post. Also works as: "Make me a document that will teach any AI everything you know about my business."

**Mining client-call transcripts for content** (Bryn's method, endorsed for anyone running client Zoom calls):
> "What are all the questions and answers that happened in these calls?" — fed a client Zoom transcript into Claude/ChatGPT. Captures real Q&A plus your natural brand voice and how you actually handle situations — called "a gold mine for content."

**Reading Zoom-chat sentiment** (Matt's own method for tuning Rank Week day-to-day): feed the day's full Zoom chat log into Claude alongside the transcript and ask **"how are people feeling today?"** — use the read (e.g., "people are frustrated, today was hard") to adjust next day's difficulty or re-explain a concept. Zoom auto-emails the chat log to whoever recorded the meeting; there's also a manual "save" option in the chat panel.

**Topic-picking prompt for a maturing blog** (Marita's, to a member 23–24 posts in — compile an overview of every post title so far, then ask):
> "here, my blog post I write so far and please made me three suggestion as what makes sense and what is useful for me and the community as to write next."

**Imprecise-but-effective refinement instruction** (worked despite wrong terminology — Claude inferred correctly anyway): *"Can the first sentence be an answer to the question in the title tag?"* (meant the H1 heading, not the title tag).

**Reverse-engineering an AI citation** (when Google AI Mode/Gemini names your business, interrogate it): ask *"what did you analyze to recommend these companies"* and *"what search terms did you search for."* To build a prospect list, go further: *"who specifically are the names, phone numbers, mailing addresses of the property managers"* — then use that output for direct outreach (personal letters + brochures).

### 2.3 Model selection & settings
- **The dominant split**: use **ChatGPT for the voice/interview stage** (Whisper transcription is genuinely stronger — "the best of the best" for voice-to-text), then **paste the full Q&A into Claude for the actual writing/drafting pass** ("Claude is just better for like 99% of things... all of my agency stuff, all the output is with Claude"; "Claude is brilliant" / "insane" — multiple members). Reasons cited for Claude over ChatGPT specifically: follows word-count/format instructions far more precisely; makes conservative, minimal edits on a rewrite vs. ChatGPT's tendency to over-rewrite; markedly better at spreadsheet/data organization (one member burned 5+ hours in ChatGPT on an Excel task Claude did almost instantly); doesn't need em-dash correction reinforced every session, whereas Claude does (ChatGPT "remembered" the correction permanently once told; Claude forgets between sessions unless reinforced or set as a standing custom instruction).
- **Gemini** (free tier) was found by one member far more accurate than ChatGPT for meta-description length compliance and GBP-aligned copy; also usable for the topic-picker use case above.
- **Reasoning-effort / model setting**: ChatGPT's Instant/Medium/High selector — use **"Instant"** for the fast back-and-forth Q&A stage, switch to **"High"** for the actual writing stage (Instant is faster but shallower/"surface medium," High is slower but deeper). One member's ChatGPT degraded to one-line answers after ~10 posts in a single thread on "Instant" — the fix was switching effort level (or moving to Claude) rather than fighting it.
- **Cross-AI relay** is now common practice: several members independently converged on ChatGPT→Claude without being told to — described as "what several members are now doing."
- **A/B testing AI writers against each other**: write the same target post with two different AIs, publish one on your main site and the other on an idle secondary site, compare which actually ranks.
- Running both ChatGPT and Claude subscriptions simultaneously is fine but watch subscription creep — Matt estimates spending "a couple hundred bucks a month" across his own AI stack; Claude's annual plan is cheaper than paying month to month.
- **No way to fully automate this.** Even the most advanced pipeline still needs a human to answer the interview questions and review the draft — skipping that step is what produces generic "oatmeal"/"AI slop" content. "There's no way to automate writing blog posts... that's creating a much better output."
- Optional efficiency step: save a standing Claude **"Project"** with a fixed prompt template (e.g., always requesting H1, H2 tags, and meta description as outputs) so you don't retype instructions each session — same idea works in ChatGPT or Grok.

## 3. Internal Linking System

- **Standard structure per new post**: 3 links to other relevant existing blog posts/pages + 1 link to a money page (product/service page) = 4 total, using **descriptive anchor text**, never "click here." (One session specified 2 old + 1 money page = 3 total; another gave a 3–4/page range — treat 3+1 as the dominant rule, adjustable downward if you genuinely don't have 3 relevant posts yet, e.g. a site's very first post.)
- **Reciprocal linking — the "circle" tactic (core, walked through live repeatedly):**
  1. Publish the new post; grab its live URL.
  2. Go into **3 existing, already-indexed** older posts that are topically related.
  3. Find or add a natural sentence/phrase in each old post that can serve as an anchor.
  4. Hyperlink that anchor to the brand-new post.
  5. Update/republish each old post.
  - This runs in **addition to** (not instead of) the outbound links placed inside the new post while writing it — links must flow both directions (old→new and new→old).
  - Rationale, stated as a circle analogy: "if you join a circle of people and everybody in the circle is introducing you to the next person, everybody's going to know about you... if you're standing outside the circle just saying 'I know those people over there,' you're not going to get invited into the group." Because Google already trusts/crawls the old pages, their pointing to the new page gets it discovered and indexed fast — sometimes within an hour or two, without ever manually submitting it to Google.
  - Can be delegated: "you can definitely ask ChatGPT" to find the 3 relevant existing pages to link from.
- **Always use the CMS's "existing page" link-picker**, never paste a raw URL, when linking to your own pages — an existing-page link auto-updates everywhere if that page's URL ever changes; a hardcoded URL breaks silently and needs manual redirects.
- New sites with no back-catalog yet: just publish without internal links; once you have ~10 posts, start linking where it makes sense.
- **Avoid pure reciprocal link exchanges** when facilitating community backlink swaps or directory listings — "don't link back and forth," since an obvious mutual link pattern ("almost like an incest hub... everybody's linking to each other") dilutes the signal versus one-directional links.
- Press/media mentions: fine to write "featured in [publication]" and show their logo — do **not** hyperlink to them.

## 4. On-Page & Formatting Rules

- **Headings**: exactly one H1 (the post title); H2/H3 only in the body, strictly sequential — never jump H1 straight to H3 ("you can, but you shouldn't"; if you want H3's visual size, restyle H2's font instead of skipping a level).
- **No dividers/tables/callout boxes** — the writing prompt explicitly strips these; "just headings, paragraphs, and bullet points where appropriate." (One separate session noted bullet points, tables, videos, and images are all fine to use — the only real formatting sin is keyword stuffing.)
- **Images**: one every 200–300 words; ~750px × 400–500px; priority order = your own real photos/screenshots (fully unique, can't be replicated by competitors) > AI-generated (only if genuinely relevant to that content, never random filler) > stock; match brand colors. Don't stuff a post with photos "because people like pretty pictures" — every photo should illustrate a specific point; put excess/pretty photography on a separate gallery page instead.
- **Title tags**: target keyword toward the front; ≤60 characters going forward (Squarespace's own checker allows up to 81 chars — don't let that override the tighter standard); don't pad with extra flavor text on top of the core phrase (live fix: *"What to wear for a newborn photo shoot in pregnancies by smart photographer who's seen it all"* → cut to just *"What to wear..."*); "The Ultimate Guide to X" framing is fine to keep using if it's still converting for your audience — don't force it if it doesn't read naturally; this is "the psychology of it versus the SEO of it."
- **Meta descriptions**: unique per page (duplicates get flagged by audit tools); Google says they're not a direct ranking factor, but they still drive or kill click-through ("that's like the billboard saying click me instead of your competitors"); write benefit-forward copy, don't force a secondary keyword in just because a tool keeps suggesting it — "it'll come up naturally." Google can and does rewrite your title tag/meta description regardless of what you set — the SERP snippet not filling both preview lines is not a problem (device-dependent rendering).
- **Meta keywords field** (flagged "missing" by some WP SEO plugins like Detailed SEO): ignore completely, dead since the 90s/2000s, does nothing for Google now.
- **FAQ blocks**: plain question-then-answer text directly on the page — never behind an accordion/toggle/dropdown, which adds parsing friction for Google/AI.
- **Categories/tags**: 5–10 categories total for the whole site, each post in 1–2 max (usually one); **never use tags** — "never, ever, ever" — if tags already exist, delete them (they create piles of thin, low-value pages).
- **Readability**: don't chase an "all green" Yoast-style score — rule of thumb, "if an eighth grader could understand what you wrote, you're good"; the underlying algorithm "is not 100% accurate." Ignore transition-word-count complaints and "key phrase in introduction"/"key phrase density" checks entirely at this stage.
- **Em dashes**: cut them out of every AI draft — treated as a visible "I used AI" tell.
- **CTA rotation**: don't end every post with the identical CTA — rotate through multiple CTA types/destinations on roughly a 3–4 post cycle.
- **Video embedding**: embed your best videos directly on the relevant page rather than sending visitors to YouTube; note that "hundreds/thousands more" exist and link out to the channel for the long tail; use embed settings to strip "watch next"/recommended clutter and control autoplay; always put a contact-us link in every video's description. Video repurposing only pays off SEO-wise when tied to a real keyword/question target — turning an untargeted product video into a blog post "won't do much"; correct order is keyword/question first, blog post second, existing video attached underneath as supplemental content (don't start from the video).
- **Vimeo vs. YouTube**: Vimeo embeds still get indexed and technically count for SEO — a UX/aesthetics-vs-reach tradeoff, not an SEO penalty. Infrequent YouTube posting doesn't hurt SEO either — "you're not trying to become a YouTuber."

## 5. Keyword Strategy & Cannibalization

- **One keyword per page, always** ("Focus keyword" in Yoast terms) — never reused across pages. Google's own mechanism: "Google tries to find one page per website that makes sense for that keyword" and will only ever show one of your pages per query — so unmanaged overlap directly costs ranking, even though multiple own-pages "competing with yourself" can feel harmless.
- **What counts as cannibalization vs. not:**
  - Same exact keyword (or its natural secondary/variant phrasing) split across two pages = cannibalization. Fix: consolidate to one page, or 301-redirect the lower-traffic page into the higher-traffic one.
  - Two genuinely **different** keyword targets (e.g., "small business bookkeeping services" vs. "outsourced accounting services," volumes 40 vs. 440 in one real example) must go on **separate** pages — never combine two distinct targets onto one.
  - A collection/category page and a blog post covering similar subject matter is **not** cannibalization if the blog targets a distinctly different (typically longer-tail) keyword — e.g., collection "natural granola" vs. post "benefits of natural granola."
  - Your own brand's product-comparison pages (e.g., Botox vs. Dysport vs. Xeomin) are a **distinct, valid content type** (consideration-stage), not cannibalization of the individual product pages.
  - A blog post's target keyword should never be your **homepage's** primary keyword — use secondary/variant keywords in blog content instead to avoid self-cannibalizing your own homepage.
- **Detection tools**: the Insiders' **Keyword Conflict Detector** (scans a site's own pages with AI, no external ranking-data API — default scan capped at 50 pages, bump toward 500 for full-site coverage) and the standalone **keywordconflict.com** (built by Matt, may scan more than 50) for a second opinion. Not 100% accurate — one live demo only scanned 50 pages on a site with more, cause unclear; one flagged "conflict" turned out to be a phantom (a date-based blog-archive URL slug redirecting to the homepage, not real content — the fix was removing the date link entirely, not touching real pages).
- **Diagnosing a false-positive conflict**: cross-check the tool's flagged URLs against `sitemap.xml` and `robots.txt` — if they're absent from both, they're likely redirect artifacts, not real indexed pages.
- **Ranking without using the exact-match phrase on-page**: send the topical signal via (a) internal links from other posts using the phrase/variants as anchor text pointing to the target page, and (b) backlinks using phrase variants as anchor text. A competitor outranking you without using your exact target phrase anywhere on their page more likely rests on their GBP presence, citations, accumulated authority, or tenure than on-page keyword usage — meaning the phrase can still be a real opportunity for you, but expect to need real time regardless.
- **Keyword variant selection** (when a term has multiple spellings/forms — "stand-up" vs. "standup" vs. "stand up"; "balcony bra" vs. "demi bra"; "biomass boiler" vs. "wood-chip boiler"; accurate niche term "rug"/"Ayavastra" vs. market term "mat"): don't guess or try to educate searchers into new terminology — check which variation the market/competitors actually use and which gets real search traffic, then target that. "Your job is not to train people... just use the terms that people actually use in public or in real life and look at what your competitors are doing because they've built a roadmap for you." If a niche has fragmented naming across many competitors (one real-estate continuing-ed course sold under 7 different brand names by 7 competing schools), write a paragraph on the page listing all the alternate names it's known by, consider a dedicated comparison blog post, and/or build separate pages per name variant gated by keyword research confirming each variant is actually searched — start with whichever is easiest to rank, then expand.
- **Ignore keyword-density scoring tools entirely** for long-tail/question content (Rank Math/Yoast are "geared toward 3–4 word" short-tail phrases and actively work against full-question posts). For blog/Q&A content, the literal question **is** the target keyword — you don't separately insert it; it appears naturally through writing the answer, typically repeating ~3–4 times on its own (title tag, H1, meta description, once in body) without deliberate stuffing. When editing an existing page for density, match however many times competitors use the exact phrase on their ranking pages (could be once, four times, five — if a competitor isn't using it at all, use it once yourself); ignore other tools' "density too high" warnings — "whatever they're doing, ranking #1, Google likes, you're gonna do that too."
- **The 15%-new-searches philosophy** (recurring, foundational justification for the whole question-answering method): roughly **15% of Google's ~2 billion daily searches are brand new, never searched before** and won't appear in any keyword-research tool (SEMrush, Ahrefs, Moz, etc. only ever show keywords they've already detected). "Keyword research is kind of... becoming less important than it was before" as people increasingly type full sentences/questions or talk into their phone rather than typing keywords. This cuts both ways: a niche site that's invisible to competitor keyword-research tools is also harder to forecast/prove ROI on before publishing — trading measurability for genuine white space.
- **Short-tail vs. long-tail defined**: short-tail = 3 words or fewer; long-tail = a full question.
- **Novel/category-defining products with no existing search demand**: don't force the standard question workflow — write an "old thing vs. new thing" comparison post instead.
- **Cross-language SEO**: run the identical keyword-research/blog-writing process natively in the target language from scratch — don't translate existing English content.

## 6. Local & Multi-Location SEO

- **Page vs. post, the core rule**: "we offer this service in this town/city" = a dedicated **page**. "X vs. Y" / comparison / informational / local-color content = a **blog post**. Applies broadly across niches. A brand-new business location gets a page, not a blog post.
- **One city/location per blog post** — never combine multiple cities in a single piece; cycle through cities post by post.
- **Only build dedicated location pages for places you actually service or are physically based** — ranking anywhere else is very difficult. Don't force geo-targeting if you're not location-bound (a purely online, non-local business should chase broader, lower-competition general keywords instead).
- **Blog posts vs. geo-targeting**: do **not** insert your target city into general educational/how-to posts meant to cast a wide topical net — outside-area traffic that lands and stays builds site authority, which indirectly helps local rankings. One member's edit inserting a town name into broad-ranking posts (on Claude's advice, contrary to this guidance) dropped traffic; reverting all affected posts back to broad at once (not gradually) is fine — give any such reversal 1–2 months before judging.
- **Multi-location scaling order**: main domain = flagship/best-performing location; build one dedicated page per additional location (listing the services actually offered there); only add per-service sub-pages per location later, gated on real search volume — don't overbuild upfront. For a business too small/new to rank at the city level, target **state-level keywords first**, then build individual pages for the **top 10 highest-population cities** in that state; a brief mention plus a link at the bottom of an otherwise-generic post (e.g., "if you're looking for website design in West Virginia" → link to a "Website Design WV" money page) is enough — a full hub/pillar structure isn't required.
- **Location pages must be genuinely unique** — reference real local specifics and name real local partners ("best mortgage broker in [town]," a named local realtor) rather than swapping the city name into a template; risk of demotion/penalty for copy-paste-with-city-swapped content at scale.
- **Local-color "tour guide" content** (for businesses like real estate that serve a region): write blog posts — not new dedicated "community" pages — about what it's like to live in a specific town (local festivals, wineries, walkability, specific first-hand anecdotes), grounded in genuine first-hand experience, angled to avoid duplicating existing comprehensive service pages.
- **Large provider/directory sites** (many independent contractors under one brand): don't SEO-optimize each individual provider page — optimize shared informational pages for what people actually search ("medication management," "therapy"); individual providers rank via their own name or GBP.
- **Repurposing local-radio/paid sponsorship mentions**: splice the recording down to just your segment and reuse as free publicity content.
- **Multi-piece weekly system** (one real-estate example): 1 main blog post/week on your own site + 3 short "mini" posts/week published directly to Google Business Profile targeting specific sub-areas — 4 content pieces total per week.
- **Google Business Profile rules**: 300-character service-description limit; work the city/keyword in **once** per listing in natural copy, describing what the service is (optionally the neighborhood) — never repeat it across every service entry, never put a street address in the description; must be set to **public** (a silent "not public due to missing information" error can block visibility for months); it's safe to **rename** a GBP listing (e.g., add "Bookkeeping" to a bookkeeping business's name) without starting a new profile if every local competitor has the service word in their name and you don't — Google may resist/delay the change but it should go through; posting the same content you post on social media directly to GBP is fine, optionally add the city name into the caption; a shared-address second business line should add a suite/unit number if possible — multiple listings at one address in a shared-office scenario are believed tolerated now, though not confirmed with certainty.
- **When no map pack shows and one competitor visually dominates** a location+keyword query, that's a signal you're competing purely organically/on-page now, not via GBP/map pack — different tactics apply (out-rank them on your website).
- **Directories/citations**: local directories (Yellow Pages, etc.) are for businesses with a real local/physical presence or defined service area — skip them for a nationally-shipped ecommerce brand with no storefront; use competitor backlink-profile analysis for citation targets instead. BBB: free basic listing available to any business (not just local); paid tier only buys "accredited" status — Matt kept his listing's presence even after he stopped paying, framing it as a plausible trust/ranking signal without hard proof.

## 7. Backlinks

- **Backlink-gap analysis tools**: **Link Seeker** (aka "Links IO," Matt's tool) — type in what you sell, generates a query to paste into Google, returns high-authority guest-post/backlink opportunities (same underlying method as Matt's older manual spreadsheet, now automated); two separate members each got **41 backlink opportunities** back from it, and one converted the first one tried into a guest article on a Texas automotive site ("3365 Motoring") that delivered **143 clicks in 24 hours**. **Hey Keywords** (heykeywords.com) and **SEMrush/Ahrefs** also do backlink-gap and keyword-gap analysis; SEMrush/Ahrefs disagree with each other and with Hey Keywords on every number — "none of them match up with each other anyways," treat any single tool's figures as directional, not ground truth.
- **Backlinks are page-specific, not domain-wide** — check follow vs. nofollow status too; a competitor's "430 backlinks" headline number can be misleading if most are low-authority or nofollow — your real competitive set may be much smaller.
- **Order of effort for acquiring backlinks**: (1) run a gap analysis to see what competitors have that you don't — many turn out to be free directories or guest-post-friendly sites; (2) pitch guest posts on those sites (often paid, sometimes free; benchmark **~$20–50** per placement, occasionally free); (3) once organic traffic grows, links start arriving naturally and outreach specialists start emailing you — reply and negotiate reciprocal links.
- **Competitor-directory piggybacking**: pull a competitor's backlink profile, get listed on the same directories they're already on — "they've already done the work for you to find all those."
- **"Powered by / website design by" footer credit links**: a legitimate bootstrap backlink tactic for new web designers building client sites, but explicitly framed as early-stage only — must graduate to press releases and local-association links (chamber of commerce, etc.) for the long term.
- **HARO ("Help A Reporter Out")**: still works for expert-source backlinks, but now heavily saturated with SEO practitioners — unclear ROI; described elsewhere as "a lot of back-and-forth and pitching."
- **Directory/membership reciprocity hygiene**: when prompting members of your own directory/network to link back to you, avoid pure reciprocal exchange — link out only to their social profiles from their directory listing, not their own website, to avoid a mutual "link to each other" pattern that dilutes signal; when facilitating community backlink swaps generally, tell participants explicitly "don't link back and forth," one-directional only.
- **Never buy backlinks from Fiverr** or similar cheap sellers; ignore unsolicited backlink-selling emails. **HOTH.com** is a paid backlink service Matt has used "for years" — "good, but not cheap." **Never disavow a backlink** — "it can do more harm than good" — even confirmed spammy/toxic-flagged links rarely need action unless truly egregious (e.g., a porn site linking to you); most accounts don't actually have harmful backlinks despite what checker tools flag.
- **Cold "you're not ranking for anything" outreach emails from other agencies are a positive signal**, not negative — it usually means competitors have noticed you (one member got 4 in a single week).
- **Domain acquisition for backlink/brand consolidation**: if someone owns the exact-match domain you want, DM them directly (Instagram worked for one member) to negotiate buying or trading — get it cheap (a $100 price point was deemed a clear "take that deal"), migrate the actual site to the new domain, and 301-redirect the old one rather than running both. Never post a domain name you're considering buying into a public/group chat — treat it as compromise-able information.
- **Buying up competitors' available domains** so they can't get their own dedicated product/landing pages is something one member did successfully (Guttersafe/Guttersaver Pro vs. "cheap overseas knockoff" competitors) — not framed as a recommended offensive tactic elsewhere in these calls, just observed.

## 8. AI Visibility / Answer-Engine Optimization Tactics

- **Reviews micro-site tactic** (explicitly **not** white-hat — "this is not like a white hat SEO strategy... it is to manipulate the search results, which they don't want you to do"; Matt does it, hasn't seen concrete downside, but won't write about it in his book and advises against connecting/verifying these domains in Google Search Console):
  1. Register a domain like "[business name] reviews" or "[product] reviews" — its own separate root domain, never a subdomain or hyphenated path off the main site (a separate site reads as a more independent third-party source to Google/Bing/ChatGPT).
  2. Fill it with real reviews pulled from **anywhere** they exist — Google, Reddit, email, verbal feedback — not limited to reviews already public elsewhere.
  3. Build cheaply/fast via **Claude Code**: dump review screenshots in, describe the business, say "here's what I like, build this out" — no formal design brief needed. Host via Railway (free tier, then ~$5/month) or Netlify.
  4. **Link FROM your main site TO the reviews site** (direction matters); keep your on-site testimonials/reviews page too — additive, not either/or.
  5. If registering multiple such domains, don't redirect them into each other — each stands alone.
  - Not recommended: buying up competitor-style review domains offensively.
- **Press releases for AI-answer citation**: real, repeated tactic. Providers: **EIN Presswire** (normal $149/release; a June promo bundled 7 releases for ~$500), **AB Newswire** (used successfully but a viral podcast clip about the tactic burned it — some outlets, e.g. the Globe and Mail, stopped accepting releases from this group afterward; Matt declined a repeat podcast episode to protect the still-working tactic), generic paid distribution benchmarked at **~$80–90**. Effects reported: 5 backlinks within ~5 hours of one release; a client's AI Mode ranking rose to ~3rd for a "best contractor" query the week after a release; a contest-nomination win ("[Business] voted #1 [category] in [region]") is worth its own press release plus social push, framed explicitly as AI-citation bait, not just vanity PR. One caveat from direct experience: press-release-driven ranking bumps have sometimes only lasted about a week before fading — "if it works, it works," not guaranteed.
- **Reddit/social signal stacking**: post something conversational in a relevant subreddit about your own brand/event, get your community to upvote/comment — one such post got indexed and pulled into a Google AI Overview in under 24 hours. Stack other controllable signals around the same brand-name query (your own Facebook/Instagram posts, a press release, your BBB listing) — these get pulled together into AI Overview/ChatGPT summaries about your business. Posting fresh social content regularly signals to Google you're a real, active business.
- **You cannot manipulate ChatGPT/Claude recommendations via prompting your own private sessions** — has zero effect on what the model surfaces to other users; AI recommendations come from a live Google/Bing-style search at answer time, not from your chat/session history. Keeping your own AI chats private doesn't cost you any discoverability either.
- **Reverse-engineering an AI citation** (see exact prompts in Section 2.2): once cited, interrogate the model for its reasoning and search terms, then push further to extract concrete prospect/lead data for direct outreach.
- **Google Search Console's "Generative AI" report** shows impressions only (no clicks) for how content surfaces in AI-driven results, and isn't available on every property yet — a partial picture, not proof either way.
- **Google Business Profile appears to have little to no pull in AI-recommendation results** in Matt's own repeated testing (searching "hey Tony reviews" surfaces Reddit and random sites, never his own GBP) — treat GBP as a discovery/local-pack asset, not a lever for AI-answer visibility specifically.
- **Schema markup and other commonly-cited "AI SEO" tactics haven't moved the needle much**, per Matt's own observation (not a proven universal claim) — consistently publishing genuinely good content is, in his view, the single biggest lever for AI-answer visibility.

## 9. Site Health, Technical Troubleshooting & Structural Rules

- **Orphan pages are not inherently bad for SEO** — fine to have many (Matt keeps plenty on his own site). Only worth fixing ones that are backlink-worthy or should be ranking: pull a full backlink report (e.g., in SEMrush), cross-check which orphan pages actually have an inbound backlink, and for each ask "is this rank-worthy, do I want it to rank for something?" — if yes, add an internal link to it from anywhere reachable (doesn't need to be the homepage); if no, leave it. Keep lead-magnet/ebook orphan pages regardless of backlink status — they still drive business through email capture. Caveat: the Insiders audit tool only crawls up to ~1,000 pages, so orphan/missing-H1 counts on larger sites can be incomplete and shift between runs (it samples a different subset each crawl).
- **URL/slug change rule — check all three before touching a URL**: is it ranking? does it get traffic? does it have backlinks? Only change if **all three** come back negative. Changing even one character makes it an entirely new URL to Google — it must be re-indexed, existing rankings for the old URL may not carry over, and any backlinks to the old URL become void unless redirected. If you do change a URL, update every internal link that pointed at the old one. Homepage URL should just be the bare domain — already correct, don't try to "optimize" it further. Exact-keyword-match domains have "dropped in the last two Google algorithm updates" as a ranking help — having the target keyword literally in your domain no longer reliably helps.
- **Redirect logic**: replacing a page's content (even under a new URL) → set a **301 redirect** from old to new, keep the new content topically related to what the old URL covered. Deleting content with nothing to replace it → let the old URL **404**, don't redirect it.
- **Deleting stale/non-performing pages**: safe only if Search Console shows **no ranking, no indexing, AND no backlinks** for that page — and even then, be cautious about bulk deletion (Matt deleted ~70 old posts, some dating to 2022, this way and saw only minor traffic loss — explicitly not offered as a template to copy blindly).
- **Building a new keyword-specific page vs. editing an existing broad one**: don't rename/replace a working broad category/collection page — build a separate new page/collection targeting the specific keyword variant instead (e.g., keep "Necklaces," add a new "Murano Necklaces" collection page and link to it), so the original still covers the broad category.
- **Duplicate H1 fix** (often caused by a popup element carrying its own heading tag): open the popup element, change its heading to plain paragraph text, restyle visually so nothing changes for the visitor — only the underlying markup changes. Every page needs exactly one H1.
- **Full WordPress/Avada reset** (for a blank/broken live site after a template switch left orphaned pages): confirm the live URL has no stray `/wp` in the path and the site is actually published → clear cache → in wp-admin → Pages, find the page set as homepage → click "View" (not Edit) to see what's actually live → if blank, open the page editor and check the first element (e.g., hero) for genuine content → right-click the live page → View Page Source to check for real HTML → if the source is truly empty, don't patch it — do a full reinstall: Softaculous → Uninstall (removes WordPress entirely, not just the theme) → confirm → Remove Installation → reinstall fresh (again check no `/wp` in path) → set a new admin password, change the admin username away from the default "admin" → reinstall the theme from scratch. If the installer throws "files already exist in target folder" (index.php, .htaccess), delete those leftovers first or select another folder. Stop screen-sharing before typing/saving a new admin password.
- **Debugging a mystery CSS issue** (e.g., an unexplained border in Avada/WordPress): inspect element on the container, then each column; check computed margin/border-box/padding/border-position layer by layer. If it can't be isolated live, escalate to the community's technical lead (tagged, needs site access) rather than continuing to guess — an AI tool insisting a fix is "100%" correct isn't proof it can actually locate the source.
- **Platform-native AI assistants can give confidently wrong SEO advice** (e.g., a Shopify built-in AI warning that repeated CSS/formatting could get flagged by Google as repetitive — conflates styling with content, likely wrong) — sanity-check against a human technical lead rather than trusting it directly. Similarly, don't fully trust ChatGPT's own SEO opinions ("combine these two keywords on one page," "is this a good idea") — it tends to agree with whatever you propose; "you are the expert, you're doing the research."
- **Google Search Console basics**: enable the indexing setting on every post; use Search Results → Pages tab on a money page to see its actual queries and average position — ignore branded terms (your own business name, which will already rank fine) and prioritize non-branded/local terms ranking poorly as the next target; a page ranking #1 with zero clicks in week one is normal, not a red flag — CTR optimization (title/meta rewrites) is a later step, not a week-one task; new-post rank-then-dip-then-stabilize in weeks 1–3 is normal ranking volatility, not a problem — "keep writing, keep doing the internal linking and have faith, have patience."
- **Editing during a live Google core update**: touch **one page at a time**, never batch multiple pages — check impact before moving to the next. A page that's already dropped to the bottom of page 1 or off it entirely has little ranking left to lose, so it's safe to fully rewrite; a page still holding a reasonable rank (e.g., dropped #1→#4 but no further) should get conservative edits only. Confirm any density/content gap predates the update before acting on it mid-update.
- **Recovering rankings after an algorithm-update drop**: run your existing page head-to-head against the current top-3 competitor pages for that keyword through an LLM, ask it to analyze for SEO best practices and explain why you're ranking below the top 3 with recommended changes, rewrite using that feedback, let Google recrawl — "generally within a few hours, you'll be often in the top three."
- **Site-analyzer/scoring tools**: the Insiders site-scorer took one rebuilt site from ~40 to ~70; another member's own site scored 96% (94% on the homepage alone) vs. 64% for a named competitor after ~3 months of following the program.
- **Building custom on-domain pages/tools with Claude Code**: describe your CMS/page-builder/theme to Claude Code and ask it to build the page in that same builder/theme so the resulting code can be pasted directly into your native editor — keeps the page hosted on your own domain rather than an external app/builder. Landing pages should stay on your own domain whenever ranking matters; an externally-hosted page (e.g., via Netlify as a redirect target) can still generate leads/conversions but likely won't rank — if used, still link back to your own homepage from it (counts as external traffic).
- **Menu/navigation UX test**: hand your site to someone non-technical ("a mom or a grandparent") and watch, unassisted, whether they can find a specific page; simplify based on where they get stuck — "the more things in your navigation menu, the less impact it has." Pick nav category labels that match how the business actually breaks down (not a generic "Services") and fold low-traffic standalone pages (e.g., a "media" page) into the relevant service pages instead.
- **Broken-site quick diagnosis**: try the `www.` version of the domain; screenshot the issue (Cmd+Shift+3 on Mac) and upload it to ChatGPT asking directly what's wrong.
- **Photo/asset management at scale**: for very large photo libraries (500,000+ images), a cataloging tool like Picoto/Peakato (search "purple shirts," indexes thumbnails + video clips from external drives) becomes too slow to be useful — better suited to smaller libraries; fixing folder/viewer settings that only allow browsing one folder at a time can free up real time in a weekly content routine.
- **Site security/hack recovery**: install Wordfence (Scan) proactively; if compromised, change all passwords immediately, and if hosting support restores a stale backup, push back and ask again for the most recent version. Backup plugin/service options referenced: BlogVault, UpdraftPlus, or host-run daily backups (confirm with your host).

## 10. Publishing Cadence, Editing Discipline & Risk Management

- **Cadence**: minimum 1 post/week; steady-state recommendation **1–2/week** — described as sustainable "forever, ideally" until revenue/traffic justifies hiring in-house or an agency help. A brand-new site launch may batch **5–6 posts at once** (exception). An already-live site should never publish a dozen at once — Google effectively gives "demerits"/treats it as spam-like instability at high velocity (a web designer's rule of thumb: no more than 3, ideally 1–2/week). Higher sustained cadence (one member ran 5/week while building a ~50-post content "hub") is acceptable only if quality genuinely holds and the goal (finishing a defined content structure) justifies it — explicitly framed as the exception, not the default; forcing a schedule risks publishing weaker posts just to hit a number and running out of good topics. Topics realistically never run out — a single seed term in a question-finder tool returns 40+ questions, and a different term yields 40 more; one agency writes 8 posts/month per client without running dry.
- **Don't make rapid repeated changes** to core site elements (H1, target keyword) — one change is fine, then let it simmer at least a couple of weeks before touching it again, so Google's spam-detection doesn't flag instability.
- **Fixing a discovered internal-linking or other systemic mistake**: don't retroactively fix everything at once, even with spare time — fix "one or two a week or a day" and otherwise keep moving forward with new content. Overwhelm from trying to catch up completely is explicitly named as the top cause of people quitting the whole practice.
- **Never publish a "coming soon" placeholder page or link** — zero value to a first-time visitor, signals "this site is under construction."
- **Pre-launch/seasonal content**: publish supporting blog content *before* the thing you're promoting is ready (a new product line, a seasonal offer), so it's already indexed and ranking by the time demand hits — e.g., a florist should start optimizing for major gifting holidays roughly 6 months out, not a month before.
- **Writing itself should be fast** — the actual drafting step (once you're in the workflow) takes roughly 30–45 minutes; what actually eats time is finding/sourcing images, formatting, and internal linking. If writing alone is taking 3 hours, that signals something's off in the workflow, not that the task is inherently slow (sourcing images is a legitimate exception that can validly take longer, especially for photographers).
- **Don't overthink or chase "perfect" before publishing** — a post is never going to be perfect, and being more polished doesn't guarantee ranking anyway; hit publish, revise later if needed.
- **"Best X" / comparison-listicle content**: if answering a searched question means listing competitors (e.g., "best LinkedIn courses"), don't publish that piece on your own site unless you can place your own brand somewhere on the list — a "best of" list that omits you actively signals to Google/AI you're not a contender. If you don't want to name yourself or competitors, get the piece placed on a third-party site instead.
- **Editing an already-ranking page carries real risk of hurting its ranking** — only worth attempting when the page is currently converting at zero anyway.
- **Content-type mixing for topical authority**: alternate specific "common issues with [make/model/variant]" posts with general service-explainer posts rather than only doing one type — builds broader topical coverage.

## 11. Agency & Business-Model Notes

- **Pricing model**: flat monthly retainer, never hourly. Benchmark package (given as the answer to a "what should we charge" question): **$3,000 USD/month or more**; **8 pieces of content/month** (8 blog posts, OR 8 page updates, OR 8 new pages such as location pages); **4 backlinks/month**; ongoing technical fixes; monthly reporting; a dedicated point-of-contact per client.
- **Two proven client cadences, contrasted**:
  - **With backlink work**: 2 blog posts/week + 4 backlinks/month, nothing else — the model behind a 3-year, undisclosed-niche client now doing **$1.3M/year**, ranking for **~9,600 keywords**, **51,000** reported visitors (Matt estimates the real number closer to **15,000**), **$56,000/month** in estimated traffic value, having grown from ~$20K/month to $100K+/month with seasonal spikes to ~$200K within 2–3 months of the year.
  - **Without any backlink work**: 1 blog post/week + 2 city/location pages/month — used on a 3-month client engagement that ended with the client "at capacity" and hiring more staff; delivered via a pre-mapped content plan (12 blog topics + 6 city pages, client sign-off up front) and an efficient ~4-general-questions-per-topic interview shortcut (versus ~10 hyper-specific questions per topic).
- **Client onboarding/mapping process**: map the full content plan in advance — topics, which pages will internally link to which, which topics tie to which money pages — get client sign-off on the map before starting the interview-and-write cycle per topic.
- **Weekly client "win" discipline**: check whether ranking for more keywords, getting more traffic, whether one piece of content "popped off," whether ranking better for money-making terms, or log into the client's analytics and confirm actual sales; non-analytic wins count too (e.g., a client solving a site problem themselves).
- **Revenue is capped by fulfillment capacity, not just lead volume** — more SEO traffic doesn't move the bottom line once a business/team can't absorb the additional work; past that point, conversion-rate work and pricing become the real growth levers, not more SEO volume (illustrated by Matt's own agency: 2.5% lead-to-client conversion rate, inbound volume already exceeding fulfillment, team now in sales training/considering price increases rather than chasing more leads).
- **Benchmark timelines**: typical agency clients take **2–3 months** to show real ranking movement; the Insiders members following this method report meaningful movement in roughly **1–1.5 months**, faster than that norm.
- **Pricing transparency on your own site**: list pricing/cost ranges even as a boutique/premium provider — "if you don't have any prices on your website, you're getting left out entirely" from price-shoppers who will price-shop regardless.

## 14. Tradeoffs & Open Questions (cross-cutting)

- **Broad vs. local content**: broad, non-geo-targeted posts can pull far more total traffic and build domain-wide authority that indirectly helps local rankings later, but pulls in visitors who'll never convert locally — treated as a feature (trust signal), not a bug. Localizing narrows to more qualified visitors but can shrink total traffic and the authority signal. No fixed right answer; test and give it 1–2 months before judging.
- **ChatGPT vs. Claude** is a real, repeatedly-confirmed practical split (voice/transcription vs. writing quality/instruction-following), not just preference — but not universal: Gemini beat both for one member on meta-description compliance, and model choice is explicitly framed as fungible ("use whatever ones you want").
- **Word count, keyword density, and readability scores are all deliberately left to judgment**, not hard rules — the recurring justification is that usefulness/depth and genuine expertise should outweigh hitting a target number or a green checkmark.
- **Press releases and Reddit/social AI-visibility stacking work, but effects can be temporary** (a press-release ranking bump fading after about a week in one direct experience) and the reviews-microsite tactic is openly acknowledged as manipulative and kept deliberately low-profile to preserve its effectiveness ("I don't want to promote that outside of a group like this... at least for the next couple months").
- **Publishing velocity is context-dependent, not absolute**: new-site launch batching, Todd's 5/week hub-building push, and the standard 1–2/week are three different valid regimes for three different situations — the risk in all cases is quality erosion or Google's spam-like penalty for unstable/rapid changes on an otherwise-steady site.
- **Full site redos and rapid core-element changes carry real, sometimes severe ranking risk** (Jason Bush's March redo wiping out all rankings) — the guidance leans conservative: change one thing, wait, observe, rather than batch-editing.
- **Keyword-research-tool blindness cuts both ways**: the same invisibility that lets a niche site "sneak past" competitor research also means you can't use those tools to forecast or prove ROI before publishing — trading measurability for genuine white space, most visible in the therapist and LinkGap.io case studies.
- **Directory/backlink reciprocity, contest/vote campaigns, and "powered by" credit links** are all treated as legitimate but time-limited or context-limited tactics (bootstrap-only, or requiring explicit non-reciprocal structuring) rather than durable, universal strategies.
- **AI/LLM visibility levers remain genuinely uncertain**: GBP appears to have little pull on AI answers per Matt's own repeated testing; schema markup and most "AI SEO" tactics haven't shown a clear effect to him; Google's Generative-AI impressions report is partial (impressions only); and Matt's own working theory that browsing/conversion data drives rankings (from the LinkGap.io result) is explicitly unproven and still being investigated, not asserted as fact.
- **Content hubs / pillar-and-spoke structures** are actively discouraged by Matt as his own opinion/bias ("I don't believe in content hubs... I feel like SEO people are trying to confuse other people"), offered as a simpler alternative to a member's hub plan rather than a settled, evidence-based refutation — a bias to weigh, not a rule.
