# Why Doesn't It Rank — Diagnostics Synthesis (Group 2)
*Source: 15 "Why Doesn't It Rank" / WDIR live-audit calls, Matt Diamante, HeyTony Insiders (Jan–Apr 2026)*

## 1. Core Operating Principles

- **One page = one keyword = one topic/question/problem.** Stated in nearly every call as the single foundational rule. A page can still legitimately rank for many *phrasings* of that one intent (people search the same thing differently) — that's not a violation. What breaks it: two pages chasing the identical or near-identical intent (word-order/pluralization variants count as identical — "Chicago luxury newborn photographer" / "Chicago photographer newborn" / "Chicago newborn photography" is ONE topic, not three).
- **Keyword cannibalization** = Google will only reliably surface one page from your domain per query (serving multiple results from one domain is a bad searcher experience) — so if two pages could rank for the same query, fix it by making each page's keyword a distinct variant (`[keyword] + location`, `[keyword] + best practices`) or by consolidating into one hub + dedicated sub-pages. Cannibalization can also happen **within** one page's own copy: using 3+ near-duplicate phrase variants ("indigenous art fly net hat," "mosquito net hat," "indigenous art protection print fly net hat") instead of committing to one primary phrase confuses the target signal.
- **Money page vs. blog post — the dividing test:** if content ends with a "conclusion," is purely informational, or answers a *question* someone has, it's a blog post — regardless of what page type it was built as. A service/money page assumes the visitor is already past education (they know they have the problem) and just needs to know you offer the solution + how to book. Blog posts are informational-intent by default and **usually aren't meant to convert** — "it's to build trust," not sell — so a high-ranking blog post with weak conversion isn't automatically broken; it may just need a business-relevant CTA added, not a rewrite into a sales page.
- **Match content format to what's actually ranking, not to what you assume the format should be.** Before writing or optimizing any page, Google the exact target phrase and look at the SERP: is it dominated by product/collection pages, directory/listicle sites, service pages, or blog posts? Build the matching format. Examples: a "best video production company in NY" query is dominated by list/directory sites (Clutch) — a single-company "we are the best" page fights the wrong intent; "ski t-shirt" / "tennis shirts" SERPs (including Amazon) are dominated by **collection pages**, so optimizing a *product* page for that term is fighting the wrong format; a literal question like "can I put a fridge in a dumpster" or "fly net hats" may return product/shopping results, meaning the fix is the product/collection page, not the blog post that already exists.
- **Homepage targeting depends on business model:** local/service businesses should target `"[service] + [city]"` on the homepage (e.g., "dentist in Hamilton," "Residential Roofing in Cincinnati," "Same Day Dumpster Rentals in Fort Smith"). Ecommerce/content/national sites should **not** target the homepage — ranking should happen on lower pages (collections, products, blog posts); the homepage just needs to make a visitor understand what the site is about once they land from an entry page. National/virtual services with no single physical base should optimize the homepage for the single largest nearby metro, not a generic "nationwide" term, then spin out location pages for smaller markets.
- **If your homepage naturally outranks your intended money page for the target term, stop fighting it** — treat the homepage AS the money page (add the keyword/topic there) and point future blog links at the homepage instead.
- **"About" pages, contact pages, and blog category/taxonomy pages should not be keyword-targeted** — they behave like the homepage, aren't expected to independently rank, and duplicate H1s or SEO gaps on category pages usually don't matter since those pages were never going to rank anyway.
- **Ranking isn't the only success metric.** A page can convert entirely through another channel (phone calls after a page view, direct outreach after a $100K B2B lead) without ever ranking — "not ranking" doesn't mean the page is failing the business, just that it's a candidate for further SEO work.
- **"Always think of the user. If it's going to be beneficial for somebody, great. If not, leave it."** — the default judgment call repeated across nearly every audit (quizzes, pricing pages, nav items, CTAs).
- **SEO is a marathon, not a sprint / it compounds.** Realistic timelines: **3–6 months** before a "big win" (real traffic increase), can be faster or take up to a year depending on authority/competition/market; commit to **at least 6 months** of consistent work before judging results. Rank-check **monthly, not daily** — "if you look at things daily, you're going to drive yourself crazy," make too many changes, and never let Google settle. Once a page shows movement (even just page 3), **stop touching it** — a member who over-optimized after reaching page 3 dropped out of rankings entirely as a direct result. **Page 3 ≠ position 3** — a critical distinction repeatedly flagged; don't panic-react to page-level movement as if it were exact rank.
- **A/B test rather than guess** when guidance conflicts with what's clearly working for a sloppy competitor: publish one page best-practice, one page keyword-stuffed like the competitor, leave both a month or two, compare. "Marketing is all about A/B testing. SEO is all about A/B testing."
- **Don't let AI fully auto-write-and-publish content unsupervised.** "I do not recommend this because it's a slippery slope if you just let AI do whatever." (A friend's fully-automated AI blog pipeline is reportedly still working and even getting recommended in Perplexity — Matt acknowledges this but still won't recommend the unsupervised path for Hey Tony or client sites; he's only willing to test the idea on his own side project.)
- **Don't chase 100% on any audit/scoring tool** (builder's on-page checker, Yoast, Rank Math, Rank Report Card, SEO plugin green lights). "Sometimes you need that D to help you rank." These tools gamify SEO — passing the checklist is decoupled from being correctly optimized, and chasing them can actively produce the cannibalization problem (a plugin telling you to use a keyword N times regardless of what the actual #1 competitor does) or make you reject a genuinely correct keyword because the plugin doesn't recognize it as "the" focus keyword. A **90% health score is "fantastic"** — stop there, don't keep chasing flags that don't matter (e.g., duplicate H1s on category pages).
- **Domain Authority / DR-style scores are "a made up metric."** A low score (DA 7, DA 8) is not proof of failure — sites with low DA rank well constantly. The only real lever to raise it: more organic traffic + more/better backlinks. Don't panic over a low number; don't treat a high one as proof of quality either.

## 2. The Standard Diagnostic Workflow

This is the same recurring sequence run live on nearly every site audited across all 15 calls — treat it as the default troubleshooting checklist for "why doesn't this rank":

**Step 0 — Is Google even seeing the site?**
1. `site:yourdomain.com` in Google — compare the indexed-page count to your real page/post count. (Courtney: ~30 indexed vs. 30+ actual posts, several mislabeled as testimonials.)
2. Open the sitemap URL referenced inside `robots.txt` and confirm it actually resolves — a dead sitemap reference (e.g., left over after removing a plugin like Yoast) silently blocks discovery.
3. Check `robots.txt` for a `crawl-delay` directive and remove it — even a 10-second/10-millisecond delay can trigger a bot-challenge screen instead of serving real content to an audit tool (diagnosed as the cause of a tool returning "only nine words" on one page).
4. Add an explicit user-agent allow rule in `robots.txt` for any SEO-audit crawler being blocked. Prompt used: *"How can I add a user agent to my robots.txt file?"* (asked to ChatGPT).
5. Re-run the audit tool after fixing.

**Step 1 — Page-level checklist (run on every page reviewed):**
- Title tag: does it name the business/service and include the target keyword at the start? (A homepage title tag literally reading "Homepage Prime Services, Fort Smith" was the first thing flagged on one site.)
- Meta description: exists, and is a genuine sales pitch (not a copy of the title tag).
- H1 count: exactly **one** per page, containing the target keyword, positioned at the very top (not buried under a hero image or a run of H3s).
- Heading order: must descend without skipping a level going down (H1→H2→H3); skipping backward *up* the hierarchy (H5 back to H3/H2/H4) is fine. A repeated site-wide module (e.g., "what's going on in the [region]") rendered as an H1 on every blog post is a hard no — remove it site-wide, since it tells Google every page is the same page.
- Internal links: count them — 1–2 per post is too few; more than that plus links pointing at money/tool pages using descriptive anchor text.
- External links: cite at least one outside authority (government/official body, stat source) for factual/regulatory claims.
- Every link and nav item must be visibly clickable to a human — "if a person can see it all the time, Google can see it all the time."
- Canonical tag: view source on the live page and confirm the canonical points to the page's own URL, not a duplicate/different URL (this was the actual root cause of a page being excluded from an XML sitemap despite showing "indexed" in the SEO plugin's own settings).
- Page speed / render-blocking resources / image weight (see §9).
- Word count and heading structure vs. the top 2–3 ranking competitors (see §3).

**Step 2 — GSC-specific diagnosis:**
- **Indexing → Pages** report → toggle to "Not indexed" → read the *stated* exclusion reason. Ignore reasons like "not found with redirect," "duplicate without user-selected canonical," "blocked," or paginated/feed/author pages you don't care about. Focus specifically on **"Crawled – currently not indexed"** as the reason worth investigating.
- **Insights tab**: shows which queries/pages are trending up vs. down — if something you changed is trending down, either give it time or revert that specific change and watch for recovery.
- **Performance/Queries report**: always compare **28 days vs. previous 28 days**, never 7-day vs. 7-day (too noisy, search-term usage shifts month to month). For a newer site, pull the **last 12–16 months**, not the default last-3-months, to see the real trendline.
- Read **Impressions first** — trending up = early traction even before clicks show up.
- Ignore any query row with a tiny impression count (e.g., "average position 1" on a query with only 8 impressions across 16 months) — statistically meaningless.
- Expect junk/off-topic queries to start appearing as a site ranks for more terms (stray addresses, foreign-language phrases, unrelated brand names) — this is normal noise at scale ("Google just does its weird thing"), and a growing row count (8–10 rows → 67 rows) is itself a positive signal of expanding reach. GSC's own query table caps out around **~1,000 rows** shown.
- New pages don't always need to "sit and wait a month" — properly internally-linked pages have been seen to index and rank within **~2 hours** (Kenneth's experience) to **~2–3 hours** (Matt's team, incl. AI-recommendation within hours) — but other client sites with insufficient authority have taken **up to ~2 months** with no movement. "It depends on every single website."

**Step 3 — Click-depth / orphan-page test:** starting from the homepage, manually click through to the target page and count clicks. Footer-only links count as a path but carry less weight than header/on-content links. 3–4+ clicks deep via a footer-only link is a plausible reason a page isn't indexing or ranking (found on a blog post that was 4 clicks deep and non-indexed). Fix orphaned pages via a proper sub-nav/dropdown so every page is reachable within roughly 3 clicks (not a flat unstructured link dump — group logically by category).

**Step 4 — Reverse-engineer competitors before rewriting anything:**
- **pageaudit.com** (free): paste a competitor's URL, leave the keyword field blank, click Analyze, then check the "Content" tab's word-density view at 3-word phrases to reveal what keyword the page is actually built around. Don't assume their apparent choice is optimal — verify/improve it by running it through a keyword tool for easier variations.
- Check keyword usage **two ways**: exact-match count AND total variation count combined — a page can look under-optimized on exact-match while being heavily optimized on variations (Melissa: exact keyword 10x but keyword+variations 17x; her competitor: exact keyword only 1x but variations 30x).
- Pull a competitor's **XML sitemap** (via `robots.txt` → sitemap index) to see their blog post count and whether they run separate location/landing-page sitemaps.
- Check the **oldest indexed blog post's publish date** as a topical-authority proxy — a competitor with a 2016 post has years of accumulated signal a newer site can't shortcut.
- **Verify a competitor is actually winning before copying them**: paste their exact URL into Google — if nothing comes up, they aren't ranking for anything there. Check the **Google Ads Transparency Library** to rule out paid traffic. Don't let ChatGPT's competitive analysis stand in for this — it can assert a "primary keyword" for a competitor that doesn't even rank for that term.
- **Tool-reported competitor word counts can be flatly wrong** — one page reported at "1300 words" was manually verified at only **174 words**; another set of competitor pages Money Page Doctor reported at 2,200–3,400 words couldn't be visually located on the actual page (suspected hidden/pop-up content). Always sanity-check by eye before using a number to set your own target.

## 3. On-Page Optimization Rules

- **Title/H1/meta formula, repeated across nearly every audit:** (1) target keyword at the very start of the title tag; (2) target keyword at the very start of the H1; (3) meta description rewritten as an independent sales pitch naming specific services/differentiators — never a copy of the title tag; (4) state the full location name explicitly and repeatedly (not an abbreviation like "D.C." if "Washington D.C." is the real search term) — *except* when the searched abbreviation IS the short form (a dumpster-rental site ranked poorly because it spelled out "Saint Lucie" everywhere while searchers type "St. Lucie" — match the exact abbreviation people actually type).
- **Local-business title tag reorder:** put keyword/city at the front, brand name at the end — e.g. "Movers in Washington D.C. — Hire Movers, Free Quotes | Your Moving Men" instead of leading with the company name. Company name in the title tag is otherwise neutral either way ("not going to hurt it, but it also might not help").
- **URL vs. H1 split:** put the long, natural-language phrase in the H1/title (e.g., "Bookkeeping services in St. Petersburg, Florida for small businesses"), but keep the URL short (e.g., `/services/bookkeeping`) — never cram the full long-tail + city string into the slug. Matt's own SEO page ranks #1 in Hamilton without "Hamilton" anywhere in the URL, H1, or H2 — proof a hard local keyword doesn't require URL keyword-stuffing.
- **URL-change decision framework:** before renaming a URL to match a "better" keyword, check whether the current page (a) ranks for anything, (b) gets traffic, (c) has backlinks. None present → safe to change. Any present → don't change the URL (a changed URL starts from zero authority — "new kid at school, nobody knows who they are"); adjust on-page keyword focus instead or leave it alone. Confirmed cost case: a re-optimized money-page URL still wasn't indexed 2–3 weeks later despite internal links, external links, a 301 from the old URL, and sitemap inclusion.
- **Heading hierarchy ("chapters of a book"):** H1 = book title (main topic/keyword). H2s = chapter titles — keep them short, and most (not all) should reinforce the main topic. Flowery/long marketing-speak sentences should be demoted to plain paragraph text, never formatted as a heading. Putting the keyword "early" (per tools like Rank Math) doesn't mean literally duplicating the H1 as an H2 — better to let it appear naturally within the first couple of body sentences; Matt calls this an ongoing, unresolved internal test, not settled guidance.
- **FAQ formatting:** mark as H2 (or plain question-then-answer with no literal "Q:"/"A:" prefix needed); answer directly and briefly, then link out to the full blog post for depth (FAQ → blog → product/service page chain). **3–4 FAQs per page is the norm/max** — 12 on one page is too many, split onto a dedicated page. Each standalone FAQ question can also become its own blog post (free content + internal linking).
- **Answer the question immediately, no hedging:** cut phrasing like "the short answer is no" — just state "No, [X] cannot [Y]." Don't make people scroll to reach the answer — a magic-tricks post that restated its own H1 inside a giant hero image directly below it was flagged for this exact mistake.
- **Word count:** no fixed number — match/slightly exceed the top 2–3 ranking competitors' actual count, not a padded arbitrary target. Concrete anchors seen across calls: a **5-6-700-800-word floor** for a normal blog post; a rough **2,000-word ceiling** per page (one homepage measured ~10,000 words total, ~2,000 of that inside heading tags alone — over-optimized); **~400 words** can be plenty for an extremely low-competition long-tail phrase; **300 words** flagged as the minimum threshold inside one automated Rank Report Card tool. More words is not automatically better — a 2,000-word page outranked a 3,200-word page from the same niche once search intent/formatting matched better.
- **Keyword density:** no fixed repeat count — "use it three or four times and see what happens" as a starting calibration, then check the actual #1 competitor's count via a page-audit tool (seen ranging from competitor pages using the exact phrase only once up to 27 times, or a duplicate-content-risk page using it 41 times). Never chase a plugin's fixed rule (Yoast's internal logic can push you to hit a keyword 5 times when the real #1 competitor uses it only twice) — over-hitting a plugin's checklist can literally BE the over-optimization / keyword-stuffing problem, and can cause the cross-page cannibalization problem when a plugin nudges the same bare keyword onto every page.
- **Never format a blog post title/URL like "[Post Title] – [Target Keyword]"** (e.g., "What to Pick in Your Hospital Bag – Chicago Newborn Photographer") — "if it feels spammy, it probably is spammy." The target keyword belongs to its own dedicated page.
- **Superlatives are fine even if a competitor already uses the same word** ("best") — "you're not copying... you're saying, well, actually, I'm the best."
- **Cut vague catch-all phrases** ("and more," "beautiful holidays, effortlessly handled") — Matt's test: if an unrelated business could reuse the exact line verbatim, it's too generic.
- **Avoid industry jargon** — write for "the average Joe": "help desk" (internal/corporate) → "how do I fix my computer" / "computer help" / "computer services near me"; for remote/nationwide services get specific with virtual modifiers ("virtual IT support," "virtual Outlook inbox IT support"); "Grade 1" → "four year olds"; "basics" → "essentials"; job-seeker-shaped phrases ("business operations analyst/specialist") read as job-search queries — prefer client-facing phrasing ("business operations consultant").
- **CTA consolidation — one clear action per page.** Identify the single thing you want a visitor to do, then strip every competing CTA. Repeated fixes: 5 competing CTAs (Book Now/Call Now/Email Now/WhatsApp Now/Inquire Now) trimmed to one ("send an inquiry"); 5 competing labels ("speak with Courtney," "meet Courtney," "retain your workforce"...) trimmed to one. Fewer options convert better — "the more options you give people, the less likely they are to take action" (Costco analogy: only ~3 variations stocked per product category). On a contact/booking page, skip filler copy like "complete the form below" — just show the form plus the alternate contact channels.
- **CTA/button placement:** phone numbers must be tap-to-call/clickable everywhere, especially on mobile — anchor a labeled, clickable number at the **bottom** of the screen for a mobile-heavy audience (bottom beats top for thumb reach while scrolling). Emails must be clickable too. Functional tools (calculators, etc.) belong at the very top of the page, not 3 scrolls down.
- **Nav simplification:** identify the one primary action (e.g., "Book"), move everything non-essential (store, careers, policies, partnerships) into the footer or an "About" dropdown; **~7–8 top-nav items** is a workable ceiling. Never build a giant per-condition/per-niche dropdown, especially on mobile (cited at **~60% of internet traffic** — dropdowns are "terrible" UX there). Collapse individual niche/service links into one "Services"/"Treatments" hub linking to sub-pages instead. Nav menu belongs next to the logo, not wrapping/pushing content down. Nav items and logo text should never be marked up as heading tags.
- **Hero/header proportions:** the whole header/contact block should occupy roughly **1/6 of the page**, not half of it. Shrink oversized logos — "what the fuck is your logo doing so big" (one flagged logo ate a third of the screen; contrast case used a logo at roughly a sixteenth of the page).
- **Never use an autoplaying video/rotating image carousel as the hero.** A carousel that freezes on hover (so most visitors, who don't hover, never see any slide's headline) hid the page's own H1 entirely. A blurry/over-compressed autoplaying hero video reads as broken and slows load — replace with one static hero image (ideally real people/crew) plus persistent, always-visible descriptive text.
- **Collection/category page fix (repeated across multiple ecommerce audits):** move informational/marketing copy **below** the product grid — someone landing on a collection page is ready to buy now, not read. Put products/images front and center on load (especially mobile). Add real product photography (a person wearing/using the item) plus descriptive alt text (alt text can be picked up by search). Make sure the URL slug and H1 target the *same* keyword/intent (one page's URL said "polo-shirts" while its H1 said "tennis shirts" — different search intents despite similar products).
- **Testimonials can move higher on a page** (e.g., right under the hero) if it aids conversion — no fixed rule against it. Link testimonials/client lists out to real, verifiable reviews (Google reviews, etc.) rather than only static logos/quotes — "anyone can fabricate a 'review' screenshot with ChatGPT now." Don't use client/company logos you don't have explicit rights to use — consult a trademark lawyer rather than guess.
- **Restyle links** so they're visually distinct: underline them and use a color meaningfully darker/different from body text — barely-distinguishable links were flagged as a real usability defect. Label an in-post outline "Table of Contents," not an unlabeled list of words. **"When in doubt, make it black"** — Matt's default contrast fix.
- **Break up long text with visuals.** Live demo: paste the full post title + body into ChatGPT, prompt *"Create me an infographic for this content,"* then refine with something like *"Make me one that looks like the third"* (selecting a specific style option ChatGPT presented) — produces a Canva-ready, postable infographic in seconds; you can also upload a reference image to steer style.
- **Never rely on hidden text** (font color matching background) to stuff a keyword — an old black-hat trick; Google devalues/ignores it ("these words are white, the background's white, we're going to ignore that"). One Services page had two H1 tags, one hidden via white-text-on-white-background — a classic cloaking pattern, removed on sight.
- **Exception to heading-hierarchy rules:** using an H1 purely for visual styling is acceptable ONLY when organic Google search isn't the page's real traffic source (Matt's own Rank Week landing page draws traffic from Instagram/email, so heading hierarchy "doesn't matter in this case"). Whether Google indexes an H1 present on only one rotating-carousel slide is genuinely uncertain — "it depends on what Google sees when they render the page."

## 4. Content Strategy & Blog Operations

- **Blog cadence:** Matt's own agency runs **2 high-quality posts/week** for clients with good results — technically you could publish 5/week, but quality, not capacity, is the limiting factor. Absolute floor for a struggling site: **1–2 posts/week** minimum to get Google crawling deeper. A site with only 3 blog posts total (since a training event) and most pages "Discovered — currently not indexed" was diagnosed as simply not giving Google enough internal links/content to crawl deeper — prescribed 1–2 posts/week going forward.
- **Once a page starts ranking (even weakly, even just page 3), stop editing/re-optimizing it.** Reinforce it externally instead: publish topical blog posts and build internal links pointing to it, then let time do the rest. A member who kept re-optimizing after reaching page 3 fell out of the rankings entirely as a direct result.
- **Updating an old/underperforming post beats writing a fresh one on the same topic.** Diagnostic checklist for a post that isn't ranking: too long? too short? doesn't include the target keyword enough times? doesn't answer the question immediately?
- **Content depth for "cost/price" queries:** cover the full range (low/mid/high end) and mention multiple relevant regions/locations even if your business only operates in one — mirrors what top-ranking competitors already do for this query shape.
- **Competitor-structure teardown:** pull up whoever ranks #1 for your target keyword, note exactly what subtopics/H2s they cover, and make sure your own piece covers the same ground (not necessarily the same length).
- **Reformat an evergreen page that was mistakenly published as a blog post.** Important, permanent pages get absorbed into the "recent posts" feed and lose prominence — rebuild as a real site page (proper nav placement, e.g., a dropdown under the relevant service) and have blog posts link INTO it, not the reverse.
- **Avalanche method (keyword sequencing):** target low-volume/low-difficulty keywords first to win them — this signals to Google that your site can be trusted with traffic (engagement, clicks, time on page, conversions). Only after that foundation is built do higher-volume/higher-difficulty keywords become realistically winnable. "Think of it as a snowball rolling down a hill" — build floor by floor.
- **Content pruning rule (site relaunch/redesign):** pull every existing piece of content and check three things — is it indexed? is it getting traffic? is it optimized? If all three are "no" ("which is like 99% of the time"), delete it. Google gives every site a crawl budget; a majority-unindexed site is a bad signal. **Exception:** keep a page if the underlying thing is still actively searched for (a sold-out/discontinued product, e.g., a closed theater show) — mark it unavailable in visible copy, set schema inventory to 0, and add a "notify me if this returns" signup rather than deleting.
- **When unsure about cutting a content block, move it to draft, don't delete** — re-test over the next month or two and adjust from there.
- **Blog-vs-money-page cannibalization guard:** the money page owns the exact buyer-intent phrase; blog posts cover adjacent educational angles and reference the exact money-page phrase **at most once**, typically near the bottom as a link back — never repeated throughout the post.
- **Don't build a keyword-optimized SEO blog post per individual client/session using the same city keyword over and over** (e.g., "Chicago Newborn Photographer" reused post after post) — that's self-cannibalization ("you're now competing against yourself"). Treat these as case-study pages instead, not SEO posts.
- **Retrofitting internal links onto early posts:** don't force links before there's enough related content. Publish first (aim for 1–2 posts/week); once you're at roughly **10–20 posts**, go back and add about **3 internal links per post**. Links added after the fact still work but take longer to show ranking effect than links present at publish time.
- **Tags vs. categories:** never use blog tags — platforms like Wix (and Go High Level's blog editor "keywords" field) auto-generate one thin archive page per tag, often holding only 1–2 posts, wasting crawl budget site-wide ("this is just wasting your crawl budget" / "tags don't work"). Use **5–10 categories** instead and place all content into those. One real case: **162 auto-generated tag pages** found and deleted via a Semrush/GSC crawl, replaced with a handful of consolidated categories (example: one "SEO" category replacing separate "SEO," "local SEO," "marketing techniques," "local SEO for beginners" tags).
- **Never allow open/unmoderated member or guest blog posting without SEO guidelines** (meta description, internal links, H2 tags, minimum word count) — it wastes crawl budget site-wide. Fix: either (a) enforce lightweight guidelines before publishing, or (b) move member-submitted content out of the blog entirely into a discussion forum, since forum content still counts as indexable UGC without diluting the SEO-structured blog.
- **Turn blog comments off entirely for a small-business blog.** The realistic outcome is spam/backlink-fishing, not real engagement — "the only comments you're ever going to get are spammers." Check the rendered page source even after disabling comments in the CMS: one theme still exposed comment-count/comment-action/comments-feed markup that a spam bot was exploiting to post backlink spam despite no visible comment form. Change any "leave us a comment" post-footer CTA to a link to the Contact page instead.
- **Portfolio/project pages are for conversion (visual proof), not necessarily built to rank.** Pair a portfolio page with a separate topic-focused blog post (about the material/service type used, e.g. roofing material) that links to the specific project — captures search intent while the portfolio page keeps doing its trust/conversion job. Add a third-party quote (GC/architect) linked to their LinkedIn profile for added credibility.
- **LinkedIn-post-to-blog-post workflow:** take existing LinkedIn posts (written by SMEs/leadership), use ChatGPT to flesh each into a full blog post on your own site, then cross-link both directions (blog links to the LinkedIn post as a quote/authority signal; LinkedIn post can link back for full detail). Never publish the *same* full content on both your site and LinkedIn — LinkedIn has higher authority, and Google can flag the weaker copy as duplicate content, hurting your own SEO.
- **Social sharing rule:** post short-form takes with your own added context (roughly **~100 words**) on platforms that support clickable links (LinkedIn, Facebook, X/Twitter, Reddit, Tumblr, Pinterest) linking to the full post — never a bare link with no framing. **Never post links on Instagram or TikTok** — those platforms don't support clickable links in posts. Don't build a plugin/widget that auto-pulls your latest social posts onto your site (slows the site, adds no value — compared to old "latest tweets" widgets); let content exist once as a real blog post and link to/from the social version instead.
- **Judging whether an on-page change worked:** wait roughly **2–3 months**, using that window to keep publishing blog posts and internally linking to the changed page — framed as helping "the ecosystem," not passive waiting.

## 5. AI Visibility & AI Overviews

- **"AI Markdown" post structure (Matt's live experiment):**
  1. **TL;DR / Summary at the very top**, labeled "TL;DR" or "Summary" — answer the target question in 1–2 sentences immediately, written so an AI Overview/ChatGPT/Claude could clip it verbatim. Example: Q: "How much does SEO cost in 2026?" → *"Quality SEO typically costs between [X] and [Y] per month. For most small businesses, it takes six to 12 months to see real results. [Pricing] usually includes content creation, backlinks, technical fixes, strategy. Cheap SEO often costs more in the long run."*
  2. **Restate the answer again in the opening paragraphs** with more context before the full detail — a second extraction point in case the TL;DR is missed.
  3. **Key Takeaways section at the end**, bulleted, reinforcing the same core facts a third time — a safety net for readers/crawlers who miss the top. (Collapse this with any FAQ/Conclusion sections that just restate the same thing — don't stack three separate closers.)
  4. Write like decent social content — cut filler ("ums," irrelevant asides), get to the point in the first couple of paragraphs.
  - **Test protocol:** only run this on **2–3 pieces of content at a time**, never 10–20 — too many simultaneous changes makes it impossible to track which change caused what.
  - **Result:** adding this structure to one post was the post that hit **Google Discover** — no other change made to it.
  - **Schema isn't the deciding factor for AI-overview recommendation.** Matt researched who ranks #1 for a term vs. what AI recommends: the #1-ranked page had heavy schema (product + review schema) *and* better content, but AI overviews still weren't recommending it first. His read: AI is optimizing to "pull the answer out and hand it back to the user" — extractability of the answer seems to matter more than schema's presence. He runs **no schema** on his own site. Presented as an inference from one observed case, not a proven rule.
- **Getting cited in AI Overviews/ChatGPT via social platforms:** AI Overviews and ChatGPT reportedly weight community-based platforms (LinkedIn, Quora) alongside website mentions/backlinks — the goal isn't to rank the social post itself, it's to give AI models more mentions of your brand next to the topic/keyword. Method: pick a target question/keyword (e.g., "How much does SEO cost?"), post it on LinkedIn with a short ChatGPT-written answer, then in the **comments** (not the post body) add a line like *"Here's the link to the full SEO cost breakdown"* with the link to the full article. **Live risk surfaced on-call:** Matt's own LinkedIn account got banned mid-call ("Your account has been banned because it's multiple violations, spam policies") with no advance warning — cause unconfirmed, but a real risk of this repeated self-linking comment pattern.
- **AI visibility currently cannot be tracked by any tool**, including Semrush's "AI visibility" feature — these tools invent their own test prompts/searches and check if you show up, which isn't the same as tracking real user prompts. "Anybody selling AI visibility, they are making up terms, they're making up searches or prompts." **AI-referral traffic** (ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity as a *source*) CAN be tracked inside Google Analytics — that's a different, measurable thing from "AI visibility"/citation appearances.
- **E-A-T build-out for expertise/health niches:** create individual sub-pages per credentialed team member (doctor, therapist, etc.); add a relevant quote from that expert plus credentials into existing articles; if no published study exists yet, link to the expert's LinkedIn or other authoritative profile; conference talks and published papers are good credibility "juicers." Any quote/external link must be topically relevant to the specific content — don't add random expert quotes just to check an E-A-T box. Don't link away from your own site to a person's old press coverage — instead go TO those sites and ask them to link back to you (unlinked-mentions play applied to a professional bio).
- **Google's own image-understanding AI is improving** ("if you upload a picture to ChatGPT and say what's in this picture, it'll tell you") but Matt is explicit he doesn't know how much ranking weight this carries yet — an open question, not a settled factor. Realistic product photography (e.g., people actually skiing/in-context) is confirmed to matter more for user trust/conversion than for ranking directly.

## 6. Local SEO & Google Business Profile (GBP)

- **GBP post formula:** target keyword at the very top (optionally in quotes) → link to the specific service page you want that keyword to rank for → add your GBP CID link → link to a PREVIOUS GBP post at the bottom (internally linking your own posts to each other). **Posting cadence: Monday/Wednesday/Friday at minimum; 1–2x/week is the absolute floor.** One aggressive test case posted 6x/day and combined it with geotagging (see below) to hit #1/#2 across a multi-county area.
- **Getting your CID link:** open Google Maps → search your business → click Share → copy the link → paste into Matt's free "What is my CID" tool. Linking your site to your GBP via the CID link passes more authority than linking to a generic Maps place-page link.
- **GBP "Edit Services":** click into every listed/suggested service and add a brief description for each relevant one — a quick, high-value fix for a dormant profile. Caution: filling out MORE services than your website actually documents can look like profile padding/"overkill" to Google rather than purely beneficial — audit for a services/website mismatch rather than assuming more is automatically better.
- **Geotagging images** for GBP posts/site content: not required, but recommended; embed GPS metadata in image files (even different corners of the target market's map grid) before uploading. Google is confirmed to strip EXIF metadata on download (Matt tested this himself ~2 years prior) — yet clients using this tactic still show correlated ranking movement. Presented as an anecdotal, works-in-practice tactic with no proven mechanism, not a settled ranking factor.
- **Citations:** use a citation-building service (Bright Local: **$2/citation**, roughly **$180–200** for a full round, or cited elsewhere as **~$200 flat**) to list across Yelp, Bing, Apple Maps, Facebook, Yellow Pages/411, etc. — **one-time job, no ongoing monthly management needed.** Also check what backlink/citation sources your dominant local competitors already have (Chamber of Commerce, Better Business Bureau) and get listed in the same places.
- **NAP (name/address/phone) must be present and crawlable in the site footer**, linking to the GBP via the CID link — Google "looks for that to determine if it's a local business or not." A site rebuild by an outside agency once broke this along with canonical tags on **roughly 60–70% of pages** — fixed via footer NAP re-add, CID re-add, canonical audit, and confirming site-wide HTTPS (installed SSL doesn't guarantee every internal link was updated — use the "Better Search and Replace" WP plugin to bulk-swap hardcoded `http://` to `https://`, after backing up the site).
- **Replace live Google Maps iframe embeds with a static WebP screenshot** that links out to the real Maps/GBP listing on click — Google's own PageSpeed Insights test flags its own embed code as a page-speed drag, and visitors weren't interacting with the live embed anyway.
- **"Near me" keywords cannot be forced via on-page text.** Near-me results are location/device-based (whoever is physically closest, with location enabled) — not a keyword-stuffing opportunity. Fix instead by adding genuine local signals: explicit service-area names and a visible business address.
- **Location-page architecture, by business shape:**
  - **Multi-city service business:** build a dedicated page per location, in order: (1) location pages first, full stop, before anything else; (2) blog posts that link to those specific location pages; (3) citations/GBP for every location you can — skip citations you can't cover rather than blocking on it.
  - **Don't build a page per city × per category** (one photographer had drafted 25 cities × 6 event types = 66 pages — flagged as far too many). Target the primary/home city on each core service page, then add one "locations we serve" page/section linking to secondary cities.
  - **Don't blanket-write a location blog post for every target city** — only write one where genuine local value exists ("best venues in Dallas"); for general topic posts, just name-check 2–3 service-area cities in the body and link to those location pages.
  - **Multi-location templating is fine, but content must be substantively unique per page** — reference real local landmarks, parks, monuments, venues, neighborhoods, or (for a florist) nearby funeral homes/hospitals/high schools. Duplicate-looking, city-swapped pages CAN still work, but only if the business also has real backlinks, subtly-real content differentiation, or long domain tenure — the safer, controllable path is genuine local specificity.
  - **Expanding beyond an already-won mid-size market:** rather than pouring effort into a much bigger, more contested market, build dedicated **service pages** (not just blog posts) for smaller nearby towns with low/no visible volume but essentially zero competition.
  - **Studio-town vs. bigger nearby city:** keep the homepage targeting the actual studio/base town; build a distinct page targeting the bigger nearby city, framed around proximity (e.g., "20-minute drive from Schaumburg").
  - **Get new local pages indexed faster** by linking to each one directly from the homepage (a real "areas we serve" section with real links), not only buried in the footer — footer-only links index slower.
- **Multiple GBPs for one business:** if a business genuinely serves two distinct audiences (e.g., commercial vs. residential), set up a **second, separately-optimized GBP** rather than funneling both segments to one shared, un-optimized portfolio landing page. A second GBP at the same street address generally requires proof of a physically separate entrance (a window sticker sufficed in one case). For a multi-division company, don't send GBP traffic to a shared hub/portfolio page listing every sub-brand — point it directly at the ONE specific, relevant, already-optimized site; check the hub's real organic traffic in Semrush first (usually near-zero/mostly branded). **Never flip-flop a GBP's linked website** — Google can take **1–3 months or more** to "learn" what a linked site is about after a change, so pick one destination and leave it. While a division has no GBP yet, build backlinks to it in the meantime.
- **GMB troubleshooting when a ranking/map-pack position drops:** (1) check if you changed anything recently; (2) compare review count AND review velocity against top competitors (velocity can beat a competitor even if their total count is higher); (3) confirm citations are built; (4) confirm NAP is in the footer and links to the GBP via CID; (5) check whether GBP-listed services match/exceed the actual website (mismatch may look like padding); (6) factor in that Google periodically updates its map-pack algorithm independent of anything you did.
- **Hours:** if you don't have fixed hours (appointment-only), use GBP's "appointment only" option rather than posting fake fixed hours. Don't set hours to 24 hours — bad UX (someone calling at 3am still can't reach you) — use real regular hours.
- **A fully online-only business with no real local service area will get its GMB listing rejected** — verification for local-service businesses may now require video + signage proof (not just an old mail-in postcard code). Service-area-only listings (no fixed address, e.g. a traveling photographer) are allowed and still helpful, but **less effective than a listed physical address**.
- **Diagnosing weak GBP call-conversion:** check the mobile vs. desktop split in GBP analytics; if mostly mobile, make sure the phone number is clickable and text-labeled (not just an icon), anchored at the bottom of the screen. One profile's first month showed 269 profile views/33 total actions/1 phone call (likely a drop)/33 direction requests/9 website clicks — root cause was a floating icon-only Viber button, no visible/clickable number.
- **A single business-name keyword can dominate an entire local result** if the literal business name IS the exact local keyword (e.g., a business named for "unblocking of canals" + city) — a gray-area tactic Matt has seen work (some businesses put their target keyword directly into the GBP business-name field) but explicitly does NOT recommend, since he's seen Google contacted directly about similar cases with no resolution.
- **Relocating a business:** start publishing blog content about the NEW location now, before moving, while keeping current site copy pointed at the CURRENT location; try to register a GBP in the new location ahead of the move; once the old location closes, go through every page and swap location references old → new. New-location ranking timelines vary: **3–6 months, possibly up to a year**, depending on existing authority, competition, and market.

## 7. Backlinks

- **You do NOT need hundreds of backlinks to rank.** Many members already rank from blog posts alone (long-tail, low-competition keywords) with zero meaningful link building — several low-competition local/niche keywords across these calls ranked #1–#3 with **zero backlinks** (e.g., a page ranking 3rd for "Metal Roofing Vancouver," a page ranking #1 for "wedding locations Hamilton" at 880 searches/month with 0 backlinks, three zero-backlink #1-rankers — Indigo, Scholastic, "Russo Book" — for an educational-workbook keyword set).
- **HeyTony's own standard client cadence: 4 backlinks/month** (~48–52/year) — "that's all we do" and it gets "crazy results." Don't chase 10, 20, or 50 links/month. HeyTony's own backlink profile as a benchmark: **1,229 total referring domains, but only 598 active + follow**, built over ~5 years — raw link counts overstate real equity; many links are low-authority, citation-only, or no-follow.
- **Domain Authority is volatile and non-linear** — one member's DA rose 24→28 (+4) after six months of steady decline, credited to a single press mention/backlink from The Guardian, even though other press links he had didn't move it. More press/mentions is still the stated path to keep climbing.
- **Backlink-building tactics (favorites, cheap/relationship-driven):**
  - **Chamber of Commerce:** join, then ask them to build you a member landing page with logo/text + 2–3 links to *specific* service pages (not one generic "visit website" link) — most chambers don't know SEO and will let you structure it however you want. You can join multiple neighboring-town chambers if they'll allow it. Bonus: local networking events. Real results: one member went from 3 backlinks to 12 in one push for **$75** total; another paid **~$500/year** for what he believes is the oldest Chamber in Canada, negotiating a homepage link + featured profile for an extra **$750**. Chamber quality varies — at least one member left theirs, calling it overly focused on new-member acquisition.
  - **Charity/local sponsorship (Matt's own favorite):** ask what a small donation (**$20–50** cited, or become a "bronze sponsor" for a small fee) costs to get your logo listed as a donor/contributor on a local charity's site — "you're basically buying a backlink, but you're just donating to charity... 100% tax write-off." Make sure the logo itself links to your site, not just alt text.
  - **Vendor/partner backlinks:** if you buy from or supply a local business, ask to be listed on their site as a vendor/partner ("it'll help me sell your products more").
  - **Unlinked mentions:** find every place already mentioning your name/business without a link, then email them the exact paragraph already written with the link inserted — all they do is copy-paste it in (a weed-store client's rankings "went through the roof" after this). Set up **Google Alerts** for your own name/business for future mentions. Ahrefs has a paid, expensive unlinked-mentions finder as an alternative. Even paywalled mentions are worth a direct ask for a link — takes ~5 minutes, and Google likely still crawls behind the paywall anyway. One case: a large organization's unlinked mention became a confirmed dofollow link from a site with roughly **60–70 domain authority**, just by asking.
  - **Guest posting:** reach out to adjacent-audience sites (e.g., certification schools in your field) offering to write content for them in exchange for a link — frame it as "I want to help you create content," never "give me a link."
  - **Backlink-gap analysis:** compare your backlink profile against competitors to find links they have that you don't, then go get those same links (chambers, directories, news sites, local listings). HeyTony's own **Link Gap Analyzer** tool (see §10) automates this.
  - **"ABC linking"** (A links to B, B links to C, C links to back to A — no direct reciprocal pair) — one of Matt's favorite backlink tactics, avoids the "cancels each other out" problem of reciprocal links.
  - **Press releases:** AB Newswire for national syndication/credibility/"training AI" (see §10 for pricing); for local businesses, ALSO get a press release placed through the LOCAL newspaper specifically — it sends local-relevance signals a national wire doesn't. A site relaunch after years offline benefits from a simple "we're back, here's what we do" release — doesn't need to be "big news."
  - **Blog content is the real linkable asset** — most sites won't link to a commercial service/product page but will link to genuinely useful informational content; write the blog post, then optionally point paid links at that post instead of the sales page.
- **Reciprocal/mutual link exchanges between peers are discouraged** — "if two people are voting for each other... that kind of cancels it out." A member's 3–4-person link-exchange accountability group was corrected off this approach in favor of one-directional opportunities from a larger community.
- **Standard reply when someone asks you to place their link:** "sure, but I need you to get me a link from a third-party site first" — they source it, you place theirs in return.
- **Out-of-state/out-of-geo backlinks carry lesser relevance weight than same-state/same-region ones** for a locally-focused business.
- **Ignore bulk-backlink spam pitches** ("100 links," etc.) — "ignore that shit." Named bad-value example: **Backlink Management IO** (~$40/month bulk subscription) flagged directly as "don't do that." A prior vendor charged one member **$1,500** for 75–78 identical directory listings plus a **$199/month** "refresh" upsell, falsely claiming "Google partner" status — **the only real "Google partner" status is a Google Ads partner; nobody has special algorithm access.** Equivalent citation work costs ~$2/citation one-time via Bright Local — cancel any such retainer, no ongoing management needed.
- **Disavow vs. dilute:** Matt has personally never disavowed a client's links and has seen previously-strong rankings tank after a client disavowed on their own initiative — "sometimes toxic links or spam links are helping you, but there's no way to know." **If you're getting real traffic/rankings from a domain, do not disavow.** Disavowing is more reasonable specifically when a domain has no real traffic/rankings to lose. Preferred default: **dilution** — build more good backlinks so bad ones become a smaller share of the overall profile, rather than risk removing a link that might secretly help. To remove a bad link: contact the site owner directly first (they may comply free, ignore you, or demand payment — treat a payment demand as extortion, don't just pay); disavowing is the fallback, done via Google Search Console (Links → Security & Manual Actions) or via Semrush if connected to your GSC.
- **"Link farm" flagged domain, two remediation paths:** (A) stay on the domain — contact referring-domain owners to request removal of spammy/irrelevant links, then actively build new legitimate ones; (B) if the domain has no real rankings/traffic to lose, register a similar new domain (add a word, change TLD) and migrate fresh rather than fight the existing link profile. Spammy/zero-authority referring domains (random directories, foreign-TLD junk, adult sites) are **normal for every website** — don't panic over their mere presence; the issue is volume/relevance, not existence. A "natural" backlink profile needs: a mix of follow/no-follow (100% follow looks unnatural/suspicious), a unique link profile (not identical to competitors'), and a diverse pool of referring IPs/subnets (not one network).
- **301 redirect vs. rebuilding at the original URL, on a site relaunch:** for backlinks you don't care about keeping, do a straight 301 to the closest relevant current page. For backlinks from high-value/authoritative sites (The Guardian, Wikipedia, Pinterest, niche press), consider **rebuilding a page at the exact original URL** instead of redirecting — this passes more link authority through than a redirect, especially valuable since a linking site might later clean up its own outbound links and drop ones that only point to a redirect.
- **No-follow links still carry real value** beyond direct link equity — brand credibility and "training AI" to associate your brand with a topic, even without passing link juice (e.g., a national wire syndication that was no-follow).
- **Two competing/old-vs-new sites both ranking against each other:** don't reflexively 301 one into the other. Leave both live, pick the one with more traffic/better rankings to actively work on, and just stop updating the other — reversible later, not an immediate abandon-or-delete decision.

## 8. Keyword Research Method

- **Semrush is Matt's stated primary/preferred tool** — "there's not really anything free that does this kind of keyword research." Always set the location to the correct country/city before reading volume/difficulty (a common mistake: leaving it on the wrong default region). Free trial available (**~1 week** per one mention, **2 weeks** per another). Matt made a ~45-minute public YouTube walkthrough of Semrush end-to-end (UI has shifted slightly since recording, but the strategy still applies). Beware Semrush free-trial abuse: you can retrigger trials with different emails, but cancel immediately — Semrush charges and reportedly does not refund.
- **Keyword research workflow, from scratch:** open Semrush → type a seed keyword → set location to the correct country → check volume → widen/narrow the term systematically (e.g., "bookkeeping" → "bookkeeper" → "St. Petersburg FL bookkeeping" → "bookkeeping service in St. Petersburg" → "bookkeeping service, St. Petersburg") → treat it as guesswork, start broad then get specific. Same looping logic for hard-to-name niches (cat behavior consultant → coach/therapist/trainer/behaviorist, US/UK spelling): run every plausible variation, compare volume + difficulty, then commit to only 1–2 of them.
- **Zero-volume keyword handling:** roughly **15% of all searches every day are brand-new queries** never searched before — cited repeatedly as the reason a 0-volume reading isn't automatically disqualifying. Decision test: check who ranks on page 1 for the term and look at their backlink counts — if any page-1 result has zero backlinks, the keyword is winnable despite the 0-volume reading. Zero/near-zero volume across every phrasing tried is still a real signal that people genuinely aren't searching that exact phrase — not a tool failure — but low volume often correlates with high buyer intent for niche B2B verticals or very specific service combinations, so it can still be worth targeting.
- **National keyword-difficulty/volume numbers can badly mislead local intent** — a term can show **KD 88 nationally** with no realistic chance a single city accounts for that volume; check the local pack directly instead of trusting a blended national number.
- **Keyword difficulty is calculated largely from the backlink profiles of pages currently ranking for that term** — heavily backlinked top results make a keyword harder, independent of the term's actual volume (demonstrated live: a keyword with 0 search volume still scored ~60 KD).
- **Ranking-target strategy:** don't fixate on beating an entrenched #1 that has a mountain of backlinks — aim for position 2 or 3 instead; still delivers meaningful traffic without an unwinnable fight.
- **Singular vs. plural keyword variants can carry very different difficulty** — check them separately (one case: plural form KD ~16–17, singular form KD 8 at higher volume — the singular was the better tradeoff).
- **Vertical/niche-specific page strategy:** build one dedicated page per client-type/audience served (e.g., for a bookkeeper: "bookkeeping for HVAC companies," "...landscaping and lawn care," "...pressure washing," "...pest control," "...pool service," "...junk removal") — roll out over time, not all at once; write to that industry's actual specifics (name real vendors, note their location for local relevancy, link to them).
- **Systematic optimization order for large catalogs:** list every candidate keyword with volume + difficulty, sort by highest volume + lowest difficulty, and optimize pages in that order.
- **Competing against giant/head-term-dominant competitors** (department stores, national chains, Adidas-scale brands): concede the broad head term and shift to descriptor-first long-tail variants (modifier before the product/service name — "navy leather ballet flats," "silver ballet flats," "funny tennis shirts") instead of the bare category term.
- **Tool comparison:** Semrush (preferred, most accurate per Matt, priciest), Ahrefs (sometimes cheaper plan than Semrush; **Ahrefs Lite $29/month, capped at 100 searches/month**), Ubersuggest (cheapest, least accurate, disliked layout, runs on a lifetime-deal business model that's profitable because most buyers quit SEO within a month or two), KeySearch (used by at least one member). "Cheap and good — you can't really have both when it comes to a keyword research tool." Each vendor applies its own undisclosed adjustment on top of the same underlying Google Ads data, producing different volume/difficulty numbers per tool — cross-tool comparisons are inherently unreliable. Workaround: bulk-discover cheaply in Ubersuggest, then verify the shortlist in the pricier/more-accurate tool.
- **Hey Keywords Tool** (Matt's own) pulls keyword volume directly from Google Ads — the underlying source every other tool re-estimates from — positioned as more honest than "making up the volumes," with accuracy expected to improve as more users feed its database.
- **ChatGPT for keyword brainstorming:** *"what are some keywords and keyword variations that I should be going after for this page?"* — then cross-check results in Semrush. Caveat: ChatGPT "did not do a good job whatsoever" when used to actually write keyword-targeted content, and "will make shit up" regarding actual SEO best practices — don't trust its on-page SEO suggestions over the rules taught on these calls.
- **Question-based/long-tail mining for niches that have exhausted head-term keywords:** use **Answer the Public** or Insiders' own **Reddit tool** to surface full-sentence questions people actually type, then use the literal question as the blog title/H1 and answer it thoroughly — don't force keyword-first phrasing into the H1. Reddit content-mining without a dedicated tool: search Google with `site:reddit.com [keyword]` — Reddit's own on-site search is weak, but Google has indexed it thoroughly.
- **A recurring complaint found on Reddit can seed a full content batch:** blog post answering it → repurposed into a LinkedIn post → repurposed into a dozen separate Instagram posts, all from one thread.

## 9. Technical SEO & Indexing

- **Robots.txt fixes:** remove any `crawl-delay` directive; fix a broken/stale sitemap reference (especially left over after removing a plugin like Yoast); add an explicit user-agent allow rule for any legitimate SEO crawler being blocked.
- **Canonical tag bugs are real, fixable defects, not cosmetic** — a page showing as indexed/included per the SEO plugin's own settings can still be excluded from the generated sitemap if its canonical tag points to a different URL than itself. Diagnostic sequence: confirm the page is set to index → confirm it isn't excluded from the sitemap → View Source and check the canonical tag → check for redirects or a second competing URL for the same content → if the root cause can't be found, delete and recreate the page to force a clean re-index.
- **Orphan pages will not rank no matter how good the content is** — Google can't find them. Triage method: (1) try to reach the flagged URL via normal site navigation to confirm it's truly unreachable; (2) check for a duplicate (telltale sign: a `-1` URL suffix); (3) use browser find (Ctrl+F) on an admin collections list to spot the duplicate; (4) if duplicate: delete the orphan outright; (5) if not a duplicate but targeting a real search keyword: don't delete — link to it from somewhere (nav dropdown, blog post, another page) so it's no longer orphaned — "you can't hide that page." Fix systemic orphaning via a proper dropdown/sub-nav so every page is reachable within ~3 clicks, grouped logically by category.
- **Don't build near-duplicate collection/category pages that only differ by keyword phrasing for the same products** (e.g., "red cushions" / "black cushions" / "sofa cushions") — treat as product attributes/filters, not separate landing pages, unless there's a real reason for a dedicated page.
- **Sitemap issues:** a single-page site (built in a page builder like Canva-exported HTML) will have a sitemap containing only one URL — the root blocker to ranking at all; must be split into multiple pages, each targeting a distinct keyword/service/season, before ranking is possible. Request indexing in GSC after every page update as a standard nudge (doesn't force an immediate crawl but is the norm); alternatively, Google should discover new pages automatically without manual resubmission in most cases — the two guidance points reflect different calls' emphasis, both are presented as valid.
- **Fixing "Discovered — currently not indexed"**: don't resubmit URLs manually — increase internal links pointing at those pages, publish blog content that links to them, build backlinks, and keep publishing on a regular cadence so Google actually crawls deeper into the site.
- **URL nesting:** two-subfolders-deep URL structures before reaching the real content page are a red flag — one subfolder (`/services/`, `/blog/`, `/video/`, `/case-studies/`) is the norm. Don't nest blog posts as child pages of another page (e.g., `/about/news/the-roof-replacement-process`) — keep posts flat/top-level for short, legible URLs. Rename a "News" section to "Blog" if the content is just blog posts — "News" signals time-sensitive content to Google, which hurts normal evergreen posts.
- **Site speed:** on Squarespace, optimizing images is basically the only lever available. Site speed's ranking impact is relative/conditional, not absolute — if everyone in a niche is equally slow it doesn't cost you; a meaningfully faster competitor *could* theoretically outrank you if everything else is equal, but Matt has "seen slow websites outrank fast ones," so it's not deterministic. A subjectively "fast-feeling" site can still score poorly on PageSpeed Insights (and vice versa) — don't trust the "feels fast" proxy.
  - **WP Rocket** (WordPress caching/performance plugin) settings walkthrough: File Optimization tab (minify CSS, minify JS, delay JS execution — not a universal preset, varies by site/build), turn caching on, enable "Add missing image dimensions" (fixes the PageSpeed warning about explicit width/height), enable preload, use Advanced Rules' "never cache these pages" exclusion as needed. If toggling an optimization visibly breaks the site, un-toggle it and clear the cache — self-resolves. Pricing recalled inconsistently: roughly **$59/year** (previously a one-time lifetime license) vs. an initial misremembering of "~$100"; separate CDN add-on roughly **$7–10/month**. One attendee reported it took their PageSpeed score from ~20 to 75, then to 100 with additional manual tweaking.
  - **Convert PNG/JPEG images to WebP sitewide** — same/better quality, much smaller file size. Tools: **Squoosh** (one at a time), a **Photoshop batch "action"** for bulk conversion (Photoshop rentable ~$10–20/month, then cancel), or the free tool **"X and Convert"** (batch upload + resize). Some hosts don't support WebP at all (flagged as ironic for a photography host).
  - **Mobile page-speed target: at least 80%.** One flagged case: mobile score 39, desktop mid-70s, on Squarespace — mobile called "horrible."
  - Watch for a **cross-device image bug**: if images render fine on your own machine but disappear in incognito/other devices, describe the exact symptom to an AI coding assistant and troubleshoot from there; never batch-convert an entire site's images at once — change one page, verify, then move to the next, starting on a low-stakes page (not the homepage).
- **Heading-tag audits catch recurring platform defaults:** confirm the H1 is the page's actual target keyword, not the site logo/theme name accidentally set as H1 (a common theme default); watch for a repeated site-wide module (e.g., "what's going on in the [region]") rendered as H1 on every single blog post; watch for separate mobile/desktop blocks each rendering their own duplicate heading tags.
- **Iframe-embedded tools are attributed to the OTHER platform, not you.** If a calculator/widget is pulled in via iframe from a separate platform, Google sees it as belonging to that platform's domain, not yours — rebuild or integrate it natively into your own site's code instead.
- **410 (not a soft redirect, not just leaving the page live) is the correct response for a permanently discontinued service/page** — explicitly tells Google the content/service no longer exists.
- **Avoid single-page/landing-page-only sites** unless traffic is exclusively paid-ads/email-driven — Google wants sitewide context; one page blocks internal linking and limits backlink diversity.
- **Checking indexing status:** `site:` + a specific URL, or `site:rootdomain.com` for the whole site, typed directly into Google.
- **Platform migration notes:** Wix's official RSS-feed export/import tool is the path off Wix's page-bloat/duplicate-content problems (word-count tools have reported Wix pages at 60,000–72,000 words when visible content was far shorter — code bloat, not real content) — migrate to WordPress. When migrating platforms without extra SEO work on the soon-to-be-replaced site, write blog posts now but hold publishing until the new site is live, building a backlog to drip-publish at a steady cadence once live. When launching a brand-new site with no separate dev environment, point the live domain straight at the install and build page by page rather than staging offline first; finish the homepage + a couple of core pages, then submit a sitemap to GSC before building out every remaining page; run only ONE domain live even if you own multiple (people only find the unused one if you tell them).
- **Platform/builder choice for an SEO-first rebuild:** WordPress-native builders (e.g., Bricks) are preferred over non-WordPress drag-and-drop platforms (e.g., ShowIt) because WordPress gives direct control over SEO-relevant tags/settings; a non-WordPress framework with only its blog bolted onto WordPress requires extra manual workaround for on-page SEO. Trade-off: the non-WordPress platform can be easier to use/update long-term, at the cost of tag-level control.
- **SEO plugin choice:** pick exactly one — Yoast, Rank Math, All in One SEO, or SEOPress are functionally interchangeable (mainly title tag/meta description editing, sometimes internal-link suggestions) — don't switch a client off whatever they're already using just for personal preference, and don't run two at once.
- **A brand-new site (under ~1 month old) will not rank meaningfully yet regardless of on-page fixes** — Google needs time to trust it before rewarding anything; keep publishing and wait, don't panic-optimize.
- **Fixing orphan pages left behind by an old site migration:** if old pages are gone from the sitemap but still exist on the server/in the CMS, the likely cause is that internal links somewhere on the *new* site still point to the old URLs — fix by auditing and correcting every internal link across the site (flagged explicitly as a bigger fix, not a quick one).

## 13. Tradeoffs & Nuance

- **Domain Authority / backlink counts are directional, not precise** — headline link totals overstate value because many links are low-authority, citation-only, or no-follow (HeyTony's own 1,229 referring domains vs. only 598 active+follow illustrates the gap).
- **Rank volatility after a legitimate fix looks alarming day-to-day but usually isn't** — a page jumping to #7, then #9, then disappearing for over a week is expected noise after a real optimization (unpublishing a competing page, fixing keyword conflict), not proof the fix failed. Judgment should wait a full month; third-party rank trackers add their own data-freshness noise on top of Google's real volatility (a strong Semrush Map Rank Tracker position didn't match an actual incognito search once).
- **Pricing transparency is not a universal rule** — a cat-behavior consultant is pointed toward listing prices everywhere plus a pricing blog post, while a luxury photographer is validated in deliberately hiding prices because her buyer's decision journey runs through an in-person/call experience rather than comparison shopping. The real test is "what does your buyer's actual decision process look like," tracked via conversion over the following month or two — not a blanket disclosure policy.
- **Citations vs. backlinks are not interchangeable** — citations are cheap/free directory-style NAP listings (Bright Local, ~$2 each); backlinks require more relationship-driven work (chambers, charities, guest posts, vendor partners) and carry more direct authority weight.
- **GBP "services" richness can cut both ways** — filling out more services than your website documents may look like padding/overkill to Google rather than purely beneficial; audit for a mismatch rather than assuming "more is always better."
- **Third-party AI-visibility scoring isn't fraudulent so much as structurally unable to reflect reality yet** — it's a best-effort proxy built on invented test prompts, since neither Google nor the major AI chat products expose real user-query data today.
- **Local Chamber of Commerce value varies chapter to chapter** — several members got great outcomes ($75–$500 for meaningful backlinks/landing pages plus networking), but at least one member left theirs over a new-member-acquisition-only focus — vet the specific local chapter rather than assuming uniform quality.
- **Word count and keyword density don't correlate with rank in either direction on their own** — a shorter page using the exact keyword once outranked a longer page using it 10x; matching search intent and formatting mattered more than either variable in isolation.
- **Referring domains/domain authority help but aren't gatekeeping** — a #1-ranked competitor with a single referring domain still beat Wix, Canva, GoDaddy, and Weebly, likely on relevance/topical depth/content age rather than raw authority.
- **Rewriting an already-ranking page is a real gamble** — you might gain conversions but lose the existing ranking; the safer sequence is to add supporting content/internal links first and only rewrite after a defined trial window (~a month) if nothing moves.
- **AI/automation tools get a qualified yes, never an unsupervised one** — useful as a research aid/co-pilot (keyword research, drafting, infographic generation), but Matt won't deploy them unsupervised for a real client; he's separately testing a fully AI-built-and-run site specifically to see if that can rank, but is explicit it isn't yet something he'd recommend for an actual business.
- **A keyword that looks like a real customer question isn't automatically worth targeting** — check what the SERP is actually serving for that literal phrase first; if the ranking intent doesn't match your business, leave it alone regardless of internal demand for the content.
- **A page can rank #1 and still barely convert** — that's not automatically a page problem; it may mean the business needs a separate, higher-commercial-intent page rather than forcing an informational post to sell.
- **Local SEO best practice (full NAP/footer) can directly conflict with a client's design preference** ("clean footer, no visible nav") — a real, unresolved tension, not dismissed either way beyond "it should be on the site somewhere."
- **Even a practicing SEO can neglect their own site relative to client work** — several members running their own SEO business had glaring, uncorrected issues on their own site (the "cobbler's shoes" pattern) — expertise doesn't exempt anyone from the same mistakes reviewed on others' sites.
- **Location-page expansion is repeatedly framed as high-effort, high-payoff** — never presented as a quick win.
- **Disavow vs. dilute** — disavowing feels like the clean fix but is riskier; Matt has seen it tank rankings when a supposedly toxic link was actually contributing. Building more good links (dilution) is slower but doesn't risk losing hidden positive signal.
- **No-follow links aren't worthless** — they still carry brand credibility and AI-visibility value even without passing direct link equity.
- **National PR wire vs. local paper** — a national wire buys broad credibility/syndication reach; a local paper buys local-relevance signal that specifically helps local-pack SEO. Local businesses likely want both, not one instead of the other.
- **Behavioral (on-page) heat maps are usually unneeded overhead** for a typical local-business site — copying 1–2 good competitor/category sites' placement of phone/hours/address/CTA gets most of the benefit for free. They earn their cost only once you have real traffic and a genuine, unexplained conversion problem. Geo rank-heat-maps matter only if you specifically care about sub-area/neighborhood performance.
- **Portfolio/hub pages built to "showcase everything a company does"** create real tension between an owner's instinct and the SEO/conversion reality that users and Google both want one clear, specific answer — Matt sides with specificity but acknowledges the client-relationship cost of pushing back.
- **On-page rules can still fail intent-matching** — "H1 = the literal question" is still not enough if the SERP's dominant content type doesn't match your format; the intent check should override the generic formatting rule when they conflict.
- **Big-ticket B2B sales can still benefit from SEO/content discovery** even when internal stakeholders assume nobody buys via a website visit — the counter is that answering decision-makers' actual search questions drives them to the page, and the deal still closes through direct outreach, not self-serve checkout.
- **Not every flagged audit "error" needs action** — generic tools can't distinguish an intentionally noindexed/low-stakes page from a meaningful content gap; judgment is required before working down any tool's list, and a 90% health score is "fantastic" — stop chasing the remaining flags if they're on pages that were never going to rank anyway (e.g., duplicate H1s on category pages).
- **Fix vs. migrate a "link farm" domain** is worth it only if there's real traffic/rankings at stake; a domain with nothing to lose is simpler to abandon and rebuild fresh on a new URL.
- **The delete-old-content rule has an explicit exception** — legacy pages otherwise destined for deletion should be kept if they serve ongoing buyer intent (a sold-out show people still search for), handled via sold-out messaging/schema/notify-signup rather than a blanket delete.
- **Chasing a bigger, more contested market vs. defending/expanding a won one** — the "obvious" next step (a smaller nearby city → the much bigger regional hub) is framed as often the wrong move; smaller adjacent low-competition markets can be faster, easier wins than fighting for a much larger, much more contested keyword.
- **WordPress-native builder vs. an easier drag-and-drop platform** — the easier platform can be nicer to use/update long-term but sacrifices direct SEO/tag control; the WordPress-native option trades some ease-of-use for full technical control.
- **Schema markup is not confirmed as the deciding factor for AI-overview citation** — a heavily-schema'd, better-written #1-ranking page still got passed over by AI overviews in Matt's own test, suggesting extractable-answer formatting may matter more — but this is an inference from one observed case, not a proven rule.
- **Ranking (getting the click) and converting (getting the booking/sale) are separate problems** — keyword/intent match drives the former, on-page persuasion copy drives the latter; don't treat a conversion-focused rewrite as an automatic SEO fix, and don't assume a competitor who "converts better" is even winning on the same channel you're trying to win (verify their actual ranking and traffic source before copying them).
- **Comments cut both ways** — they can add keyword-relevant text/variation and engagement signal, but the realistic outcome on a small-business blog is spam; Matt's call is that it's not worth the risk, so disable them.
- **Category/blog-roll pages are built mostly "for Google" (crawlability), not for humans** — most real visitors land directly on a specific post from search rather than browsing the roll page, so don't over-invest in making that page a destination in itself.
- **H1-for-styling is normally an anti-pattern, but the rule bends when Google will never crawl the page as a search entry point** (Matt's own Rank Week landing page draws traffic only from Instagram/email).
- **Ubersuggest vs. Ahrefs/Semrush is a discovery-vs-accuracy tradeoff, not an either/or** — combine both: bulk-discover cheaply, then verify the shortlist on the pricier, more accurate tool.
- **Legal caution on client logos**: Matt declined to give a definitive yes/no on using client logos without explicit permission and deferred to a trademark lawyer rather than risk giving bad advice.
- **Patience vs. impatience**: a 2–3 month per-change wait and a 6-month overall commitment "might sound crazy," but the waiting period is framed as productive (continued publishing + internal linking), not passive downtime.
- **A real content differentiator can still lose to keyword-volume data** — a true, meaningful distinction (e.g., "private"/"custom" tours vs. group excursions) is a fine detail to keep in the copy/FAQ, but keyword research, not the differentiator itself, should dictate the primary target phrase.
- **New-page patience vs. a real structural problem can look identical at first** — a page only days old may simply need more time to index, but the same "not indexed" symptom can also mean a genuine defect (wrong content format, buried placement, cannibalization); diagnosing which one you're looking at matters more than reflexively "fixing" something.
- **Reddit self-promotion is a genuine tension** — posting your own link risks a spam flag (enforcement varies by subreddit, with the SEO subreddit called out as especially strict); workable paths are third-party/authentic posting, "give value first, reference later," or the unverified, not-endorsed option of paying for engagement via Fiverr.
- **Terminology choice can pit internal/brand clarity against actual search behavior** — a business preferring "eBook" for customer-clarity reasons still needed "PDF" in its copy because that's what people search, and even a partial fix didn't fully resolve customer confusion, showing the SEO-optimal term and the customer-clarity term can genuinely conflict.
- **A backlink's value is treated as partly dependent on geographic relevance** to the business — out-of-state/out-of-region links "definitely matter" differently than same-region ones, cutting against a one-size-fits-all "just get any backlink" approach.
- **Google's local map-pack UI may shift without notice** (one member reported reviews/star ratings missing from map-pack results, unconfirmed by anyone else on the call) — a reminder not to over-index on a single observation when local SERP features change.
- **Aged domains are a real ranking factor but explicitly downplayed relative to popular belief** — acknowledged as real, but "I don't think that has as big of an impact as people think it does."
- **A small competitor gap can produce an outsized win, while raw years-long effort without topical focus can still lose** — one local business won by simply being the only one to target a specific city name; a broad, years-old directory site with heavy sustained effort still lost meaningfully to a Google algorithm shift because it lacked topical focus.
- **Directory/aggregator-adjacent niches face a structural handicap against the directories themselves** — the fix is playing within that reality (get listed better on those directories) rather than trying to outrank them outright.
- **Retroactively added internal links still work but take longer to show effect than links present at publish time** — a timing tradeoff, not a lost cause.
- **Volume vs. value**: a business with two distinct market segments (e.g., commercial roofing worth far more per job vs. residential's higher volume/lower value) has to consciously choose which side to prioritize per page/channel rather than blur both into one hub.
- **DIY vs. agency**: traditional publishing routes (or, by extension, outsourcing to an agency generally) can offer better unit economics while still not delivering marketing results the principal couldn't have replicated themselves — a caution against assuming "professional" execution automatically beats DIY.
