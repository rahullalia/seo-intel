# Rank Week Synthesis (Matt Diamante, HeyTony)
*Source: 15 Rank Week transcripts across three live cohorts — Dec 2025 cohort (Day 1–5: calls 120/118/117/116/114), ~Mar 2026 cohort (Day 1–5: calls 077/075/074/073/071), Jun 2026 cohort (Day 1–5: calls 026/025/024/022/020). Same 5-day curriculum delivered three times; tools/pricing/prompts evolve between cohorts, core method is identical. Cohort tags below only where a fact is cohort-specific.*

## 1. Core Philosophy & Non-Negotiable Rules

- **SEO = answering real customer questions with real expertise.** The founding story, retold every cohort: Marcus, a pool-installation company owner near bankruptcy in 2008 with zero SEO knowledge, wrote down and answered the actual questions he heard on sales calls every night at the kitchen table after his kids slept → **#1 pool website in the world within 2 years**. "He was truly helpful. That is what is ranking now." Don't aim to be #1 in the world — aim to dominate your own market the same way.
- **One keyword per page, always — the single most-repeated rule in the entire curriculum.** "If you have the exact same keyword on two different pages, Google looks at them and goes, well, I don't know which page to rank. Maybe I'm not going to rank either of them." Never combine multiple distinct services onto one page. A phrase can be 3–4 words, but one phrase (or one small cluster) per page.
- **Every page is "a door into your business."** Build one solid door at a time rather than mass-publishing thin pages — "you don't want a million doors... you want to build one door at a time to make sure it's solid, that when somebody walks through it, it's not going to collapse on them."
- **"Pigs get fat and hogs get slaughtered"** (Matt's old accountant's line, repeated constantly across every cohort): never try to grab an entire market/region/keyword set at once. Take a slice, win it, then expand. Applied to: counties (Chris, custom home builder — pick 5 of 20-30), neighborhoods (Alex, DC-metro realtor — top 20 of ~200), local market size before going national (Skyler, AI automation), service×city page matrices (a ~200-page plan for 2 HVAC clients flagged as overreach — "do you want to shoot a shotgun and maybe miss your target, or shoot more of like a sniper rifle").
- **The #1 failure mode is quitting too early, not doing it wrong.** "Google does that on purpose because spammers come in... but then they give up, they go away." Google deliberately makes ranking slow specifically to filter out people chasing instant results. "The people who get results aren't the ones who learn the most in a single day or a single week. They're the ones who keep going afterwards."
- **Time budget: ~1 hour/week is enough.** The whole program is explicitly engineered against a 5–20 hour/week SEO habit — "I'm trying to make SEO so simple that anybody could do it in an hour a week." Time-commitment objections are reframed: "do you have one hour a week?" — even minimal, consistent participation outperforms 99% of businesses that do nothing.
- **Focus on the 20% of effort that drives 80% of results; explicitly skip the rest.** Deprioritized as low-ROI by Matt's own repeated statement: schema markup, FAQ schema (after "redoing our site for a year" without them — "I haven't found that they're any more or less useful"), messy/inconsistent HTML markup, "AI blocks" (content chunks marketed as gaming AI visibility — "we haven't found that that does anything, at least not yet"), chasing an audit tool's overall percentage/score, AI-detection tools on your own content.
- **Never edit a page that's already ranking #1–#3 — the single most emphatic rule in the Day 2/3 material.** "Don't edit any pages that are ranking, for the love of God" — even if the page's SEO looks objectively bad. You can't rank higher than #1, so touching it is pure downside risk (losing the spot) with no upside. If it's #1 but not converting: tiny incremental edits only (one sentence, one button) — never a full rewrite. Wait for rankings to actually drop before touching anything larger.
- **AI is a tool, never a brain-replacement.** "We're using AI as a tool and not as a replacement for your brain... you still have to go through, fact check, make sure that everything's accurate." Google and AI do **not** penalize AI-written content — they penalize regurgitated, generic, same-as-everyone-else content. "Google and AI hate AI written posts. They do not. They hate regurgitated information that's the same that everybody else is saying over and over and over again." What avoids the penalty: injecting your own real expertise/experience/opinions/stories via the interview-prompt method (see §2).
- **Never chase "humanizing" your content or run an AI-detector on your own writing** — philosophically contradictory ("using AI to look at AI... doesn't make any sense") and pointless. "We don't want to humanize our content. We want to write human content and have AI just take our words and frame it in a way that is coherent... Every piece of content you should have should be from you. It shouldn't just be random, like AI slop."
- **AEO/GEO is not a separate discipline — "it's all just SEO," "everything we're doing is SEO, which AEO is part of SEO."** Practical translation: rank on Google first (LLMs pull from existing search-engine results rather than crawling fresh each time — cited via Edward Sturm's public IG/TikTok breakdowns of what ChatGPT search actually does), add FAQs, structure content in answer/Q&A format. "If you rank on Google, your odds of being recommended in the AI results are a lot higher."
- **Off-page authority is broader than backlinks now.** "Yes, backlinks are really great, but LLMs want to see you as a professional or as an expert... it's grabbing your expertise from all these different places and kind of building a profile for you" — guest posts (which double as backlinks), podcast appearances, being mentioned elsewhere all feed into how Google/AI judge authority, on top of raw backlink count.
- **Vibe-coded / AI-built sites can rank fine.** Matt's own vibe-coded site ranks **#1–#2 for "backlink gap"** — built by literally telling Claude "put this on the Internet," which walked him through GitHub + hosting signup with no special SEO structuring needed.
- **"Would my customers actually need this? And read this? If the answer is yes, then publish it."** — the core litmus test for whether any piece of content is worth writing, used repeatedly to settle "should I blog about X" questions (news/announcements, pre-written backlogs, niche topics).

## 2. Day 1 — First Blog Post via the AI-Interview Method

**Step-by-step (fullest combined version across all 3 cohorts):**
1. Find a real customer question. Go to a question-finder tool (see §14) and type **one thing only** — what you sell (e.g., "wedding photographer," "plumber," "hot sauce"). Typing more than one query during a live session dumps you into a shared processing queue and slows/breaks results for everyone else. Deliberately go to **page 4 or 5 of results, not page 1** — everyone else fights over page-1 keywords (Cohort A/Dec2025 framing). If you already collected questions from a live sales call or tech-check homework, skip the tool and use those directly.
2. Open a **brand-new** chat in ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini/any LLM (any model works interchangeably for this). Paste the interview prompt (Prompt 1, exact wording in §13) with your question substituted in.
3. Answer the AI's clarifying questions **one at a time**, in the same thread — voice mode is faster/more natural than typing (Matt's stated preference), 2–3 sentences per answer is enough as long as each reflects genuine unique experience/opinion, not exhaustive detail.
4. Once all questions are answered, run the write-the-post prompt (Prompt 2, exact wording in §13) in the **same thread** so it can reference the interview context.
5. Copy the entire draft into a blank Google Doc/Word doc — **never build directly in the CMS yet**.
6. **Clean-up pass**, line by line — remove: AI-style horizontal/vertical bar line-breaks, the stray leading space before a line's first word (an AI "tell"), numbered-list structure (unless the post is genuinely meant to be a listicle), emojis, checkmarks. Em dashes are explicitly fine to leave in per Matt's own instruction (note: this is Matt's stated position, not a universal writing rule).
7. **Title the post as the literal question itself** — tighten only if the AI's version is generic (live example: "SEO when building a website" → "SEO when building a website, getting it right from day one"). Keep the title near **60 characters, ideally under ~55** or it gets cut off on Google/mobile.
8. **Internal-link the new post**: highlight a 2–3-word phrase that describes the destination page's topic (doesn't need exact match, just needs to signal the target page's subject) and hyperlink it. Ratio for a brand-new post/site with limited existing content: **2–3 links to other blog posts + 1 to a product/service (money) page** (this is the Day-1 baseline; the mature steady-state rule taught on Day 4 scales to 3–5 — see §5). If the exact anchor phrase doesn't appear yet, write a short bridging sentence that contains it (live example: added "We have a full breakdown on how much does SEO cost at Hey Tony" specifically to create that anchor text). If you have zero other blog posts yet, use the money page + homepage as your only 2 links. To learn your specific CMS's mechanics, ask the AI directly: *"How do I add a link to a blog post or to another page on my site from highlighted words?"* then name your platform (WordPress/Shopify/Wix/Squarespace/etc.) — asking the AI to insert the links itself "doesn't always work," which is why the manual highlight-and-link method is taught.
9. **Images**: add one featured image + roughly one in-body image every **200–300 words** — "people are lazy... people don't like just reading giant blocks of text." Rename the actual image file to something descriptive before uploading (e.g. "west-african-wedding-ceremony-toronto" instead of the camera's default filename) and set **alt text** to the same descriptive phrase, ~5–6 words, simple ("cat photography pose") — precision here is low-stakes.
10. Generate the meta description (Prompt 3, §13) in the same thread.
11. Optional: generate a cover image (Prompt 4, §13) — "in the style of" an attached reference image (a photo already on your own site, or a style from anywhere else); optionally attach brand guidelines.
12. **Publish.** Works identically on WordPress, Shopify, Wix, Squarespace, Duda, Framer, Canva, custom HTML, "Showit" — does **not** work well for Substack per Matt. No blog section yet? Create one first — every major builder supports a native blog.
13. **Close the loop**: go back into **3 older, already-indexed posts** and add a link from each of them into the brand-new post — the inbound half of internal linking, so the new page has both outbound and inbound links from day one.

**Rules & thresholds:**
- Cap the interview prompt's questions at **5–10** (Cohort A/Dec2025 explicitly capped at 10 — "Keep it limited to 10 questions" — after Matt ran it uncapped once and got ~30, "way too much"; Cohort B/Mar2026 and Cohort C/Jun2026 both use 5).
- Blog-post cadence ceiling, repeated in every single session across all cohorts: **1–2 posts/week, max.** Matt's own agency: "one blog post a week or two blog posts a week. That's the max we're going to do" for most clients (2/week reserved for top-paying clients); elsewhere cited as **4–8 pieces every 28 days** for agency clients. Daily/365-a-year publishing is explicitly "too much" — risks Google reading the site as AI-spam churn rather than genuine content.
- Zero/low measured search volume on the target question is **fine and expected** for this exercise — these are long-tail, roughly 5–10 searches/month, which is exactly what makes them easy to rank. Article length and keyword-repetition count **don't matter** for this specific exercise.
- Not required for Day 1: heading-tag knowledge, title-tag/meta-description mechanics, Core Web Vitals — "Does a doctor... or somebody with a PhD in rocket science know what a title tag is or a meta description? No... You don't need to know all this stuff." (Helps "a little bit," reserved for later days.)
- Never paste raw AI output straight onto the live site — always stage it in a separate doc first. Don't over-link a repeated word/phrase — if a term appears multiple times in the post, link it only **once**.
- Squeeze/landing pages (pure conversion pages) should stay single-purpose: don't add the blog to that page's nav, don't link out from it to the homepage or blog — the one-job page shouldn't be diluted, but the blog can still exist elsewhere on the site.
- Podcast-based content: don't link directly to the podcast episode as an internal link (low search volume for episode lookups unless you're Alex-Hormozi/Gary-Vee-tier fame). Instead, feed the transcript into the LLM with an instruction like *"remember this for the future when I'm writing a blog post — this is from a podcast episode I did"* — trains the AI to pull your real stories/voice into future posts.
- Publishing-backlog management: don't launch a large pre-written backlog all at once. Launch with **10** posts, drip the rest at **2/week** — signals active maintenance to Google and buys a 2–3-month buffer against a missed week.
- Content-type-by-intent: if someone would Google the topic for *information*, write a blog post; if they'd Google it to *hire someone*, write a service page — same topic, different content, don't blend the two.
- Announcements/company news: don't force them into the blog stream unless people are actively *searching* for that info — put quick announcements on social media instead; only blog an announcement if it can be reframed as genuinely searchable (e.g. "brand new patio opens in [area]" rather than "we built a patio").
- B2B/high-end niches: write for the junior staff who search on behalf of the actual decision-maker (assistants, coordinators), not only the exec — juniors get promoted and remember you.
- Reviews as content: a review-screenshot image is fine for visual trust but Google cannot read text inside an image — embed/copy the actual review text as real text if you want its words to carry SEO weight. AI-drafted review replies are fine, review before publishing, don't auto-post. Cross-post the blog link to LinkedIn/Facebook — doesn't move rankings directly but signals "legitimate business" to Google.
- Local-traffic-from-anywhere: even hyper-local businesses should keep broader (non-locally-relevant) content up — non-local visits still send engagement signals (time on site, page visits, form fills) that build domain authority Google uses when ranking you for local/transactional terms. Close the loop with an embedded local CTA inside the content itself (e.g. "looking for a wedding photographer in Hamilton? Give us a call," with "wedding photographer Hamilton" linked to the booking page).
- No-site-control situations (franchisee with no control over the core site): redirect effort to earning placement on third-party "best of" lists and pitching local media/radio who publish them — they effectively do the SEO/authority-building for you.
- **"Avalanche technique" / "snowball effect"** (full version taught inside Insiders): keep stacking small, zero-competition, near-zero-volume question posts, all interlinked. Individually they barely move traffic, but the accumulated topical authority is what eventually unlocks the harder, higher-volume head keywords the site couldn't touch directly at the start. "It's called the snowball effect... all of these small keywords bundle up, get bigger, start rolling down, collecting more and more keywords."
- Any language works — the prompts/process aren't English-only (Matt just personally works in English). Any LLM is acceptable: ChatGPT (free or paid), Claude, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity. Upgrading to ChatGPT's paid tier (~$20/month) roughly doubled Matt's own output when he hit free-tier limits.
- Client/agency onboarding tactic: run a 30–60 min interview call with a new client ("tell me about this, tell me about this"), capture the transcript, feed it into a reference doc an LLM can pull from when writing future posts in the client's voice — or build a standing "brand book/story guide" doc during onboarding.

## 3. Day 2 — Money Page Definition & Competitive Audit

**Money page = any page a visitor lands on when ready to buy/hire** — a product or service page, not a contact/booking/appointment page ("those pages don't rank," and a third-party booking tool is explicitly **not** your money page even though your money page is allowed to link out to one). Homepage counts if there's no dedicated page. If stuck, ask the AI directly: *"I am a realtor, what would be my money page?"*

Examples across all cohorts: dentist → dental implants/Invisalign page; wedding photographer → luxury wedding photography packages; realtor → "homes for sale in Scottsdale, Arizona" / "homes for sale in Virginia Beach"; hat store → baseball hats / a collection / a specific product; hot sauce co. → "buy ghost peppers hot sauce" / "Carolina reaper hot sauce"; plumber → 24-hour emergency page; personal trainer → "personal trainer in Vancouver"; lawyer → car accident lawyer page; garage door co. → "garage door installation [city]"; ecommerce → shop/collection/product page; psychotherapist → "couples counseling" or "PTSD therapy" (never a generic "therapy" page); magician → one page per event type.

**5-step audit workflow (pageaudit.com), homework due before the next session — do it for your own page + top-3 competitors:**
1. Copy the shared audit spreadsheet (Google Sheet "make a copy" link, or CSV download if no Google login). Enter your money-page URL + your **focus keyword** (one keyword — e.g. "Hamilton Electrician," "Movers Queen West," never a generic "Movers Toronto").
2. **pageaudit.com** → paste URL + keyword → Analyze. **Ignore the overall score/percentage entirely** — irrelevant to this exercise (Matt's own site and the #1-ranked "Stack Electric" for "Hamilton Electrician" both scored 76%; a brand-new site with nothing built out scored 38%).
3. **Content tab**: copy word count, keyword occurrences, and keyword density (%) into the sheet.
4. **Metadata tab**: copy the meta title (title tag) and meta description.
5. **Chrome extension** (search "Add to Chrome" on pageaudit.com, "PA" icon) — fallback when a page returns all "NA" (usually bot-blocked): surfaces title tag, meta description, H1/H2/H3 tags (use its "Copy All Headings" button, then manually sort into columns), word count, keyword occurrence/density once you type the keyword in. If it doesn't populate: wait ~30 seconds or scroll to the bottom then back to top (lazy-load issue).
6. **pagespeed.web.dev**: run on the money-page URL itself (not the homepage, unless that IS your money page) — record mobile + desktop scores.
7. **Repeat identically for your top 3 organic competitors**: Google the exact focus keyword in an incognito window, skip sponsored/ad results, skip Reddit, skip Yelp/IG, skip directories/aggregators — take the next 3 genuine ranking business sites. Run each through the same 5 steps into the same spreadsheet, side by side with your own row.
8. Do **not** rewrite the page yet — this audit is prep for Day 3, where the group uses this data plus an LLM prompt to actually rewrite the copy.

**Rules & thresholds:**
- **Never use "near me" as a target keyword.** Google already knows searcher + business location and connects them automatically — targeting "near me" explicitly is "the sign of somebody who doesn't know how to do SEO."
- **One H1 tag per page, period.** If everything is currently tagged H1, that's a problem to flag now, fix comes the next day. Multiple H1s usually means a designer used H1 for visual styling, not semantic structure — signals to Google the page covers "a whole bunch of different topics." Minimum 2–3 H2s recommended; H3s optional, only worth digging into once everything else is already optimized.
- **No universal "ideal" word count, keyword count, or density exists.** The only real benchmark is whatever is *currently* ranking #1–#2 for that specific keyword — and that benchmark moves as the competitive set changes (Matt's own page held #1 for 2 years at 600–700 words, later lost to competitors running 2,300–2,500 words on the same term after he'd dropped to #9).
- **Density red flag**: if your keyword density sits meaningfully higher than your competitors', you're almost certainly over-optimized. Matt's own case: 5.04% density (keyword used 35 times) vs. competitors' 1.76% and 0.9% — nearly 5x higher — correlated with his page dropping from #1 to #9.
- **Absurd density readings (600%+, "923.44%") mean user error**, not a real signal — the keyword field was left blank and the tool defaulted to the domain name. Fix the input, don't trust the number. Word count will differ slightly between pageaudit.com and its extension — treat anything within ~100–200 words as close enough (one case: an inactive/unused plugin was silently adding 2,000 words to every page's count).
- **Match competitors' exact keyword phrasing/variant, not a close synonym.** Matt lost visibility for "website design" while both competitors beating him use "web design" — check the literal words competitors use in title/meta/H1 before assuming your own phrasing is "the" keyword.
- **Never use gray-hat/sneaky tricks** — the explicit cause Matt cites for his own page's ranking drop: duplicating the same H1 text once for mobile display and once for desktop (hidden via responsive CSS), and keyword text visible only on hover ("Google does not like this"). Consequence isn't necessarily de-indexing, but "you won't get results." Also don't copy competitors' actual title tags/meta descriptions verbatim — study the pattern, write your own.
- **Name products/pages what real searchers actually type, not invented brand language.** Beaver-fur hatmaker case: renamed away from a trendy invented product name to "beaver fur hat" / "beaver fur top hat" after keyword research showed 260 searches/month in Canada, 880/month in the US. Bong-shop case (secondhand story): a product filed only under generic "novelty bongs" was renamed "octopus bong" after research showed people search the specific term.
- **Wait 3–6 months** (some cohorts say 3–6–9) after making changes before re-auditing/re-checking — give Google time to process; keep publishing linked blog posts in the meantime.
- **No crawl-budget cap or "domain age" penalty exists for new domains** — an active myth to debunk, not just downplay: "there's no 100% rules"; proof cited — a domain Matt registered in 2014 went from 0 to 4 million monthly visitors (Google + Facebook combined) within 6 months.
- **Bad backlinks**: might have a small negative effect, but "likely not impacting it at all" — focus on content first. Ignore pingback notifications entirely, don't approve them (they can appear even on sites with no visible comment section).
- **Location architecture**: one keyword = one page, always. Single-area business → keyword = service + city. Multi-area business → build location pages, start with **5**, add more over time rather than trying to rank every territory at once (respect franchise-legal boundaries — never cross into a fellow franchisee's owned market). Multiple distinct service types (e.g. wedding photography vs. portrait painting) → separate dedicated page per type; a general overview page can mention all and link out to each, but never blend keyword-topics onto one page. Multi-location businesses under one brand (dental clinics, franchises) → favor location-specific pages, or even separate sites, over one shared generic page.
- **Zero-volume keyword-tool readings for local long-tail terms don't mean skip it** — tools are estimates; low/no reported volume often just means the exact phrase variation hasn't been picked up yet, or real searches are bucketed under generic "near me" queries instead (which you still shouldn't target directly).
- **Blog content should stay generalized, not hyper-localized to one narrow reader**, even when an AI draft naturally leans specific (case: Sarah Hodge's ChatGPT-drafted guide auto-tailored to her exact region after she answered clarifying questions — Matt's read: pull back toward general applicability for ranking purposes) — "go as general as the question requires, or as specific as the question requires."
- **Shopify: use the native blog, skip third-party blogging apps.** WordPress confirmed fine after 15 years' industry use; Yoast vs. Rank Math — "it honestly doesn't make a difference" which you use.
- **Heavily regulated/saturated keyword ("EMF protection," ~6,000 searches/month)**: stop fighting the head term directly. Write supporting content around adjacent questions a concerned-but-not-keyword-literate searcher would type instead ("Is WiFi dangerous for you?", "Does EMF cause cancer?") — you don't need the flagged/technical term at all. (This attendee, Tim, wrote ~60 posts, 58 about EMF protection, none ranking — the only 2 that ranked were on unrelated topics; separately got investigated by the UK Advertising Standards Authority over a "completely neutral vanilla" Facebook ad and told to remove the word "protection" from his site.)
- **Directory competition**: if directories/aggregators outrank you, get listed on them for free rather than trying to out-content them — sends a legitimacy/association signal ("the site ranking #1/#2 is linking to this business too").
- **Rank in ChatGPT/AI tools by ranking on Google first** — LLMs query existing search engines and pull from those results rather than scanning the live web fresh each time.

## 4. Day 3 — Competitor Gap Analysis & Money Page Rewrite

**Core exercise (base version, Cohorts A/B):**
1. Open your Day-2 audit spreadsheet and a fresh ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini/Grok conversation.
2. Grab the exact competitor-gap-analysis prompt from the designated prompts page (heytony.ca/prompts, or heytony.ca/rank-week-prompts/) — described every cohort as too long to hand-copy or dictate aloud; screenshot/save it, since the page comes down after Rank Week ends. (Exact wording of this specific prompt was never read aloud/quoted in any transcript — only its input/output behavior was demonstrated live.)
3. Paste the prompt first, **don't press enter yet**.
4. Paste your entire audit spreadsheet (your data + all competitor columns) under the prompt's placeholder.
5. Press enter. Output analyzes across up to **8 dimensions**: title tag, meta description, H1, H2/H3 structure, word count, keyword density, page speed, trust signals — each meaningful gap gets an exact fix with a priority flag (🔴 do this first / 🟡 this week / 🟢 nice to have), ending in a **3-bullet Quick Wins summary**.
6. **Challenge vague or self-serving AI claims.** If it says "your page is the strongest commercial page," push back: "then why aren't I ranking #1?" Cross-check every suggestion against the real SERP — especially the **lowest-ranked of the top 3**, not the highest (if #3 ranks with 500 words, aim for ~600, not 3,000 just because #1 has 3,000). Don't trust an LLM's *guess* at who your competitors are — verify by Googling the actual keyword ("ChatGPT will bullshit you. It'll lie to you").
7. Today's one task: fix your money page — title tag, meta description, H1 first ("most of this takes 5–10 minutes").
8. Core theory, works manually too: whatever your #1 competitor is doing, Google likes — mirror it, then slightly outdo it. Don't just add more (words, keyword repeats); over-optimizing past the competitor who outranks you can suppress rankings. "More keywords does not equal better rankings." "Higher density does not equal better rankings. Google increasingly interprets heavy keyword repetition as over optimization."
9. Page-speed fixes if slow: next-gen image formats, shrink/compress image file size (same on-screen dimensions), remove unused scripts, lazy-load images, or just ask the same LLM thread "how do I do this?"

**Cohort C's (Jun 2026) more advanced 2-prompt version — full workflow:**
1. Copy Day-2 audit columns A/B (your title tag, meta description, H1, H2s, keyword density) exactly as-is into **Prompt 1** (exact text, §13) — messy formatting is fine, the AI parses it correctly.
2. Paste competitor #1's column, then competitor #2's column, same row order, under the labeled placeholders.
3. Press enter — returns an updated title tag, meta description, H1, and updated H2 tags.
4. Copy the output into a **separate blank document**, not just inside the chat.
5. Manually review: confirm the keyword is present and front-loaded; swap in a better-testing semantic variant if Day-2 data showed Google prefers it (e.g. "web design" over "website design").
6. Take the new H2s, paste under a "content outline" heading, strip labels/bold formatting, rewrite any that don't sound like something a real person would say.
7. For the full rewrite, use **Prompt 2** (exact text, §13): select the entire existing page's content and paste it in; for each competitor, try pasting just their URL first (ChatGPT can sometimes read the live page directly — "sometimes it works, sometimes it doesn't"), falling back to pasting full competitor content if it fails.
8. Set the word-count range from competitor data (e.g. competitors at ~2,500 and ~2,300 words → target ~2,200).
9. Answer any clarifying question the AI asks first (e.g., keep current structure vs. rebuild vs. a third option).
10. **Never trust the AI's self-reported word count or keyword count** — verify manually every time. (Live demo: targeted 2,200 words, delivered only ~1,700 per wordcounter.net; targeted a 20–45 keyword-occurrence band, delivered only 13.)
11. Paste the output into **wordcounter.net** to check real word count and keyword occurrence/density; land in the same band as competitors, not above or below.
12. Hand-edit: confirm it accurately describes your real process/product/service, remove artificial numbered-list phrasing unless genuinely a list, fold in real case studies/experience (existing on-page case studies carry over automatically since the source content was pasted in).
13. Refine one section at a time by pasting just that section back into the same thread: *"can you rewrite this section to be more funny and personable"*; to restore skipped headings: *"you forgot these heading two tags, write a couple paragraphs each and expand on the ideas"*; to deepen a section: paste it plus *"ask me five questions to write a better unique piece of content using my expertise, experience, etc."* — answer by typing or voice, then have it rewrite.
14. Re-run the page-audit tool on the new draft before publishing to recheck density/word count.
15. Matt frames this 2-prompt process as a fast starting point, not the full method — blending it with Day 1's "ask me clarifying questions first" technique produces stronger results than treating this output as final.

**Money Page Doctor** (new bonus tool, Cohort B/Mar2026, Insiders-exclusive that week): paste in your homepage/money-page URL; it crawls the page, infers the correct target keyword (double-checking against on-page content), identifies competitors, checks their title/H1/density usage of that keyword, flags gaps ("keyword mismatch: not found on competitor pages, competitors appear to target [X] instead," "content is thin, consider adding more words," "keyword density too low/high," "blog post detected — competitors ranking are dedicated service pages, consider building one"). Shows a "congratulations, you're already ranking #1" banner where applicable. ~10 seconds per site; not run live for everyone due to processing cost.

**Rules & thresholds:**
- **Never edit pages ranking #1/#2/#3** — repeated even more emphatically than Day 2 ("don't edit any pages that are ranking, for the love of God"). If #1 but not converting: small incremental edits only (a sentence, a button), never a full rewrite at once.
- **Money page = the page that sends a visitor TO a contact/intake form.** The form itself is not the money page.
- Video is **not** required to rank. Weigh a hero autoplay video's visual appeal against page speed/mobile cost: "is that video appealing to you, or is it appealing to a person looking to hire you?"
- **FAQ schema deliberately deprioritized** — Matt's own year-long test found no measurable difference either way.
- Messy/inconsistent HTML markup (div IDs, stray data attributes) doesn't meaningfully matter — "most websites, the source code, it's just a disaster in the back."
- **No platform is inherently better/worse** (Wix, Squarespace, Shopify, WordPress) provided you have unique URLs, a sitemap, and good content — WordPress just has more SEO features built in by default, though many sites overuse default tags and create bloated, non-indexable pages that waste crawl budget.
- **Google Business Profile requires a physical location** — fully virtual/online-only businesses cannot register one. A second GBP via a subleased office in a target city is explicitly flagged as against the rules, even though Matt names it as something people do.
- Ranking for an **adjacent keyword** (e.g. a photographer ranking on "wedding venue" searches) is fine, not a conflict — capture it via a sidebar/popup/downloadable guide rather than fighting it, as long as your real service is clearly stated elsewhere on the site.
- **Evergreen pricing pages**: never put the year in the URL — keep the URL static, update on-page pricing/content in place each year; changing the URL forces Google to re-index from scratch.
- **Site redesign/URL cleanup rule**: identify pages that are ranking, getting traffic, or have backlinks — leave those exact URLs alone. Everything else can change, but must get a 301 redirect to the right page and have internal links updated to stop pointing at the old URL. Double-folder/duplicate-path URLs from an old migration = a form of keyword stuffing in the URL — clean up only on pages that aren't already ranking.
- **Expanding to a new/distant city** (~3 hours away, different market): start a **separate website** with its own Google Business Profile rather than folding it into the existing brand — unless you already have name recognition there.
- **Multi-line-of-business on one site** (finished goods + materials + a course) is fine as long as the navigation makes the visitor's path unambiguous.
- **Non-English sites**: the same framework applies without reading the language — check keyword placement (title, H1, meta, content), CTA clarity, info density. Running both a native-language and English version of a page can rank for the same keyword twice and pick up incidental cross-border/traveler traffic.
- **Reading level: 7th–8th grade throughout** ("if you can't explain it to a kid, you can't explain it") — the explicit model for Matt's own book "Get Found."
- **Blog cadence**: minimum 1/week, scale to 2–3/week only if genuinely sustainable — 4-month gaps between posts are worse than a lower, steady cadence.
- **JS-rendered pages** (React + Tailwind, etc.) that don't auto-emit semantic H1/H2: get the framework/AI coding tool to output real heading elements, then restyle via CSS — don't fake headings with styled divs.
- **Suspect/underperforming purchased domain** with no visible red flags after a year of legitimate work: register a brand-new domain, move the same content over (case: started ranking within a month after the swap — full case in §15).
- **Baseline WordPress stack**: WordPress + the **Avada** theme ("the number one WordPress theme of all time" / Matt's own go-to); add plugins one at a time as the specific need arises, no fixed a-priori list.
- Insiders pricing: **$57/month, month-to-month, cancel any time** — no lock-in commitment (consistent across all cohorts).

## 5. Day 4 — Internal Linking & Crawl Depth

**Crawl-depth model** (combined clearest version): **0 clicks** = homepage. **1 click** = anything linked directly from the homepage/nav (services, blog index, a homepage "recent posts" module). **2 clicks** = e.g. Blog(1) → a specific post(2), or Services(1) → a specific service(2). **3 clicks** = a page linked from that post/service (e.g. "next page" of a paginated blog archive). **4+ clicks = danger zone** — Google is much less likely to find/care about the page, precisely because it's hard for a *human* to stumble onto it too. Rule of thumb: **get every page you want ranked to 3 clicks or fewer from the homepage.** "The harder it is for a human to find your page, the less likely Google will actually care about it. And if Google doesn't care, you don't rank, you don't get customers, you don't make money and you can't pay your bills."

**Crawl-depth shortcuts:**
- Use blog **posts**, not static pages — posts auto-populate the blog roll AND auto-sort into category archive pages. Clicking a category tag from the homepage (1 click) loads every post in that category (2 clicks total) for an entire topic cluster — collapsing what would otherwise be a 3–4-click paginated-archive path.
- Add homepage modules that shorten crawl depth: a services bar/module, a "recent posts" module, links to category pages — all become 1-click from the homepage.
- Local businesses: link to your Google Business Profile from the homepage or footer (Matt's own site does this via an embedded map showing the GBP + service area).
- If "Blog" isn't in your nav menu, add it today. Keep the label as **"Blog"** — don't rename it for branding polish (e.g. "Learn Hub"); zero SEO benefit, pure clarity cost to visitors.

**Live internal-linking exercise (combined fullest version, ~4 minutes on the call):**
1. Open your money page and the blog post from Monday in separate tabs (published link, or the Google Doc if unpublished).
2. Open ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini — model doesn't matter.
3. Go to the designated crawl-prompt page (Cohort B: `heytony.ca/rank-week-crawl-prompt/`; Cohort C: linked from the "hey Tony CA prompts" page) and copy the prompt (again, too long to hand-copy or quote verbatim — screenshot/save it).
4. Paste the prompt; where indicated, paste the blog-post text (or Doc content) and the money-page URL/description underneath.
5. Press enter. Output returns, numbered: internal linking opportunity, recommended anchor text, why this link makes sense, where in the post to add it, the original paragraph, and a rewritten version of that paragraph with the link already woven in — plus an optional alternate CTA.
6. Copy the rewritten paragraph back into the post (doc, CMS editor, or live site) and save/update.
- Can be applied retroactively to old posts too. Claimed result of doing both linking rules consistently: "we've had pages index within an hour to two hours" of publishing.

**Cohort C's sitemap-based variant** (same underlying rule, different mechanism):
1. List your **5 money pages** in a spreadsheet column.
2. Locate your sitemap, usually `yourwebsite.com/sitemap.xml` (WordPress+Yoast, Wix, and Shopify all auto-generate one; a 404 means something is broken, fix it first). If it's an index page (`sitemap_index.xml`), find the sub-sitemap specifically for blog posts (often `post-sitemap.xml`).
3. Paste the internal-linking prompt (exact text below), replacing the bracket with your money-page URL, and paste the sitemap content underneath **as-is**, no cleanup needed.
4. Take the 3 recommended URLs, paste them next to that money page.
5. Go to each of those 3 pages on your live site and add a link back to the money page, using your own judgment — confirm contextual fit, and check you aren't already linking to that page from that post.
6. Repeat for the remaining money pages; reuse the same thread ("use the sitemap previously provided" + the next money-page link) rather than re-pasting the sitemap each time.
7. If the output lacks detail (no reasoning, no anchor text, no example sentence), reprompt: ask for "why it's a good fit," "suggested anchor text," and an "example sentence" for each link.

**Matt's Internal Linking Rules — "your entire internal linking strategy, done" (near-identical wording across all 3 cohorts' Day-4 sessions):**
- **Rule 1**: every blog post links out to **3–5 other blog posts + 1 money page** (scale down to 3 other-post links if the post is short). "Every blog post should link to three to five other blog posts and one money page."
- **Rule 2**: every time you publish a new post, go back and add a link into it from **3 older posts** (different ones than what the new post itself links to) — so the new post has inbound links from day one. "Every time you publish a new blog post, link to it from three older blog posts."
- "This is real SEO that you guys just did... This is something that experts even forget to do... They'll publish content, they'll get backlinks, but they won't do internal linking, which is massive."

**Other Day-4 rules & thresholds:**
- General link-count ceiling per page: **10–20 links** (or 5–10 links fine at ~1,000+ words, depending on cohort framing).
- **Vary anchor text** across links pointing at the same money page (Matt's own example set: "our SEO services," "done-for-you SEO services," "professional SEO services") — repeating the exact same anchor every time reads as manipulation to Google.
- Don't copy a competitor's blog content to link into your own site — Google already indexed it under them; you get flagged as duplicate content (it likely won't hurt them, since they posted first).
- Automated internal-linking plugins (named: **Internal Linking Juicer**) are fine to use ("you absolutely can"), but Matt avoids relying on them himself — there's no human judgment behind the automation, so it can add a redundant link to a page already linked for that same keyword.
- **WordPress category hygiene**: **5–10 categories max, one category per post.** Tags: **no** — stated flatly three separate times in one call: "The only thing I do on blog posts is I'll add category... but I'm not going to add any tags."
- **Custom post types** (WordPress, for sites with news/blog/case studies): limit to **2–3 total** — one for case studies, one for services, let the regular Post type cover the blog. Case studies specifically should be styled/treated as **pages**, not standard posts.
- **Orphan pages** (zero internal links pointing in) are fine **only when deliberate** (lead-magnet quizzes, job postings, ad landing pages, internal-only pages) — pair with a real no-index tag. Anything you want ranked must never be orphaned. "This is a page that has been taken out back behind the barn and shot and forgot about."
- Don't hide/bury money pages or blog links out of embarrassment — "if you hide things from the user, you're hiding them from Google."
- Dropdown/mega menus don't add crawl-depth clicks themselves ("it's when you leave that page" that counts) — but overstuffed dropdowns hurt usability, especially mobile (~50–60% of all internet traffic).
- Plurals (e.g. "broker" vs. "brokers") don't matter to Google — use naturally. Location pages: always **subfolders** (`site.com/irvine`), never subdomains (`irvine.site.com`).
- Bad/AI-junk indexed posts that aren't ranking: **301-redirect** if a relevant replacement exists, otherwise let it **404**.
- **No evidence AI-written content is penalized**, and no evidence specific "flagged" words (wellness/spiritual/alt-health niches) get filtered/suppressed — Matt has never heard this from any SEO expert he knows.
- A blog CTA is fine ("get a free quote") but understand blog intent = research, money-page intent = ready-to-buy. A single business CAN and should run **multiple money pages** (one per service/offer), each pointing to the same lead-capture form if that's the funnel.
- **Cancel any tool that pushes daily rank-change alerts** — "that is just giving you anxiety"; check monthly averages instead. Day-to-day rank bouncing is normal noise, not a signal to act on. If a competitor holds #1 steadily, that's not always a fixable discrete problem — "sometimes Google thinks this is the number one website and nobody else has earned that spot yet."
- **Setting a page to no-index** (Yoast/Rank Math, under "Advanced") is the correct way to keep it out of Google — don't rely on lack-of-internal-links alone (a hidden page can still surface, e.g. in an ego-search). **robots.txt** can also block pages, but Matt does not recommend most people hand-edit it — easy to accidentally block the entire site.
- **Local SEO expansion into a new market (3-step)**: (1) Google Business Profile in each new city, fill every field, start collecting reviews; (2) a dedicated location landing page per area served; (3) local press/backlinks, partner with local businesses, join local associations (Chamber of Commerce, Rotary Club), consider sponsoring a local sports team.
- **Multi-language SEO**: redo keyword research **natively in the target language** — don't just machine-translate the English keyword, since the real search phrase may differ from a literal translation.
- **Lead magnet on a money page**: add a short quiz/checklist giving away something of value (e.g. the author's own book) in exchange for contact info — captures leads and creates an on-page conversion signal.
- **Slow homepage from embedded catalogs**: stop embedding the catalog directly — show a cover image linking out to the catalog as its own page.
- **Reverse-engineering a competitor's odd on-page pattern** when you're not ranking at all (e.g. a competitor using 2 H1 tags): copy that specific structural pattern since you have nothing to lose — but keep the actual text/copy unique.
- **Consolidating a bloated flat catalog** during a migration (~1,000 near-duplicate steel-product variant pages): option = 302-redirect variants down to 1–2 consolidated pages. Preserve any page already ranking/getting traffic exactly as-is; for the rest, run keyword research to find which specific size/variant people actually search and build individual pages only for those, using category pages/filters for everything else.
- **Deciding whether to delete old blog content**: check GSC + Analytics — not indexed + no traffic + no backlinks = safe to delete. Backlinks present, or genuine reuse value (e.g. a recurring seasonal tour), = keep or update instead.
- **Deciding whether to redirect a ranking post into a category page**: check first whether that post's ranking is backlink-supported — if so, expect to lose some backlink value on the redirect; workable, not a clean win.
- **Diagnosing inflated keyword density caused by on-page reviews**: mentally exclude review text when comparing your density to competitors (keep reviews live for conversion). Note: running an AI tool over a product description to shorten it for readability can unintentionally push density even higher — recheck density after any AI rewrite.
- **Near-identical "bundle" pages of the same core product**: run keyword research per bundle — keep separate if search intent differs page to page, merge if it doesn't.
- **Backlink building for a no-storefront business**: join the local chamber of commerce/business groups (often hands you a backlink), get listed in local directories, set up a Google Business Profile even without foot traffic.
- Not every page needs SEO: ad-traffic/email-traffic landing pages can duplicate content and skip optimization entirely, as long as they don't cannibalize a keyword you actually want a different page to rank for (Matt ran 6 versions of the same Rank Week landing page, one per traffic source, zero SEO concern).
- **Priority order** when a site has many problems at once: helpful content first (can't optimize what doesn't exist) → internal linking as you write (self-solving if done concurrently) → backlinks.
- A **single relevant backlink** to a specific underperforming page can push it over the edge even with a large sitewide backlink count already in place (~700 backlinks cited in one case, stuck at position 3–5) — relevance to the exact page matters more than aggregate count. Backlinks from your own country are best; other English-speaking countries (US/Canada/UK) fine too.
- **Don't build a keyword/location page for every possible service×city combination** — a ~200-page plan for 2 HVAC clients was flagged as overreach; pick one keyword per location instead, chosen by keyword research and by which service is worth the most per job.
- **100% originality required** across near-identical service pages — "you need 100 original, my friend," no acceptable percentage of duplication. **Never target the same keyword on more than one page** — flat cannibalization rule, repeated to multiple attendees.
- **Shopify collection-page structure**: intro sentence(s) → product grid → long blog-length paragraphs with images underneath, covering the collection in depth — "the biggest missed opportunity when I look at a Shopify store."
- **Accordion/dropdown content**: don't hide genuinely important SEO content in a box — surface it directly and work with the designer to make expanded content look good; supplementary technical detail (e.g. product specs) in an accordion is fine, not an absolute ban. "Well, if this information is so important, why is it hidden in a box?"
- **Go after low-volume keywords deliberately** (0, 10, 20 monthly searches) — winnable, still convert; treated as the desired outcome, not a problem, since being the "obvious result" for a low-competition term converts once someone finds it.
- Ranking higher on Google also raises your odds of surfacing in ChatGPT/Perplexity/AI answer engines — a direct byproduct of the same internal-linking and authority work, not a separate task.

## 6. Day 5 — The 30-Day SEO Action Plan

**Structure (identical shape across all 3 cohorts):** 4 weekly cycles, ~1–2 hours/week, same 4 tasks repeated with a **new** blog post + **new** money page each week:
1. Publish 1 blog post that links to **2–3 other blog posts + 1 money page** (cohort variance: some cohorts say 2, others 3 — treat as "2–3").
2. Fix **5 "serious"/red-flagged issues** via the audit tool.
3. Fix that week's money page: title tag + meta description (treated as 2 separate sub-tasks even though conceptually one, so progress feels faster — one prompt fixes both at once, ~5 minutes).
4. Add **3 internal links** to that week's money page from other pages on the site.
- If one task already satisfies another (e.g. the new post already links to the money page), don't redo it — just check it off.
- Total over 30 days: **4 money pages, 4 blog posts, ~20 red issues fixed.**
- Commit out loud, on the call, to 4 specific calendar dates and paste the plan-spreadsheet link into each invite (Matt's own examples: "Dec 15/22/29, Jan 5" and "March 16/23/30, April 6" — all Mondays, but any consistent weekly day works; skipping a holiday week is fine). "If it's in the calendar, it will get done. At least it should get done. That's how I live my life."
- **"This is not a sprint, it is a marathon"** — don't compress into one sitting. Scope is deliberately capped (5 issues not all of them, 4 posts not more) specifically to prevent overwhelm and the burnout-driven quitting that causes people to stop by week two. "I'm not trying to get you guys to do everything. I'm trying to get you guys to make progress."

**Blog-topic sourcing** (two valid methods across cohorts):
- **Question-finder tools**: findquestions.com, rankweektraining.com, app.rwideas.com, answerthepublic.com — type what you sell, grab 4 questions.
- **Google's own SERP features** (Cohort B, deliberately avoiding AnswerThePublic after it crashed once already — "I don't want to crash it again"): Google your core niche keyword → scroll to **"People also ask"** → click a question to surface more similar ones → also check **"People also search for."** Pull **4 different questions**, not near-duplicate angles (e.g. not two blood-pressure variants back to back). If the seed keyword returns nothing useful, type a full question instead of a bare term.

**Money-page sourcing**: go to your own site's services/products section, pick your **4 most important** pages.

**Cannibalization reassurance / topical-authority logic** (hot-sauce demo): 4 blog posts plus a money page all mentioning "hot sauce" don't cannibalize each other, because each *full question* is its own distinct keyword ("what is hot sauce made of" ≠ "what is the healthiest hot sauce"). Analogy: "if a dentist could only talk about teeth on one page because teeth is a keyword, that doesn't make sense now, does it?"

**Money-page fix priority order** (Matt's own, repeated): **1) title tag, 2) meta description, 3) H1** — keyword in all three — then **wait 1–2 weeks** to see if rankings move before touching on-page content further.

**Checking whether a fix "worked"**: don't rely solely on incognito Google checks; refresh inside a rank tracker (SEMrush/Ubersuggest) since those pull from a fresh crawl, or use **Google Search Console filtered by page** as the definitive source.

**Handling an old page that outranks its intentional replacement** (headshot-photographer case): don't delete either page. Re-add the old/combined page back into site navigation, leave the newer split-out pages as they are, and make only small/minimal edits (e.g. a CTA tweak) to the page that's already ranking well.

**Deciding what to fix on a large old blog** (150+ posts): don't attempt to rehabilitate everything. Only chase **"low-hanging fruit"** — pages/posts already getting impressions or clicks but stuck on page 2 or lower on page 1. De-indexed posts with no meaningful impressions: delete and either 404 or 301-redirect (redirect only if a relevant destination page exists).

**Scaling location pages for a huge multi-location client**: run keyword research across the entire location list first, rank opportunities by search volume vs. ease of ranking, execute in priority order — even a large client is paced at roughly **2 new pages/week** ("sniper rifle" precision over "shotgun" volume) — a deliberate choice to protect execution quality, not a hard ceiling (the org could technically do more).

**"Local vs. broader" decision** for a growing service business: check actual search behavior — people search "[service] in [city]," not "[service] in [state]." For a large metro, build suburb-level pages within it before expanding to new cities; only expand geography once the existing metro's demand is closer to tapped out.

**WordPress "project"/case-study pages that won't rank on their own**: build the actual location or service page as the thing meant to rank (talk about the local area, landmarks, etc.) and link out to the relevant completed-project/case-study pages from within it — project pages support the ranking page, they aren't expected to rank themselves.

**Diagnosing a mismatched/unclear niche keyword**: Google broad candidate terms and look at what category of business actually shows up. If results are the wrong type of business, that keyword is miscategorized for your niche — keep testing variations. Also usable: ask ChatGPT directly *"who are my local competitors, this is what I do."*

**Reading a page audit's keyword-density flag**: don't trust the tool's density verdict alone — look at what your actual ranking competitors do (count their keyword usage) and match that rate rather than the tool's raw high/low label (example: 10 uses of a niche 3-word phrase flagged as likely too many; 2–3 mentions suggested as more appropriate).

**Readability fixes**: paragraphs to **2–3 sentences**; vary sentence length (drives a Flesch/ease-of-reading score).

**Heading structure fix**: exactly **1 H1** per page. For H2 count: use **one more** than what top-ranking competitors use.

**Getting real reviews without gaming**: hand the customer a business card with a QR code after service; offer something like **10% off** their next service for leaving a review — never specify "5-star," just ask for "a review." If offering nothing gets no reviews, that's useful internal data — adjust the incentive, but keep review arrival gradual/consistent, not a burst.

**Audit-tool troubleshooting flow**: (1) try a recrawl, (2) try re-adding your website, (3) try adding a different website (a friend's, or one you own) — if that one crawls fine and yours doesn't, the problem is specific to your site. Only after trying these, email support@heytony.ca (response may lag to Monday near end-of-day/week).

**Rules & thresholds specific to Day 5:**
- **Stop reciprocal link exchanges between friends'/accountability-group sites ("private blog network"/PBN) immediately** — "stop doing that right away." Illustration: "If I'm walking down the road and I pass by Brad Pitt, and Brad Pitt goes, 'Jessica is the best house cleaner I've ever had. You need to hire her.' What is going to carry more weight?" vs. 4 friends all vouching for each other.
- **Stop "review trains"** (community groups of 4–5 businesses trading review requests monthly) — reads as gaming the system; can get those reviews taken down and drag down other, legitimate reviews on the same profile as collateral.
- Never explicitly ask for a **"5-star"** review — just ask for "a review." Reviews arriving in a sudden cluster (e.g. 5 within 20 minutes) is a red flag, especially if not all reviewers are local (Google can see reviewer IP/location and weights accordingly). Don't fake a "moved from elsewhere" story to mask reviewer location. General rule: **"if it feels sneaky, don't do it."**
- Don't treat a page-audit tool's overall "site health %" as a real ranking-quality score. Over-optimization is real and penalized past a point (a web-hosting company with thousands of backlinks was flagged as over-optimized in places — "more is obviously too much").
- **Don't use Google Trends to judge keyword search volume** — "no, don't use that." Use a real keyword tool instead (SEMrush 7-day free trial suggested).
- Keyword/competitive tool pricing (this cohort): **Ubersuggest ≈ $30–40/month** (cheapest); **SEMrush ≈ $130–140/month**; SEMrush and Ahrefs most accurate for volume/difficulty (though every tool's absolute numbers are somewhat "made up"/estimated — the "who's actually ranking for what" data is accurate across tools). **Cached-data gotcha**: these tools show cached data (sometimes weeks or years stale) — click "update"/refresh to force current numbers.
- Don't generalize page copy to a whole state/region if search behavior is hyper-local (e.g. "nannies in Cincinnati" vs. "nannies in Ohio").
- **Insiders registration closes at midnight** the night of Day 5 — deliberately, so members join with a cohort/momentum rather than individually and adrift.
- **Local backlink tactics for a business starting from zero**: join local chamber of commerce, local business associations, local directories, cross-link with other local businesses (e.g. clean a business's office in exchange for a link), run a giveaway (free service), or support a local charity (~$50) for a logo/link/shoutout.
- **Expanding a local service into a new city**: build a separate, unique-content location page per target city (no duplicated copy) — expect **3–6 months minimum** before ranking movement even if the page is solid.
- **Seasonal/multi-country service business**: keep doing SEO year-round despite seasonality; set Google Business Profile to a **service area** (home address doesn't have to show publicly); if the service area is a different country, ideally have someone local to that country complete/verify the profile; run 2 separate money pages if the service is genuinely delivered in 2 distinct markets.
- **Splitting an all-in-one service page**: give every individual service its own page; structure top-level overview pages that each link down into individual-service sub-pages — link format (H2/H3/plain text) doesn't matter as long as the link exists.
- **Hyper-niche product, assumed low volume**: don't guess — run it through a real keyword tool; build a location page per serviced city even with one HQ city; for a known future event/spike in a specific city, stand up a location/event-specific page **6 months ahead** of the event.
- **Competing as an affiliate in an oversaturated category**: build "[Brand X] vs. [Brand Y]" / alternatives / dupe-style comparison pages to capture branded search traffic even without your own checkout — can sit in the footer, not main nav; pair with direct influencer outreach (roughly **5K–20K+ follower range** — "most of them will accept stuff for free").
- **Fixing a fragmented site** (hidden/unlinked pages, blog on a separate subdomain from money pages): consolidate everything under one main domain, make every page reachable from the homepage/nav.
- **Batch-write, paced-publish**: write/schedule posts in batches weeks in advance rather than writing one right before it's due — decouples writing effort from publish cadence.
- **Diagnosing "why does a technically-worse page outrank me"**: once your own on-page signals check out, look at backlink count, anchor-text profile, how other sites describe/reference the page, internal link count, blog-content volume/topical authority ("are they seen as the expert?").
- Weekly task load intentionally capped low (~1–2 hrs) specifically to prevent burnout — "I don't want this to feel like a burden on you."
- Blog cadence: **1 post/week target, 2/week max** recommended to most attendees; HeyTony's own agency publishes 4–8 pieces of content every 28 days for clients.
- Backlinks: HeyTony builds **4 per month** for its own clients — explicitly NOT 4–10/week ("that's too many, you don't need that many"). **Never use BNI** (Business Network International) for backlinks/networking — "BNI is a no, no" (repeatedly pitched to join, refuses).
- A third-party booking page is **not** your money page. New-city location pages: 3–6 months minimum to rank, rarely faster.
- Broadening a GBP's service area to reach a new city risks diluting or losing the map-pack ranking already held in the original area — treat as a real tradeoff, not a free expansion.
- Exact-match keyword in a URL/domain is **not** rated as a strong ranking factor ("some people have theories that exact match keywords matter, but I don't think that it plays that big of an impact") — don't migrate a working, recognizable domain just to force a keyword in.
- Not every "broken link" the audit tool flags is actually broken — verify manually (crawler false positives happen on domains it can't reach, e.g. canva.com).
- Fix money pages first when a site has multiple problem areas, but don't stop weekly blog publishing to do it — run both in parallel, since blog posts supply the internal links money pages need. Missing H1 tags matter on real content/money pages; they don't matter on throwaway pages with no SEO purpose (Matt's own example: an internal ChatGPT-built mini-game page).

## 7. Local & Multi-Location SEO (Cross-Cutting Reference)

- **Never target zip/pin codes as keywords** — "people aren't typing in zip codes," they type city names.
- **Location pages live under a dedicated "Locations" page**, not nested inside a specific service page (a recurring developer mistake — nesting location pages under one service page instead of a standalone Locations hub).
- **Word count for local pages**: 400–500 words is fine if the term isn't competitive; if it is, audit who's currently ranking and mimic their depth (can go up to ~2,000 words) — but always prioritize organizing it so an actual human reader can consume it and decide to hire you.
- **Tiered location-keyword structure**: keep a consistent base keyword, vary only the location. Tier 1 = main city keyword; tier 2 = neighborhood keyword — don't repeat the tier-1 wording on tier-2 pages, let the neighborhood name be the differentiator (example: 1 main city + 3 neighborhoods per city = up to 21 total location/keyword variants, using a stable root like "we buy junk cars" with the neighborhood as the only variable).
- **Multi-location GBP now requires video verification** (recording yourself entering the building) per location. Recommended sequencing: prove out one location first (rank speed, workload) before committing to a second — broadening a GBP's service area or adding a location risks diluting/losing the map-pack position already held.
- **Service-area business flexibility**: start a GBP as a service-area business (no public address), upgrade to a physical address later — no need to restart from scratch.
- **Real estate/dense-metro example** (Century-21 realtor, DC metro covering Landover MD, Suitland MD, DC, Prince George's County, Montgomery County — geography too dense/fragmented to target broadly, "top realtor in Maryland" = competing with 10,000+ agents plus Zillow/brokers): pick top **20 neighborhoods** first, build a "[service] in [neighborhood]" page for each with unique text (no duplication), separately publish "hidden gem" local content (best coffee shops nearby, phone-photographed) speaking to relocation buyers' lifestyle concerns beyond just listings. Pace 1–2 pages/week for a 200-neighborhood list, and **stop once a subset is already ranking/converting** — you don't have to finish the whole list.
- **Sarah, nanny agency, Cincinnati (~2M metro)**: build suburb-level pages within the existing metro before expanding cities, since a market that size still has untapped local demand.
- **Alejandro, cleaning company, Fairfield OH**: ranks top-5 organic/#2 map pack for "cleaning services Fairfield Ohio," expanding into Hamilton OH; built 2–3 location pages ~1 month prior with no movement yet — told to expect the standard 3–6-month floor.

## 8. E-commerce & Marketplace SEO (Cross-Cutting Reference)

- **E-commerce platform decision rule**: if your current site is already ranking/outranking competitors, do not move the main site/domain — bolt any new shop onto the **same domain/URL** to protect existing SEO equity. If the current shop isn't ranking at all, you're free to rebuild wholesale on a stronger platform.
- **Shopify collection-page structure**: intro sentence(s), product grid, then long blog-length paragraphs with images underneath — "the biggest missed opportunity when I look at a Shopify store." Use the **native blog**, never a third-party blogging app.
- **FAQ strategy for near-identical product pages** (jewelry, collectibles): write **different, product-specific FAQs** for each page (carat, metal, care/cleaning instructions) — imagine what a customer holding that exact item would ask; **never duplicate the same FAQ block across every page.**
- **For e-commerce with many SKUs**: audit collection pages and top-selling products first, not the full catalog. Add real content (a mini blog post) underneath the product grid on collection pages — most competitors leave them bare. Rotate which money page gets linked from each new blog post (e.g., alternate collection/product links every other week for a mixed catalog).
- **Two-sided marketplace / two-audience sites**: pick which side of the marketplace to court first — the side you understand best, or the side that's cheaper/easier to acquire and will pull the other side in (analogous to how Airbnb targeted hosts first). Write content answering that side's actual Google-able questions, not invented brand terminology.
- **Never let marketplace vendors publish their own posts under your domain** — risk of them free-riding on your domain authority and of spam/backlink-selling abuse.
- **Consolidating a bloated flat catalog** (~1,000 near-duplicate variant pages): preserve any page already ranking/getting traffic; for the rest, keyword-research which specific variants people actually search and build individual pages only for those, using category pages/filters for the rest.
- **Near-identical "bundle" pages of the same core product**: run keyword research per bundle, keep separate only if intent differs.
- **Accordion/dropdown content on product/collection pages**: don't hide genuinely important SEO content in a box; supplementary technical detail (specs) is fine there, "don't work as well anymore" for stuffing extra words.
- **Alt text bug caution**: alt text should never visibly inflate on-page word count — if it does, it's a rendering bug (seen on Shopify), not normal behavior.
- **Marketplace/AI-product keyword strategy**: don't try to rank for your invented product term (nobody Googles it) — identify the underlying problem your user is Googling instead (e.g. an author would Google "nobody buys my book, what do I do," not a platform's internal feature name).
- **Backdoor content route for a saturated product category** (supplement brand competing on general difficulty vs. a domain-adjusted difficulty gap): write blog posts answering the adjacent buyer questions (dosage/safety/timing questions) instead of fighting the head product term directly; publish 1–2/week, route traffic to the product via in-post mentions/links or a pop-up discount code.

## 9. International & Multi-Language SEO (Cross-Cutting Reference)

- **Do keyword research natively in the target country/language** rather than assuming home-market research transfers — Matt's own agency ranks broadly across the US while based in Canada, and this is stated as the default approach for any international expansion question raised on any call.
- **Targeting an overseas/lower-cost-market client base** (e.g. an India-based agency selling to US/UK): don't chase generic head terms (they return local-intent results) — go long-tail with the qualifiers your buyer actually cares about (e.g. "website designer in India who speaks English and works 9 to 5 Eastern") and state those reassurances explicitly in your page copy.
- **No country tag needed in the URL** unless the site is genuinely multi-language or has different regional pricing/currency.
- **TLD doesn't matter for geo-targeting** as long as the content itself is in the target audience's language — a Swiss `.ch` domain can still generate real leads in Florida, for example.
- **Cross-border/traveler content**: running both a native-language and an English version of a page can rank for the same keyword twice and pick up incidental foreign/traveler search traffic, even with no intent to sell there.
- **Writing to the pain-point question, not the category term**, is especially important across sensitive/cross-border niches (e.g. an online therapist working across multiple countries) — informational content earns the click; the money page itself should stay a shorter-tail term.

## 10. Blog Cadence & Content Strategy (Cross-Cutting Reference)

- **1–2 posts/week is the universal ceiling** — repeated dozens of times across every cohort and nearly identically worded each time. Agency's own cadence for clients: "one blog post a week or two blog posts a week, that's the max," 2/week reserved for top-paying clients; elsewhere framed as "4–8 pieces of content every 28 days." At 1–2/week: roughly **104–208 posts over 1–2 years** — a long-term compounding game, not instant.
- **Daily/365-a-year publishing = too much** — reads to Google as AI-spam churn rather than genuine, useful content; one attendee publishing 5 posts in a single week was told "that's too many... probably won't hurt, but most of us can't sustain that."
- **WordPress category hygiene**: 5–10 categories max, one per post, no tags.
- **Cornerstone/pillar content vs. many smaller posts**: Matt personally leans toward smaller, direct-answer posts over giant cornerstone pieces (readers want the answer, not a 5,000-word scroll — "are you reading all of that?"), but frames this as a question back to the room, not a hard rule. Don't force content architecture around the pillar-page concept upfront — "don't let that dictate what you should focus on first"; let cornerstone pages emerge naturally from keyword research and regular publishing.
- **Counter-example**: Kevin (30-year real-estate site owner, site live ~3 weeks) wrote a **5,000-word cornerstone article in ~4 hours** using ChatGPT (including 2 AI-generated infographics) and was already ranking around position 28 within weeks. A separate infographic boosted on Instagram for **$8** got roughly **40 shares and 40 saves**.
- **Batch-writing/scheduling weeks ahead** is a legitimate, higher-cadence-tolerant practice for experienced operators (a limo-company owner batch-writes and schedules posts weeks in advance, well above the 1–2/week ceiling given to newer attendees) — Matt doesn't flag this as a contradiction, suggesting the ceiling targets sustainable onboarding pace rather than a permanent hard cap for consistent, established operators.
- **Auditing an old/messy blog back-catalog**: pull each page in Semrush or GSC, check if it's ranking page 2–3 (improvable) vs. buried; check impression volume — ~2 impressions, don't bother; low-value pages get deleted/drafted to stop wasting crawl budget. Don't outright delete — move to **draft status** first (restorable), and give a dormant post **6 months to a year** before deciding it's truly dead (internal linking can revive it). Counter-data-point: Matt's own aggressive move (mass-deleting **70 old posts** with no backlinks/traffic/indexing on his own site) had **zero negative traffic impact** — the "safe" incremental rule and Matt's own actual practice diverge.
- **Keyword overlap across blog posts is fine** as long as each targets a distinct question/specific angle (e.g. "how much does it cost to get my roof replaced" vs. "roof replacement timeline") — overlapping *words* aren't the problem, overlapping *intent* is.
- **Auto-embedded "recent posts" block** (3–5 previous posts shown under a new one, via email/CMS template) is a free, automated internal-linking mechanism if your tool supports it — no extra manual work required.

## 11. Backlinks & Off-Page Authority (Cross-Cutting Reference)

- **Bad backlinks**: minor or no negative effect — "It might impact it negatively. It is likely not impacting it at all, though." Focus on content/on-page factors first.
- **HeyTony's own agency cadence**: **4 backlinks/month** for clients — explicitly not 4–10/week ("that's too many, you don't need that many"). **Never use BNI** for backlink networking.
- **Local backlink tactics for a business starting from zero**: chamber of commerce, local business associations, local directories, cross-linking with other local businesses (e.g. clean an office in exchange for a link), giveaways, charity sponsorship (~$50) for a logo/link/shoutout.
- **Private blog networks (PBNs) between friends' sites**: stop immediately — this crosses into something Google penalizes for most people, even though a thin technical line exists that some advanced SEOs walk.
- **Off-page "expertise profile"**: LLMs increasingly weigh being mentioned/referenced across the web (guest posts, podcast appearances) alongside raw backlink count when judging authority — "not in the same way that it used to" be purely about backlink volume.
- **A single relevant backlink to a specific page can outweigh a large sitewide backlink count** if it's more directly relevant to that exact page (case: ~700 backlinks, mostly low Domain Authority, stuck position 3–5 — advised that 1–2 well-targeted backlinks to that specific page, not more generic volume, could be the missing push).
- **Domain Authority/DR-style scores are directional at best** — not independently cited as a hard metric to chase in this material (see also the parallel diagnostics synthesis on this point).
- **Guest posts double as both a backlink and an authority/expertise signal** — count them as doing double duty rather than treating link-building and authority-building as separate workstreams.

## 12. Reviews & Reputation (Cross-Cutting Reference)

- **Ask every single customer, consistently, without stopping** — this is also what LLMs surface on: "if I type in best Italian restaurant near me, it automatically looks for the ones that have the most reviews, the highest average star rating."
- **Never specify "5-star"** — just ask for "a review." QR-code business card + a small incentive (e.g. 10% off next service) is fine; specifying the rating is not.
- **Sudden review clusters are a red flag** (e.g. 5 within 20 minutes), especially if not all reviewers are local — Google can see reviewer IP/location and weights accordingly.
- **"Review trains"** (community groups of 4–5 businesses trading review requests monthly) = penalized — reads as gaming the system; can get those reviews taken down and drag down other legitimate reviews on the same profile as collateral. General rule: "if it feels sneaky, don't do it."
- **Reviews as visual trust vs. indexable text**: a review screenshot is fine for human visual trust but Google cannot read text inside an image — embed or copy-paste the review's actual text as real text if you want it to carry SEO weight; use both together, not one or the other.
- **AI-drafted review replies are fine** — review/edit before publishing, don't auto-post.
- **Google Business Profile completeness**: fill out every field (menu, services, descriptions) in addition to collecting reviews.

## 18. Tradeoffs, Nuance & Open Contradictions

- **Deleting vs. drafting old content**: the stated safe default is draft-and-wait 6 months to a year, but Matt's own aggressive move (mass-deleting 70 posts outright on his own site) had zero negative traffic impact — the "safe" rule and Matt's actual practice genuinely diverge; he doesn't reconcile this on-record.
- **SEMrush — a real, unresolved contradiction across the material**: praised repeatedly as the most accurate keyword/competitor tool (worth its ~$130–150/month price, 7-day free trial recommended for validating niche search volume) in the majority of sessions, yet in one Jun 2026 session Matt states he's "stopped using/recommending it," calling it "way too expensive" post-Adobe-acquisition with reported billing/cancellation problems. Both positions are stated as his own view at different points — treat as an evolving stance, not a settled one.
- **Blog cadence is a deliberate ceiling, not a floor, for newer sites/attendees** (1–2/week to avoid Google's spam/AI-content-churn detection) — but experienced, consistent operators (CJ's batch-writing far above that cadence; Matt's own agency at 4–8 pieces/28 days for clients) aren't flagged as contradicting the rule, suggesting the ceiling targets sustainable *onboarding*, not a permanent hard cap.
- **Word count/depth is competition-relative, never absolute** — benchmark the *lowest*-ranking top-3 competitor, not the highest, and the right target moves over time as the competitive set changes (Matt's own page held #1 at 600–700 words for 2 years, then needed to compete with 2,300–2,500-word pages after losing rank).
- **Refreshing an already-#1 page is theoretically appealing but practically pure downside** — there's no ranking upside above #1, only risk of losing the spot; the only legitimate reason to touch it is poor conversion, and even then, incremental edits only.
- **Multi-location GBP expansion**: amplifies presence/authority in theory (map-pack eligibility per area) but dilutes/splits review volume and doubles maintenance workload; sequencing (prove one location first) is the safer path, not a free expansion.
- **Migrating e-commerce/website platforms**: freedom to rebuild wholesale only applies when the current asset isn't ranking; if the primary site/domain already ranks well, protect that equity by keeping new builds on the same domain rather than risking a fresh URL.
- **Old/dated-looking websites**: some longtime customers explicitly say they like the simplicity and readability, while visitors calibrated to slick modern sites may trust an outdated design less — no universal right answer, weigh audience against category norms.
- **Off-page SEO importance**: still real, but "not in the same way that it used to be" — pure backlink volume is being supplemented by broader expertise/authority signals LLMs pull from guest posts, podcasts, and mentions.
- **Aged/used domains carry a real but hard-to-quantify risk**: registration age is a minor positive ranking factor, but a clean-looking backlink profile doesn't guarantee Google hasn't penalized the domain over past-owner behavior — Matt's own experience with aged domains has been mixed enough that he now defaults to registering new ones (case: a client's ~$500 marketplace-purchased domain did "literally all the right things" for a full year with zero movement; migrating to a brand-new domain and moving the same content over started ranking within a month).
- **Rank-tracking-tool disagreement with reality isn't necessarily a site problem** — Google capped third-party/LLM tool visibility to roughly page-one SERP data around Aug/Sept, so a genuine position-24 ranking (confirmed by Search Console) simply won't appear in SEMrush/Mango-style tools; the disagreement reflects a structural blind spot in the tools, not a ranking or indexing failure.
- **Outranking Wikipedia/government pages on a shared head keyword** is framed as very difficult, not flatly impossible — the practical path is shifting to more specific, related long-tail keywords rather than a direct assault on entrenched SERP leaders.
- **Location-page strategy scales down deliberately to very small, low-search-volume towns** precisely because competitors ignore them — low tool-reported volume doesn't mean low real-world value; "somebody might look up that keyword in that small town and then you're the only one."
- **Ranking timelines are probabilistic, not fixed**: most participants should expect 3–6 months (new content/new markets), though a handful of Day-1 posts on established, already-linked sites have reportedly ranked (even reaching AI Overview) within 24 hours to a few hours — the exception, built on existing site authority, not the norm for anyone starting cold.
- **Squeeze/landing pages and content-marketing strategy aren't in conflict, but shouldn't be merged** — keep the high-converting single-purpose page pure (no nav links out) while building a separate blog section for organic/AI discovery; the two serve different jobs on the same site.
- **Podcast content is valuable as raw material for training the AI on your voice, but a weak internal-link destination** for most businesses — reserve direct episode-linking for creators with outsized personal-brand recognition (Hormozi/Gary-Vee tier).
- **NotebookLM podcast embeds** are a plausible engagement/SEO upside (time-on-page, alternate content format) but explicitly flagged by Matt as untested by him, not an endorsement — a genuine open question.
- **Whether to invest in SEO at all is conditional on where a business already converts** — Matt told one attendee that thriving via Instagram/events/referrals is a legitimate reason not to prioritize organic SEO spend, since "everybody runs a business in a different way"; for a novel marketplace model, Matt was explicitly open to partnerships/influencers/community distribution being the better lever than SEO.
- **Scaling unique FAQs across many near-identical SKUs** (e.g. jewelry) is labor-intensive, but Matt frames the effort as justified because it's in service of selling more product, not just an SEO technicality.
- **Traffic from outside a business's service area or core audience isn't wasted** — it's reframed as an authority/trust-building signal to Google (time on site, page visits, form fills, newsletter opens/clicks) that indirectly helps local/transactional rankings; this gives newsletter cadence/consistency and broad-content publishing an SEO dimension beyond pure relationship-building.
- **Reverse-engineering a competitor's exact on-page structure** (duplicate H1s, etc.) is framed as a reasonable experiment only when you have "nothing to lose" (not ranking anyway) — Matt is explicit he isn't "the end all be all" on strategy and treats this as testing, not doctrine.
- **Automated internal-linking plugins are not forbidden** ("you absolutely can" use one) — but Matt's own practice avoids them, an acceptable shortcut he personally still chooses to skip in favor of manual judgment.
- **Hiding content in accordions/dropdowns** is discouraged for genuinely important SEO content, but treated as fine for supplementary technical detail a real subset of users actually opens — a judgment call per page, not a blanket rule.
- **Insiders scaling concern** (raised across multiple cohorts): more members joining doesn't necessarily mean less individual attention, because people stop needing to ask once their question's been answered, and not everyone attends every call — only the ones relevant to that week's problem.
- **Keyword-research tool numbers actively disagree with each other** (SEMrush, Ubersuggest, Google Keyword Planner, heykeywords.com) — treat any of them as directional only, never authoritative; Matt personally weighs "what are people actually searching" and difficulty-to-rank over raw tool output, and believes keyword research is becoming less important generally as AI search grows.
- **The 30-day plan's slow, ~1-hour/week pace is itself a deliberate tradeoff** — members could likely finish faster, but the pacing is designed specifically to protect follow-through and prevent the overwhelm that causes people to quit by week two.
