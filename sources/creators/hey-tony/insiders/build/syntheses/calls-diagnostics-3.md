# HeyTony Insiders — Diagnostics Synthesis

Source calls: **#124** (Why Doesn't It Rank, 11.21.2025) · **#113** (Why Doesn't It Rank, 12.12.2025) · **#109** (Why Doesn't It Rank, 12.19.2025) · **#105** (WDIR, 12.26.2025). All four are the same recurring live-audit format: attendees submit a page that's been live for months (never brand-new, never the homepage — it "can rank for too many things"), Matt Diamante live-screen-shares an audit using SEMrush + a page-audit tool + the live site, diagnoses the specific blocker, and assigns one small homework task rather than a full rebuild. Closes each time with public accountability ("post what you're going to fix this week").

## The Master Diagnostic Sequence

Merged from all four calls into the single strongest version. Run in this order:

1. **Get one specific URL — never the homepage.** Ask what keyword they're actually trying to rank for.
2. **Context first:** overall site traffic (near-zero vs. established?), does a sitemap exist and is it submitted, does Google even know the page exists at all (Search Console coverage/indexing check).
3. **Confirm the literal target keyword string actually appears on the page.** Sounds obvious; frequently isn't true (Manny's case: the exact phrase was missing entirely).
4. **Keyword research:** pull the term into SEMrush, set the correct country/location (Matt himself once had it stuck on Canada instead of US — check this every time), pull Volume + Keyword Difficulty (KD), and cycle through synonyms/geographic variants to find the easiest winnable variant (e.g., "couples counseling" vs. "couples therapy"; "Times Square" vs. "Midtown Manhattan").
5. **Search intent by content type:** open whoever ranks in the top 3 and identify what content type dominates — service pages, blog posts, job postings, or "platform" sites (Vimeo-style, marketplaces). Your page's content type must match what's already ranking; a keyword with 0 difficulty is still wrong if the ranking intent doesn't match your business.
6. **Run a page-audit tool pass:** confirm the keyword is present in the title tag, meta description (near the front, not buried), URL, and heading tags; confirm a canonical URL is set.
7. **Heading hierarchy check:** exactly one H1; every H2 must directly reinforce/support the H1's specific topic (not generic value-prop lines); never skip a level going down (H1 straight to H3 is wrong — must go H1→H2→H3); skipping backward/up a level is fine (H3 back to H2 is no problem). Demote low-value H4s to plain paragraph text.
8. **Word count / depth vs. the #1–3 ranker:** compare directly against what's actually ranking, not against "what feels thorough" — this can mean cutting (excess) or adding (thin content), whichever direction the top ranker demonstrates. Keep it scannable either way (short text block + image + short paragraph, repeated), not a wall of text.
9. **Internal link anchor-text audit:** check what anchor text points to and from the page — misdirected anchor text (linking to the homepage using the exact keyword a different page should own) actively tells Google the wrong page is about that topic.
10. **Visual/media check:** images vs. the top 3 (blurry/cut-off vs. crisp and cohesive), video presence, page cleanliness/whitespace.
11. **For local businesses:** Google Business Profile, directory/citation listings, address visibility, reviews, review responsiveness.
12. **Backlinks/authority vs. top rankers:** referring domains, Authority Score, follow vs. no-follow mix.
13. **Page speed — always last,** only after content/keyword correctness is confirmed equal-or-better than competitors.
14. **Decide and assign ONE small, specific homework task** — not a full rebuild. **Unless the page already ranks or converts — in which case leave it alone entirely, regardless of how many rules above it breaks** (see governing rule below).

## The Governing Rule: Never Touch a Page That's Already Ranking

The single most-repeated principle across all four calls, stated independently and forcefully each time:

- "My recommendation is always like, look at the pages that are already ranking for the keywords you want and leave those alone." "I really don't care why they're ranking it." "I'm not trying to be like a police... every page on your website has to be perfect... sometimes Google ranks it... even if things are technically wrong. And that's totally fine." [105]
- "If you're already ranking, don't touch that page." "My job isn't to do the perfect SEO on your page." — said to Brett, who ranks #2 for "Miami probate Realtor" with zero backlinks and a page Matt calls thin/not useful; and about Ellen Mitchell, who ranks #1 while repeating "probate" 122 times, running two duplicate H1 tags, and hiding overlay text behind the nav ("this is a nightmare... is this useful to you? No, not at all") — still: leave it. [124]
- Applies even to swapping a homepage: promoting an already-better-optimized page to be the new homepage (Jessa's case) is explicitly framed as "a little bit risky" — the mitigation is monitor rankings for days/weeks and revert if performance drops, never "just avoid testing." [113]
- Scope of what "leave it alone" covers: duplicate H1s, keyword stuffing, non-standard structure, hidden/stuffed text — none of it matters if the page ranks and converts. Fixing it "for correctness" risks breaking what's actually driving business. UX/CTA fixes on an already-ranking page are still fair game — the freeze applies to content/structure that could shift rankings, not to conversion-element polish. [105, 124]

## Keyword Research, Difficulty & the Avalanche Method

- **Prioritize low-KD keywords first.** These can rank with zero backlinks — the fastest path to traction before investing in link building. [109]
- **The Avalanche / Snowball Method** (named explicitly): deliberately target small, low-competition keywords, even at only 10–20 searches/month. Rank for a bunch of these first; as rankings accumulate, Google trusts the site more, ChatGPT starts surfacing it, and that compounding trust makes harder keywords rankable later. A KD of 21/100 is still called "easy" — a green light even at only 110 searches/month. [124]
- Backlink/referring-domain volume matters far less for low-competition keywords — you can outrank sites with vastly more backlinks than you when the keyword itself has little competition (Brett ranks #2 with zero backlinks and zero referring domains purely because there's no real competition for the phrase; Marla's "Sunset Cruise" term — 50 searches/month, KD 0 — has #1 and #2 ranked competitors with only 1 and 0 backlinks respectively, proving it's winnable on content alone). [124, 113]
- **Ranking-position obsession is outdated.** Doesn't matter if you're #1, #2, #3, or anywhere on page one — all get traffic. "The new SEO" = aggregate traffic from many sources plus being recommended, including showing up inside LLM answers — not fixation on the #1 spot. [124]
- Diagnostic order when a keyword seems wrong: pull keyword variants + difficulty/volume in SEMrush, compare the current target against easier variants before assuming the page itself is broken (Boston Towing case: chasing "Boston Towing" at high difficulty instead of "tow truck Boston" (KD 29) or the still-easier "tow truck Boston MA"). [109]
- Spot-check LLM/AI-search visibility directly: run the exact target query in ChatGPT and compare who it recommends against who ranks organically in Google and who appears on authoritative directory/list pages (e.g., a `.gov` business list) — a read on what currently feeds AI-assistant recommendations for that niche. Caveat: in this test, ChatGPT's recommendations favored 5-star-rated businesses but didn't appear to weight review volume/count — a business with fewer total reviews outranked one with more, purely on star rating. [109]

## Search Intent Must Match Content Type

- Determine intent by literally looking at what's ranking: is the SERP full of service pages, blog posts, or job postings? Your page's content type must match what's already ranking, not just the keyword. If a blog post and a service page both seem to target the same term, Google the term yourself and write/keep whichever content type the SERP is actually dominated by — don't run both against each other (keyword cannibalization). [105]
- If the top-ranking results for a keyword are all "platform" types (e.g., Vimeo-style results) and you're a local service business, that keyword's intent doesn't match your business — deprioritize it even if keyword difficulty is 0 (Trevor's live-streaming case). If top results are blog posts, ranking a hard sales/service page for that same term will be difficult, and vice versa. [113]
- Discerning intent when the SERP looks like the wrong audience entirely: Google the keyword yourself, or run a SERP analysis in SEMrush, and read what content type/audience actually ranks. Nick's voiceover-actor keywords were pulling job-seeker content (people looking for voiceover *work*) instead of hiring-client content — described as inherently manual, trial-and-error diagnosis. His narrower "elearning and corporate voiceover" category terms performed better than broad "voiceover" terms. [105]

## On-Page Structure Rules

**Headings**
- One H1 tag per page. Always. Two H1s (including duplicated H1/H2 pairs from mobile/desktop section duplication) confuses what the page is about to Google. A second auto-generated H1 (e.g., a theme's auto "Latest Blog Posts" heading) is a bug, not a feature — demote it. [105, 124]
- H2s must directly support/reinforce the H1's specific topic — they are subtopics of the main topic, not generic value-prop lines. Matt rejected nearly all of Nick's actual H2s against his H1 ("video marketing for small business") for this reason: "give me one hour and you get an entire month of authority content," "attention consultants," "you're wasting 40 hours a month on content nobody sees," "gut punchline" — none reinforced the stated topic. [105]
- Weight order: H1 most important, H2 secondary but still very important, H3 is "where things fall off a little," H4 carries far less weight than H2 — convert unnecessary H4s to plain paragraph text. Never skip a level going down (H1 straight to H3 trips you up); skipping backward/up a level is fine. [124]
- Turn generic "step" labels into keyword-reinforcing headings: instead of "Step 1, Step 2, Step 3," use the actual action as the H2 (e.g., "Clean the door gaskets") — the literal word "step" adds nothing. [124]
- Keyword-stuffing red flag: repeating the target keyword 122 times on one page is explicitly bad practice — even though, in Ellen Mitchell's case, it correlated with a #1 ranking (don't touch it; don't copy it). Hidden/auto-collapsing text (e.g., keyword-loaded text behind a nav overlay, visible only on scroll; breadcrumb nav that flashes and disappears) can read to Google as keyword stuffing and is Google-unfriendly regardless of intent. [124, 109]
- You can style an H1 to be visually *smaller* than an H2 via custom CSS/font-size if you want the top tag to carry SEO weight without visually dominating the layout (Matt does this experimentally on his own site). [105]

**URLs**
- URL should contain the keyword/location, not a vague truncated version (e.g., `.../compactor-rentals-new-jersey`, not `.../compactors`). [105]
- Never put "near me"/"near you" literally in URLs or on-page copy — Google geolocates the searcher/device automatically; it does nothing for rankings. Matt: this is "amateur SEO" — "whoever you hired to do this is a hack." Strip unnecessary category-folder slugs too; use a flat pattern like `service-city` (e.g., `landscape-lighting-montreal`). [109]
- URLs should be human-readable and describe the page's topic on their own — not auto-generated ID/number slugs. [124]
- Only redirect/change a URL if it has no existing backlinks pointing to it — if backlinks point to the old URL, don't casually change it. [105]

**Meta / titles / one keyword per page**
- Don't keyword/service-stuff a single URL, title, or meta field (Anthony's client had "trash compactors NJ / buy, rent or lease / commercial waste solution" all crammed together) — one specific keyword per field. Meta description: put the target keyword toward the front, not buried mid-sentence. [105]
- One keyword per page — every page on a site should target exactly one keyword; don't let a single homepage try to rank for two distinct services (Trevor's homepage diluted between "live streaming" and "video production"). [109, 113]

**Word count / depth**
- Match the top-ranking competitor's depth — don't maximize for its own sake, and don't assume more is safer. Anthony's client page ran ~2,800 words vs. the #1 competitor's 823 (roughly 2,000 words of excess burying the actual product info). Conversely, James's 697-word page sat under the #1 competitor's 2,500 words, 18 H2s, step-by-step structure with an image per step — here the fix was to add depth, not cut it. Same underlying rule, opposite directions: **match what's actually ranking, not an absolute target**, and keep it scannable either way. [105, 124]

## Site Architecture, Internal Linking & Cannibalization

- **Internal linking, both directions, with descriptive anchor text:** link the target page from at least three other relevant posts using the exact phrase/keyword (e.g., "deep clean your dishwasher"); from the target page, link out to related pages the same way (e.g., "if you're looking to clean your kitchen cabinets…"). In-body contextual links (inside actual paragraph content) carry more weight than sidebar "related posts" links. [124]
- Never use vague anchor text like "get your copy here" — rewrite to describe the destination ("get your copy of [book title] here"). Brett's site had no internal links inside blog-post body copy at all until the very bottom, where the only link used this exact vague phrasing pointing to Amazon. [124]
- Audit internal anchor text sitewide: if a page links to the homepage (or any other page) using the exact keyword a *different* page should rank for, that's misdirecting topical relevance — audit and correct anchor text to match the intended target page per keyword (Manny's Montreal page linked to his homepage using "landscape lighting," diluting the very page trying to rank for that phrase). [109]
- Track internal link equity to individual pages: add a column for how many other pages link to a given page — especially useful for "gateway"/navigational pages whose main job is routing traffic into filtered or dynamic sub-pages. [105]
- **Never let two sites you control target the same keyword** with the same tactics — you likely end up ranking on neither. Instead assign each site a distinct keyword target and connect them via internal backlinking/recommendation copy (e.g., a hub site publishes "top 10 restaurants in [area]" and links out to/recommends the client restaurant site, rather than both targeting "restaurant [area]" directly). [109]
- **Multi-domain consolidation:** compare Authority Score of each domain in SEMrush, keep the strongest as the single hub, and stop cross-linking to/routing bookings through weaker satellite domains. If a user has to bounce across multiple domains to complete one action (e.g., book villa → "things to do" site → cruise page → back), consolidate into one continuous flow on the strongest-authority domain so both users and crawlers stay on one site — "make it one cohesive experience." [113]
- **Cross-domain architecture confusion** (e.g., a personal domain redirecting into a brokerage subdomain, logo bouncing between the two) should be consolidated so clicking the logo always stays on the same domain — makes it unambiguous to Google which site should rank. [124]
- Fix dead-end pages: ensure the site's persistent header/nav menu appears on every page/post, including blog posts, so users and Google always have a path back to primary navigation. [124]
- Don't strip site navigation to force a single "buy" action on a landing/sales page — it kills trust and blocks people from verifying legitimacy or finding more info; add nav back even on a single-offer page. [113]
- Don't lump unrelated items under one taxonomy/page (a "Things To Do" page mixing currency info, trip insurance, airport services, golf-cart rentals, and restaurant info) — confuses Google about the page's actual topic; keep categories semantically clean. [113]
- Don't try to rank for/serve every possible keyword and page — "pigs get fat and hogs get slaughtered"; breadth-grabbing backfires. [113]
- Navigation design for many sub-offerings: don't list every sub-page in the nav — feature the top ~5 most popular items, then add a lower "other experiences" section for people who scroll further. [113]
- Don't build your own MLS/IDX-style search widget if a third-party service already handles that function and just redirects clients elsewhere anyway — a technical nightmare to implement well for low payoff (Brett's realtor site). [124]

## Content Repurposing & Multi-Format Strategy

- **Blog-to-video repurposing:** take an existing blog post (e.g., "how much does residential cleaning cost in Cincinnati"), feed it to ChatGPT to get a video script, then film and post that as a video — starts indexing on Google and reaches more people even without likes. [113]
- **Repurpose an existing tutorial video into blog visuals:** pull screenshots/frames from a video you already have to use as step-by-step images in a blog post instead of shooting new photos — "the media's already done." [124]
- **Embed the how-to video directly in the post:** add an H2 like "How to Clean X — Video," embed the YouTube video underneath. Google indexes pages containing video as an added ranking surface, and it serves users who'd rather watch than read. Only worth doing when the underlying video is already strong (in James's case, ~1M views) — the tactic assumes a good existing asset, not fresh production. [124]
- **Link the blog post from the YouTube video's description**, placed high (not buried) — YouTube description links carry limited SEO weight on their own (anyone can add one), but a high-view video still delivers outsized real-world traffic regardless of the link's technical weight. [124]
- **Pinterest driver:** add a large vertical/collage graphic per post if targeting Pinterest — drives an additional non-Google traffic source, which itself signals legitimacy to Google. [124]
- **Multi-language SEO workflow:** auto-translate via ChatGPT (exact prompt used live: **"Translate this to Spanish."**) or have a native speaker write it, then have a native speaker review/edit the translation before publishing. Separately run keyword research in the target language to confirm real search volume actually exists first — don't assume translating = ranking (in this case, Spanish-language search volume for the US-based niche keyword looked negligible). Use a WordPress multilingual plugin (WPML) to auto-generate the translated URL variant (e.g., an `es-` prefixed URL). [124]
- **Bilingual sites — subfolder, not subdomain:** keep language versions on subfolders if the goal is for Google to treat the whole site as one entity — a subdomain gets treated as a separate site (confirmed live on a French-main/English-subdomain setup that Google was indeed treating as two sites). [109]
- If your niche has both a spelled-out full term and a common acronym (e.g., "International English Language Testing System" vs. "IELTS"), use both forms somewhere on the page — searches happen in both forms. [109]

## Backlinks, Disavow & Off-Page Signals

- **Never disavow backlinks. Full stop.** Matt, verbatim: "In all the years that I've been doing SEO, we have never disavowed links... my advice is never disavow links. Never." It's impossible to know which toxic-looking link may actually be propping up your rankings — some clients who disavowed after a scary SEMrush/GSC notification saw rankings drop as a result. A "suspicious" backlink flag in SEMrush isn't worth chasing down; treat it as a dilution problem (keep earning more legitimate links so the suspicious ratio shrinks naturally), never a removal/disavow problem. [109, 113]
- **Verifying a suspicious/flagged backlink is actually real:** open the linking page's View Page Source and Ctrl/Cmd+F for your own domain string — if it isn't in the code, the backlink tool's data is stale or simply wrong. **Cross-check** SEMrush's referring-domains list against Google Search Console → Links → "top linking sites" as a second source before trusting either alone. [109]
- **If you find a real toxic link-farm pattern:** first check whether you personally own/control any of the flagged domains (old PBNs, abandoned old sites) — if so, log in and manually delete the outbound links yourself. **If you don't own the site:** contact the owner directly, name the exact page/link, and ask for removal; expect some owners to demand payment (quoted ~$50/link) and others to comply free; follow up to confirm it's actually gone. After any removal (own PBN or third-party outreach), expect a re-crawl lag before the tool or rankings reflect the change. [109]
- **Spam score benchmarks:** should stay under roughly 11%, ideally around 1% (1% is effectively the practical floor — you can't hit 0%). Anything meaningfully above that range, go audit the backlink profile. Use a free spam-score-checker tool (unbranded; Matt believes SEMrush owns it behind the scenes) — paste a domain, click "Check Spam" → returns spam score, Domain Authority, Page Authority, dead-link count, and domain age in one pass. [109]
- **Follow vs. no-follow: downweighted.** Matt doesn't care about the technical attribute — a no-follow link still counts as "another online mention" and "another place people can find you." [113]
- **Diagnosing backlinks in SEMrush:** check referring domains vs. total backlinks as separate numbers (one domain linking hundreds of times is not equivalent to hundreds of unique referring domains, and such mass-link patterns can later show as "Removed/Lost" — treat raw backlink count with more skepticism than referring-domain count). Filter to "Follow" links only when judging real link equity; ignore "Removed/Lost" entries since they no longer count; check the Authority Score of each linking domain to judge whether it's worth pursuing similar links. [124]
- **Local citations:** Google "[city] business directory" style searches, then pay the listing fee (even a cheap one — $29/year found live) to get a citation/backlink, worth it even if the link is no-follow. [113]
- **`.gov` domains carry outsized built-in authority** (only real government entities can register them) and are very hard to outrank — if a `.gov` directory lists businesses in your category, get listed on it. [109]
- **Chamber of Commerce membership:** used both for the backlink and hyper-local relevance signal. Value is inconsistent/anecdotal — worked well for local-service referrals in one case (~$800/year, "pretty expensive" but justified by a cleaner in Matt's own chamber who won real business through it) while another attendee's complaint that his chamber "focuses on growing members, not nurturing current members" was left unresolved even while still being told to rejoin after losing rankings tied to leaving. [113]
- **Purchased press-release/backlink services:** no blanket verdict — Matt hasn't personally tested the $40–60/post tier himself; some SEOs he knows swear by them; judge by results, not reputation. He is personally testing **AB Newswire.com**: $200 tier places backlinks on AP News, USA Today, Benzinga, Globe and Mail, Barchart, and TV/radio broadcast-station news sites — framed as likely a temporary ranking boost, not a lasting one ("I've seen this work... it's not going to last forever"). **Amplify** (by Chris Munch) is a press-release service one attendee had already used (credits bought years earlier) for a release tied to his book, which produced some of his existing root-domain backlinks. [105, 124]
- Big brands (Yelp, Amazon, Home Depot) can rank while breaking normal SEO best practices, purely on domain authority/trust — don't use them as a model for "what good SEO looks like." Same logic for a thin, table-only, multi-language `.gov` towing directory outranking real competitor sites on pure domain trust. [109]

## Local SEO & Google Business Profile

- A Google Business Profile with zero website can still outrank fully built competitor sites in local results — demonstrated live by a towing company ranking with no website at all, just a GBP + Facebook page and a 2.2-star rating: "this just shows how powerful just having a Google business profile can be." For a local business with no web presence at all, prioritize getting a real GBP live before anything else. [109]
- Missed/unanswered phone calls hurt local rankings — Google reads poor call responsiveness as a bad user-outcome signal. [109]
- A GBP's location must match the property/business's actual physical address, even when the target searchers live elsewhere (e.g., a Mexico vacation rental targeted at US/Canada bookers still needs its GBP pinned in Mexico) — local pack/map visibility is driven by where the pin sits, not where the searcher is. [109]
- Merging duplicate GBP listings for one location: if you have login access to both, contact Google directly through the Business Profile interface to request a merge and specify which listing to keep; flagging one as a duplicate via GBP's own reporting flow is also possible, but Google acting on it isn't guaranteed. [109]
- Rank-and-rent/no-address local sites face a structurally harder path than an owned local business with a real address — Google increasingly expects local trust signals (GBP, directories, address, citations) a pure lead-gen operator may not be able to obtain; "not impossible," but longer and harder than it used to be. [109]
- New/temporary business location: create a separate Google Business Profile for it directly (search "create Google Business profile," follow verification prompts) — worth doing even for a location expected to run only about a year. [105]
- Local-business copy must state the exact service area — a specific neighborhood, not a landmark/transit hub nobody actually searches (e.g., "Times Square" is not a real search term; the real keyword is "Midtown Manhattan/NYC"). [113]
- Before building a location/suburb page, check search volume for that specific town/neighborhood name first — don't build one just because the town exists. Separately, figure out how people in that specific market actually search (by hyperlocal neighborhood name vs. by county/city): in a DC-area case, granular town names (Potomac, Bethesda) function as income/status signals distinct from broader county names (Montgomery County) — prioritize granular pages for the wealthier micro-markets specifically. [109]
- Tool-reported local rankings shouldn't be trusted at face value — SEMrush's local-pack/map-view simulation can diverge from a live, in-market Google search (one case: SEMrush showed the client outside the top 3 while a live search from the actual market showed him at #3), reportedly because of a too-broad default location setting. Always sanity-check local/map position against a real search from the target geography. [109]
- Active social presence (YouTube, Facebook) functions as a legitimacy/trust signal that Google and LLMs weigh, even at low subscriber/follower counts — "any business who's not doing that is going to get left behind." [124]

## Technical & Platform-Specific Gotchas

- **GoHighLevel (GHL):** sitemaps do **not** auto-update when a new blog post publishes — you must manually regenerate the sitemap inside GHL, then resubmit/re-validate it in Google Search Console. Schema markup is not yet supported on individual blog posts (works fine on any other page type; Matt confirms GHL is actively working on this). Per Matt, GHL replaces Calendly (booking/calendars), Kajabi (courses — "they literally copied exactly what Kajabi does"), DocuSign (contracts/e-sign), plus invoicing, follow-ups, meeting reminders, email lists/sequences, and website/landing-page building — but he runs his own paid community separately on Membership.io. Embedded live pricing widgets (e.g., real-time gold/silver pricing) are something GHL handles cleanly that a from-scratch WordPress rebuild may not yet be able to replicate. [105, 124]
- **Diagnosing duplicate H1/H2 tags on page builders** (High Level, Wix, Squarespace all do this): open the page and look for a "desktop version" section and a separately-hidden "mobile version" section carrying the same heading text — caused by builders encouraging you to duplicate a section and hide one copy per breakpoint instead of building one truly responsive section. Confirm by resizing the browser/using the builder's device toggle and watching for a second, differently-highlighted instance of the same tag. **There is no in-builder toggle fix** — you must rebuild the section as one truly responsive block (delete the duplicate, keep one, restyle for both breakpoints). [105]
- **Testing true responsiveness is a separate check from a clean heading audit.** Resize the browser/switch device view live and watch whether content reflows smoothly vs. getting cut off or requiring horizontal scroll. A site can pass a "no duplicate H1" tool pass and still fail this (theatregym.co.uk case: clean on the heading audit, but not responsive — content cut off on smaller viewports, doesn't reflow, can't scroll properly). Google has done mobile-first indexing since ~2016 — visually cut off/non-reflowing mobile content is a direct negative regardless of desktop quality. An audit tool passing only means it caught what it's built to catch: Matt's own tool flags multiple H1 tags (or identical duplicate H2s) but does not flag multiple *different* H2 tags and says nothing about responsiveness. [105]
- **GoDaddy site builder:** sitemap didn't auto-generate despite the platform's own claim that it would (Michelle's case) — the fix given was to contact GoDaddy support directly. **Yoast SEO** (WordPress plugin), by contrast, generated a correct sitemap.xml with no issue in a parallel case. [113]
- **WordPress admin → Settings → Reading:** used to reassign which page is the homepage (Jessa's course-page swap) — the "Home" nav button simply repoints automatically; no menu restructuring needed. Don't delete the old homepage content; monitor rankings for days/weeks and revert if performance drops. [113]
- **Elementor:** check for stale references to domains you've already deleted — a client still had an old link to a deleted domain, producing a broken/insecure outbound link. Audit for `http://` (non-secure) targets in outbound/internal links, even ones that technically redirect, and change them to explicit `https://`. [113]
- **A site-wide audit tool should never be run against a site still mid-construction** — results will be dominated by temporary/incomplete-build errors that aren't meaningful; wait until launch. [124]
- **Page-speed priority order:** fix content/keyword targeting and backlinks/authority *first* — page speed is explicitly last. "You gotta fix your pages first and then you can look at page speed after that." "If your content is equal or better than everybody else's content and you're still not ranking, then... look at your page speed." Standard page-speed fixes once you get there: use WebP images instead of other formats, reduce unused JavaScript. [113]
- **Interactive Google Maps embeds:** replace with a static screenshot of the map, uploaded as a smaller-file-size static image, hyperlinked directly to the GBP/Maps listing — keeps the "we're local" trust signal without the page-speed cost. PageSpeed Insights directly flagged the Google Maps API + related JS as a slowdown source in one audit; live poll of the group found nobody actually uses the interactive-map feature on any site. [113]
- Logos rendered as an image (not text) mean Google can't read any text baked into that image ("Google can't see the photo... they can't see the words in the photo") — a logo carrying a business name or keyword as image-only text loses that signal entirely. [113]

## Conversion Elements: CTAs, Navigation, Popups, Media

- A page should have exactly one call-to-action color, visually distinct from everything else — don't run 3–4 competing accent colors plus multiple simultaneous CTAs (one homepage ran "call now for a complimentary consultation" alongside "download your free declutter report," ironically for a business branded around decluttering). [109]
- Don't lead a page with an aggressive, too-early conversion ask (e.g., "35% off, sign up now") before the visitor has enough context about what's being offered. [109]
- Don't stack two instances of the identical CTA on one page (e.g., a "Call Brett" link text sitting right beside an already-clickable phone number). Offer a mix instead — one soft CTA (contact form/quote request) plus the direct phone number — so users who aren't ready to call still have an option. [124]
- Replace autoplaying/rotating hero media (a rotating banner cycling through multiple product categories, or a background video) with a static image — faster load, easier to read, doesn't punish users on slower connections, and avoids confusing users who find a repeating/cycling banner hard to follow. [124]
- Never show a popup immediately on page load — use an exit-intent popup instead (triggers on fast upward scroll or mouse-toward-back-button). [124]
- Homepage/landing-page headline formula: state location + what you do + one clear CTA button placed above the fold (not a small side "get quote" box). Matt's example: "We are a dentist. We will fix your teeth. We are plumbers. We will fix your leaks." [113]
- Header decluttering: don't show your own logo/brand name twice (once as an image, once as text) in the header — "wasted real estate"; use the second slot for a value proposition or CTA instead. [113]
- Rewriting vague service-page headlines: replace proprietary/branded product names in your main H1/value prop with plain, literally-searched language. Matt's own homepage pattern used as the model: lead H1 = "Level up your digital marketing," followed by a specific one-line description of what the business actually does, then social-proof/logos. Cut branded jargon (e.g., "Marketing Mountain," "Lead Forge," "Intellisites," "Radio VIP Day," "The Builder's Code") from primary headlines since nobody searches those terms and a first-time visitor can't tell what the business does — fine to keep such names *within* a page that already explains itself, or on an ad-driven landing page where the ad already set context. [105]
- Instagram-specific: never put a link inside a Reel or a regular post caption — it isn't clickable on desktop or mobile; only the bio link is clickable. Allowed up to 3 hashtags, but use only 1–2, and pick ones actual customers would follow, not industry-insider tags (a maid-service hashtag mostly followed by other maids/cleaners, not customers, was flagged as ineffective). [113]
- Terminology doesn't matter (funnel page, lead page, landing page) — "it's a website and people are looking for information"; design for the visitor's task, not the label. [113]

## Blog & Content Hub Structure

- **Blog roll / index page structure:**
  1. Do not use a "filter" widget that appends a `?taxonomy=category-term-35`-style query string — Google doesn't treat this as a real, nameable page.
  2. Instead, place 5–10 topic/category names as plain links at the top of the blog page, each linking to its own real category archive page.
  3. Below that, show the full chronological list of blog previews (newest first) — don't repeat the category links again partway down ("don't over-design... don't try to do anything too crazy").
  4. Cap the chronological list at roughly the most recent 50 posts.
  5. Set this up immediately, even with a single published post — don't wait for a post-count threshold.
  - Keep blog categories to fewer than 10 total. [105]
- Bad blog-title pattern: phrasing nobody actually searches (e.g., "Choosing the right company for dump truck rental in New Jersey"). Good pattern: matches real search phrasing (e.g., "Is renting a dump truck the right choice?"). [105]
- A burst of posts (e.g., 3-in-a-row after a long gap) can signal an agency padding activity to justify/retain a contract rather than genuine content strategy — a "Key Takeaways" H2 on every post can likewise tip off that the copy is ChatGPT-written with no real editing pass. [105]

## Tradeoffs & Nuance

- **A technically "broken" page should stay broken if it's already working.** Duplicate H1s, keyword stuffing, non-standard structure — none of it matters if the page ranks and converts; fixing "for correctness" risks breaking what's driving business. This is treated as close to an absolute rule across all four calls, with UX/CTA polish carved out as the one still-safe category of change.
- **A clean audit-tool pass is not proof of a healthy page.** Heading-duplication checks, responsiveness, and page speed are independent axes — a page can pass one and fail another entirely (theatregym.co.uk passed heading audit, failed responsiveness).
- **More words is not inherently better or worse** — the signal is whether length matches what's already ranking; the same underlying rule cuts in opposite directions depending on the case (Anthony's client needed less, James needed more).
- **PR/press-release backlinks and Chamber of Commerce membership have no house verdict** — purely outcome-based, judged case by case, not by category reputation.
- **Rank-and-rent/no-address local sites face a structurally harder, longer path** now that Google expects real local trust signals — not impossible, just slower than it used to be.
- **Big-brand and `.gov` exceptions mean "copy the #1 result" isn't universal advice** — some pages rank purely on domain trust, not technique, and shouldn't be used as a content/structure template.
- **Disavowing is framed as strictly worse than direct-removal-or-dilution** — even an objectively bad-looking link (high spam score, "link farm" warning) might be contributing real positive signal; the recommended path is always identify-and-remove-at-the-source or negotiate-removal, never disavow.
- **Removing a bad link via outreach isn't free or guaranteed goodwill** — some owners demand payment (~$50/link) rather than remove for free; the "clean" alternative to disavowing can still cost money.
- **Small-town/suburb landing pages are case-by-case** — worthless with no search volume behind the town name, genuinely valuable where hyperlocal names carry income/status signal (DC suburbs) that broader county names don't.
- **Tool-reported local rankings shouldn't be trusted at face value** — always sanity-check local/map position against a real search from the target geography.
- **Swapping a homepage for an already-ranking sub-page is explicitly risky** — the mitigation is monitor-and-revert, not avoiding the test.
- **Consolidating multiple branded domains under one authority hub is a clear SEO win, but can conflict with real brand/operational-separation reasons** — the resolution modeled was to keep businesses operationally distinct while still stopping the cross-linking/traffic dilution between them.
- **Content → backlinks → speed sequencing is a deliberate prioritization, not a claim that speed doesn't matter** — it's deferred only until the bigger keyword/content problems are fixed.
- **Low-volume/low-difficulty keywords compound** — no single 10-20-search-a-month term matters on its own, but the accumulation (avalanche method) builds the authority that later makes harder terms rankable, and even feeds LLM-recommendation visibility.
- **Elaborate SEO tracking/portfolio-management systems are "SEO adjacent," not SEO itself** — useful for staying organized and moving, but time spent building the tracking framework is explicitly distinguished from time spent on keyword research, content, or link building, which are the actual ranking levers.
- **Video/description-link tactics assume a strong existing asset** — embedding video or linking from a YouTube description is only worth doing because the underlying video already performs (high view count); it isn't a reason to produce new video from scratch.
- **Translating content is not automatically worth the effort** — verify real search demand in the target language/locale first; assumed demand can be wrong.
