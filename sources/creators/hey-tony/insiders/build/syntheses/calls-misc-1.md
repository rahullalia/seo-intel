## Keyword Research

### Process (validated across three sources — merge of the fullest versions)
1. Get a tool: **Semrush**, **Ubersuggest**, or **Ahrefs** if you don't have one.
2. Set the tool's **country to the one you want to rank in**, not necessarily where you live (example: Dr. Karen practices from Newfoundland but tool is set to US since 75% of her clients are American).
3. Search your broad head term first (e.g. "natural family planning," "website design," "heat caps"). Google it or search directly in-tool; open the **#1 and #2 organic** (never paid/sponsored) results as competitor benchmarks.
4. Pull up **Keyword Ideas**/suggestions for that seed term — variations, volume, keyword difficulty (KD).
5. Click into the highest-volume variant to see its top-ranking page, then open **SERP analysis** ("the most underutilized part of Semrush") — shows page authority score, referring domains, backlinks, traffic, and total keyword count per ranking URL.
6. Open a top competitor's full **keyword report** (everything that URL ranks for) and manually scan for themed sub-niches/recurring modifiers (e.g. "deep conditioning cap," "microwavable").
7. Build a dedicated **keyword list** in-tool ("Create new empty list") named around the emerging theme; add every relevant variant/synonym, including near-duplicates; use the tool's search/filter bar to catch synonyms you'd otherwise miss.
8. Open the **Keyword Strategy Builder** on that saved list to see aggregated total volume + difficulty across the whole list — this is where a single low-volume term becomes part of a much larger addressable pool.
9. Repeat against a second/third competitor's full keyword list to make sure nothing is missed; keep adding new relevant terms to the same list.
10. Use the resulting theme(s) to build content: target the highest-volume terms directly; treat the rest as supporting concepts worked into alt text variety, subheadings, and on-page callouts — not stuffed word-for-word onto one page. **"I don't like to be like, oh, just one keyword specifically to me. I build these into lists."** / **"Using it more as like a concept — you don't want to jam all of these in, but you have them to use."**
11. For long exported lists (~100 terms), upload to **ChatGPT** and ask it to extract underlying themes — faster than grouping visually term-by-term.
- **Iteration framing**: "just type it into Google and then try and research your competitors and see what keywords they're ranking for. And then you can even take those keywords and run them through and see who ranks for those ones. And you just kind of keep iterating that process." Never guess: **"Always follow the keyword research and never guess. Never"** — even against your own pride/attachment to a name.

### Thresholds & rules
- **Keyword difficulty**: ideal range **0-15/20%**; up to **25% "still possible"**; 31% = "a little more difficult than I would like"; 100% (e.g. "website design," no location, 60,000 US searches/mo) = **"never going to rank"** — "pigs get fat and hogs get slaughtered."
- **Long-tail** defined as "more than three words long... very specific" — preferred over head terms.
- **Zero search volume is not a disqualifier** if difficulty is near-zero — still worth a dedicated page (Mark's 0-volume/7%-difficulty location keyword; a hyper-local painter term at volume 0/KD 9 still recommended because the business already ranks in the Map Pack for it). Tools can under-report/show 0 for legitimate hyper-local searches — that's tool under-tracking, not proof nobody searches it.
- **Rough volume floor for low-authority/new sites** (not a hard rule, depends on site authority/backlinks): product pages **≥ ~30 monthly volume**, collection pages **≥ ~50 monthly volume** — can go lower for a brand-new site.
- If a keyword shows **"N/A" difficulty**, refresh/update the tool's metrics (~1 min) to get a real score rather than treating it as zero (a term went from N/A to 38% after refreshing).
- **Referring domains, not raw backlink count**, is the metric to check on competitor pages — if the actual #1-ranked page for your target keyword has 0 referring domains, that's proof you can rank it without any backlink building (sellmyhousefast.com had 2 referring domains/35 backlinks; the actual #1 page for Mark's target term had 0 referring domains). "I don't like to look at the backlinks metric. I like to look at referring domains... Backlinks are expensive. They're time consuming to get. If I can avoid it, I will avoid it at all costs."
- **Complementary/lightweight keyword-vetting**: paste a candidate phrase into Ubersuggest or Semrush just to sanity-check volume/difficulty before committing a page to it.
- **Keyword gap analysis** (your site vs. a competitor's in Semrush) surfaces topics they rank for that you don't — a direct source of new content ideas, alongside AnswerThePublic.

### Tool comparison & cost
- **Semrush** — Matt Demers's and Matt Diamante's primary tool; ≈**$150/month** (Diamante's estimate); features: Keyword Ideas, Questions tab, SERP analysis, per-URL keyword reports, custom keyword lists, Keyword Strategy Builder, keyword-gap analysis, an "update metrics" refresh for N/A-difficulty keywords, referring-domains metric. Demers mentions a lifetime deal ("too good of a deal that I could pass for sure").
- **Ahrefs** — "light" version ≈**$29/month** (per attendee Alex, unverified firsthand by Matt Diamante) — viable budget option for a solo owner.
- **Ubersuggest** (Neil Patel) — "the cheapest tool out there," roughly **$20-30/month**; comparable question-surfacing to Answer The Public. **Caution: volume data may run inflated** — in a 4-way comparison on the same keyword (Ubersuggest vs. Ahrefs vs. Semrush vs. SE Ranking), Ubersuggest showed **~3,000** searches/mo vs. Ahrefs' **750** and Semrush's **600** ("it might be grouping stuff together") — still "better than guessing" if it's all you can afford. One live example: "which tile is best for bathroom walls" → Ubersuggest volume **10**/difficulty **5** ("low... chances of ranking pretty high") vs. Semrush showing difficulty **NA** for the same term (i.e., essentially no competitive data).
- **Answer The Public** (answerthepublic.com) — free; **1 free search/day** with no account, **3 free searches/day** with a free account (no credit card); surfaces question-phrased variants but no volume/difficulty scoring. Go broad on the seed term (typing bare "tiles" returned nothing; "bathroom tile"/"kitchen tile" worked). Scroll **past page 1** (highest-volume, most competitive) to page 2-3, targeting questions around **10-20 search volume**. "People are greedy. People want the whole pizza. They don't... want a slice, they want to take the whole thing" — the rationale for not competing for the biggest question.
- **Free fallback with no paid tool**: type the keyword straight into Google and read "people also ask."
- **pageaudit.com** (Matt Diamante's own free tool) — two-pass workflow: (1) paste URL, leave target keyword blank, Analyze → pulls Content tab data (meta title, meta description, H1/H2/H3 tags, total word count); (2) re-run with the actual target keyword entered → returns keyword usage count and keyword density %.
- **characterCounter.net** — manual cross-check for title-tag/description character counts and word counts against tool output.
- **Detailed SEO** — free browser extension pulling on-page SEO data (headings, alt text, word count) live; worked on WordPress but was mysteriously blocked/vanished on at least one Shopify site during a call, no root cause found.
- **On Page SEO AI** — tool Matt Demers helped build/develop with its creator; combines ~4 SEO tools into one; auto-generates page title + meta description from existing on-page content when logged in (possibly included free-tier). Cross-tool discrepancy found live: showed **136 characters** for a meta description where Semrush showed **63** for the identical URL — unresolved; underscores that the **CMS back end is the real source of truth**, not any third-party checker.
- **Image Alt Text Viewer** — audits all images/alt text/file names on a page in one view.

## On-Page / Money Page Optimization

### Core checklist (merged from all sources — the canonical list)
Keyword must appear in: product/page name (= H1/SEO title), meta title, meta description, **first 100 words** of the copy, naturally throughout the body, **at least one subheading**, an FAQ section, image alt text, and the image **file name** — plus a clean URL slug. Place the keyword **as close to the front as possible** in title tag, meta description, and H1.

### Heading tag rules (merged — the single biggest recurring topic)
- **Exactly one H1 per page**, and it must be the **first heading on the page**; all headings run in strict sequential order (H1 → H2 → H2... → H3 nested under relevant H2s). A page with 3 H1s (one empty) is a defect to fix.
- **Common cause of accidental extra H1s**: manually pressing Enter mid-headline to force a line break creates two separate H1 tags — fix by shrinking the container/box so text wraps naturally instead of forcing a break. A heading spanning more than one visual line can also register as two H1s.
- **Don't ship a different H1 for mobile vs. desktop** via responsive show/hide — Matt tried this after copying a #1-ranked competitor's apparent trick and now suspects Google penalizes it as "gaming the system" (flagged as something he may need to undo on his own site). Lesson: copying a competitor's "trick" isn't automatically safe — correlation (they rank #1 and do X) isn't causation (X is why).
- **Non-heading UI/page furniture must never sit inside or interrupt the heading sequence**: "Related products" carousels, "Items added to your cart" modules, cart/price/quantity selectors are commonly auto-marked H2 by ecommerce themes — demote to **H3, or H4 at the highest** (flag for a developer if it requires theme-code editing). Any H3s found in the **footer** should become plain paragraph text or **H5** — footer content should never compete in the heading hierarchy.
- **Rewording, not stacking**: if a page already has 9-10+ H2s, don't cram the keyword into yet another one — reword an existing generic H2 into a keyword-carrying one (e.g. "Why buy from us" → "Why is our 5-HTP Plus formula different?"). Mirror the H1 wording in at least one H2 so the page reads consistently (H1 "Stink File Number One / Series One Bundle" → H2 "Series One Bundle Includes...").
- **AI-assisted heading drafting**: ask ChatGPT for **2-3 H2 tags that support the H1** — use or adapt them as the page's actual headings.
- **Exception — if a page already ranks #1, leave it alone.** Verbatim: "I don't give a shit if you have 31 heading one tags when you should only have one. If you have 31 of them and you're ranking number one, I'm not touching that page."

### Title tag & meta description
- **Title tag: under 60 characters** — gets cut off past that. AI-draft prompt: *"Write me a title tag for this page under 60 characters. The focus keyword is [X]."*
- **Meta description: under ~155-160 characters** — old rule of thumb was 140, but Yoast has since started cutting descriptions off shorter, so exact precision matters less than before; Google increasingly **rewrites meta descriptions on its own** regardless of what's set on-page ("that's been happening a lot lately"), further reducing how much character-count precision actually matters.
- Shopify-specific bug: SEO titles auto-append the store name after a dash (product name + dash + company name) — there's an account-level setting to disable this (ask ChatGPT to locate it); **caution**: flipping it retroactively affects every product page's title at once, so confirm every page is already correctly formatted before toggling.
- **Diagnosing a duplicate/wrong meta description shown by two different audit tools**: treat the CMS back end as source of truth; Cmd+F (Mac)/Ctrl+F (Windows) the page's source/content to find exactly where the live description is actually being pulled from, then correct it there.

### Word count & keyword density — "level plus one"
- Figure out precisely what the top-ranking competitor is doing numerically, then do **very slightly more** — not dramatically more. Live example: competitor used "web design" **45 times at 1.76% density** (≈2,563 total words); Matt's rewrite plan = **~2,600 words** (add ~37) and use the keyword **46 times**. **"I want to do what is proven that Google likes. I just want to do it like 0.0001% more."** This is proportional matching, not license to maximize — explicitly not "use the keyword 100 times and write 10,000 words."
- Case that prompted the rule: Matt's own "website design in Hamilton" page fell from #1 to **#7**. Diagnosis: 2 H1 tags (mobile/desktop hack, see above); only **695 words** vs. a #2 competitor's **1,200**; keyword density **5.04% (35 uses)** of "website design" — called "way too many... insane" vs. competitors using "web design" 45x/1.76% and 9x/0.7%. Insight surfaced: "web design" (not "website design") appeared to be the phrasing Google/top competitors actually optimize around.
- **Verify tool-reported word counts manually** — copy the visible page text into a plain word counter and compare against what an audit extension reports; large discrepancies mean the tool is scraping hidden elements (popups, rewards-program text, recommended-products widgets, cart/search boxes). Live example: an audit tool reported **808 words** on a page where a manual count of visible text showed only **511** — the gap traced to a hidden rewards-program popup and other non-body elements, not real content. A related keyword-count discrepancy on the same page: tool said 13 uses of the focus phrase, manual re-verification found 11 actual on-page uses (H1 + body), with a further check landing at ~12 when hidden "recommended products" widget text was counted — vs. the top competitor's page using the exact-match phrase only **3 times** (5 times counting customer reviews).

### Images & alt text
- **Image sourcing priority**: owned/real photos first, then stock, then AI — **except graphs/graphics specifically, where AI goes to the top of the list** ("they make really great graphics").
- Every image needs **alt text — none should be empty**, and it must be **specific per variant**, not the same generic word repeated across every image of a product line (found live: every diamond-shape photo — round, cushion, pear, emerald, radiant — had identical alt text just saying "diamond," called a "missed opportunity"; fix = shape-specific alt text + matching file names).
- **Rename the actual image file** (not just alt text) to include the target keyword **before uploading** — e.g. "black-flame-paddle-shirt.jpg" not "1234.jpg." Google reads the literal file name as a distinct signal from alt text. Framed as a low-priority quick win, not something that "ranks you to the top of Google" on its own.
- Alt text exists for screen-reader users first — it should be real, descriptive text; legitimate to include a location detail (e.g. "exterior house paint, Springfield, Massachusetts") but don't just stuff a bare place name repeatedly with no real description. **Geotagging images before upload** might give "a slight edge" but probably doesn't move the needle much — descriptive alt text matters more.
- **Auditing for stuffing/fakes**: use an alt-text viewer tool to see every image + current alt text + file name on a page in one place; also check page source directly (search for the keyword) to confirm it's genuinely populating the `alt` attribute vs. some hidden field/class.

### Diagnosing "why isn't this ranking"
Compare directly against the #1-ranking competitor across: content depth/findability, heading tag count/structure, content length, keyword density, page speed, alt text, hidden-text/keyword-stuffing, and internal links. **Don't blindly copy a competitor's keyword-stuffing just because they rank** — if their keyword has near-zero real competition, they didn't need to stuff to rank, and copying the stuffing risks a penalty on your own site for no benefit ("you don't want to stuff keywords like these guys are ranking, even though they've stuffed keywords, because nobody's trying to go after that keyword... but if you do everything properly, you should be able to outrank them or at least be on the first page").

### Priority ordering
**Broken links are a bigger priority than an over-length title tag** — fix broken links first. Character-count/heading-count "errors" are low-severity (fix because "any variable we can control" is worth controlling) but not urgent like broken links or missing alt text.

### High-risk edits — proceed with caution
- **Never change a URL slug that already has organic traffic/sales** — even to fix an obvious typo — unless someone can properly implement a 301 redirect; if unclear, leave it alone. "So I would say update this with extreme caution." Even a textbook-correct 301 doesn't always fully preserve traffic: "In odd instances, I've had it — we do everything right and the traffic still just doesn't carry over the way it's supposed to."
- **Never put a year/date in an evergreen page's URL or title** (e.g. "best dental services in 2025") — it goes stale as years pass.
- **One keyword/topic per page — never optimize two pages for the same target.** Doing so "confuses Google... they're just like, these guys are trying to double dip. We're only going to show the one page... and that other page becomes useless... none of them are gonna rank." This is exactly what the platform's Cannibalized Keywords Scanner checks for.
- Don't force the exact-match keyword into a sentence that sounds unnatural spoken aloud (wrong example given: "Tampa Bay Boat Rental Tampa") — use natural variations; Google and ChatGPT both treat variations as equivalent to the exact keyword. Singular vs. plural matters too — use whichever form the keyword-research data shows people actually search.
- **Duplicated/rendered content across responsive screen-size variations**: tolerated if you already rank #1 for the term; risky if you don't. "If you're already ranking number one, Google likes that you did that, but if you're not, then Google doesn't like that."

## AI-Assisted Content Writing Workflow

### Full "AI blog post that ranks with zero backlinks" method (step-by-step, ~30 min solo / 40-70 min with Q&A)
1. Go to **answerthepublic.com**; type the broadest version of what you sell/do (broad beats specific for seeding).
2. Click **Search Engines → Questions**.
3. Scroll **past** the top (highest-volume, most competitive) questions to page 2-3, targeting **~10-20 search volume**.
4. Pick a question you can personally answer, that's specific, and fits your business.
5. Copy it into a blank doc (title the doc after the question).
6. Paste a prepared prompt into ChatGPT/Claude, swap in your question — built to return **only the 10 most important interview questions**; explicitly cap it ("keep it limited to 10 questions") or it defaults to asking 30-40. Reconstructed gist: *"here are 10 sharp questions that will let me write a killer, story-driven, unique, uniquely-mapped blog post on [topic], with your expertise, wild examples and case studies, energy baked in."* Alternate exact prompt used live: *"Ask me any questions you need to know the answers to to make this helpful, unique, and include my expertise, experience, and opinion"* — called "a really great prompt... to create a truly unique piece of content."
7. Answer the 10 questions — **2-3 sentences each is enough**, doesn't need correct grammar, budget **~1 min/question**. Include real expertise, case studies, specific examples, "family secret" details — the goal is content that doesn't already exist anywhere online. Slight repetition across answers is fine.
   - **Voice-mode option**: answer via ChatGPT's voice mode on your phone instead of typing — but answer **one question at a time** and **do not tap the mic again until it has fully stopped**, or you risk silently losing the transcript. Typing is more reliable if you don't want to risk losing answers.
   - **Personal/sensitive-topic workaround**: if a question is too personal/sensitive for AI to answer on your behalf, write that answer yourself and use ChatGPT only to build the structural framework around it.
8. Prompt: *"Write me a blog post based on my answers."*
9. Copy into your CMS (WordPress → Posts → Add New, or platform-equivalent). Clean up for a human reader: strip stray formatting artifacts (vertical lines/dividers), remove non-sequential list numbering, **remove emojis** (a "gratuitous amount" of emojis anywhere reads as AI-written — cut back; never put emojis in **heading tags** specifically, since that pollutes what Google reads as the heading text), remove self-promotional/CTA-style subheads that don't belong in a pure answer post.
10. Set title and URL slug around the core question/topic — pick whichever AI-generated title reads best, don't default to the literal question text. Save as **draft**, then **Preview** to check rendering.
11. **Internal linking, phase 1 (pre-publish)**: Google `site:yourdomain.com "keyword"` (no space after colon, phrase in quotes) using a keyword relevant to the new post. Open 3 of the surfaced pages, find matching anchor text in your new draft, hyperlink each to one of those 3 pages.
12. **Publish.**
13. **Internal linking, phase 2 (post-publish)**: run the same `site:` search again to find **3 different pages** (not the same 3). Edit each of those older pages, find matching anchor text, add a link **from** each of them **to** the new post — gets already-indexed pages (which Google will recrawl anyway) pointing at the new URL, speeding discovery. "This is a trick that a lot of people don't talk about... it's honestly like a huge, huge, huge advantage for you."
14. **Cover image**: upload existing on-site images to ChatGPT/Claude, prompt: *"make me a cover photo for this blog post [in] the style of the images attached."* Optional originality guard: *"make sure not to include any of the exact images that are in these. Make new ones... but in that style."*
15. Set **1-2 categories** (never leave "Uncategorized"), leave tags/hashtags/keywords fields **empty**, hit Publish.
16. Confirm heading hierarchy (H1 → H2 → H2 → H2, H3s nested under relevant H2s) via a Page Audit tool/extension before/after publishing.

### Client-content workarounds
- **Can't personally answer the AI's questions** (client-specific answers needed): take the AI-generated question list into a **recorded Zoom call with the client** (20-30 min), ask them live (can be up to ~50 questions in one call), feed the transcript to ChatGPT to generate the post(s). "You literally can look like a genius by just asking the questions that ChatGPT gives you to your client on a Zoom call... Don't tell your client that it doesn't [take long] — it only takes like 10 minutes to do." You don't need 20 questions per post — ask the most pertinent ones and reuse across multiple pieces.
- **Hard-to-reach clients**: batch multiple interview-call transcripts into a single uploaded file so ChatGPT can cross-reference earlier answers/case studies when writing new pieces and link back to previously-discussed examples.
- **Full page-copy rewrite variant**: feed ChatGPT the focus keyword + a generated H2 outline and ask it to write the page copy — treat as a rough first pass only; manually verify every factual claim it invents (e.g. "we offer free quotes," a painting business "testing every major paint brand") before publishing. "Don't publish anything that you can't stand behind."
- **Ecommerce collection-page FAQ variant**: reuse the same interview-prompt style — "I'm writing a collection page for [product], I need some FAQ questions underneath it" — and let the AI pull from existing owned material (e.g. the owner's book).

### AI content ranking — the acceptable/unacceptable line
- **AI-generated content is NOT a ranking disadvantage.** "A common misconception is that you can't use ChatGPT written content or AI written content because you're not going to rank. That's simply not true. We've been doing it for our clients for like, basically since ChatGPT came out. And they rank." Live proof: Matt's own post ("SEO when building a website," "I used AI to do like 99% of this," ~30 min total) ranked **#1** on his own machine, **#2** on Semrush (Canada-based check) with **zero backlinks, zero referring domains** — beating Squarespace, Reddit, Shopify, Wix, and bdc.ca in the SERP.
- **The unacceptable use is mass unedited publishing at unrealistic volume**: 300-500 AI-generated blog posts published in a single day (or even a month) is unrealistic for any site, even one with a full writing staff — Google reads it as gaming the system. One case (Sam T, escape rooms) had an estimated 3,000-5,000 such fake-name/fabricated-info pages, ~2,000 actually indexed. "Most of us are like, that's complete bullshit. It doesn't work. Google knows that you're trying to game it."
- Origin case study for the whole "answer real questions" strategy: **Marcus Sheridan / "They Ask, You Answer"** — manually (no AI, no Answer the Public) answered every question he heard daily from customers with a dedicated blog post, took his pool-installation business from "on the brink of bankruptcy" to an "**over a nine-figure exit**." Today's AI-interview method is the same idea sped up with tools.
- Method-comparison note: this Answer-the-Public-plus-interview method is fast/easy to demo live, not claimed superior to the other taught method ("writing content that ranks" from high-ranking article summaries/outlines) — effectiveness varies by industry/keyword, pick per situation.

## Internal Linking

- **The rule (merged/corrected version)**: when publishing a **new** blog post, link out from it to **3 old blog posts + 1 money page**. Then separately go back and **edit 3 different old blog posts** to add a link pointing into the new post. (Not: link the same 3 old posts both directions — it's 3 outbound + 3 separate inbound, 6 total edits across old content plus the new post.)
- **Mechanic for finding link targets**: `site:yourdomain.com "keyword"` (no space after colon, phrase in quotes) in Google, using a keyword relevant to the new post's topic — run once pre-publish (find 3 pages to link the new post TO) and once post-publish (find 3 different pages to add links FROM, pointing at the new post).
- If you don't yet have 3 existing posts to link to/from, **1 link is acceptable for now** — build out the rest as you publish more content over time; this isn't a blocker.
- **Sequencing flexibility**: fine to publish before internal links are fully added, but go back and add them **immediately** — "it's very, very important," not optional.
- **Don't use a sponsored/paid Google Ads result** as an internal-linking target — only organic results count.
- **Social media links to your own page don't count as internal/backlink equivalents** — "social media links don't really count for much."
- Extends to ecommerce: same blog-to-page (and page-to-blog) linking logic applies to product pages, not just blog posts — link "how to use/clean/care for" content to and from the relevant product page. For anchor text: link to product pages using the target keyword in the anchor, but only when it reads naturally — never force it. **"Best of" roundup blog posts** (e.g. "Best Paddle Shirts for Rain," "Best Tennis Shirts") are the recommended internal-linking vehicle — list your own products with keyword-rich anchors and an image for each (supports the internal link).
- **Mentioning/linking competitor products** inside these roundups is fine, often best practice, especially for items outside your own catalog — Google reads it as an honest, non-self-serving recommendation ("Google views this as an honest interpretation... you're not just only doing your own [products]. It's a style."). Skip for clients who explicitly opt out of naming competitors.

## Ecommerce-Specific SEO

- **Collection vs. product page keyword split**: go broad on the collection page (e.g. "Paddle T-Shirts"); go specific/attribute-based on product pages (material, style — "polyester paddle shirt," "silk paddle shirt").
- **Collection pages lacking content**: don't add copy above the product grid (visitors came to see products) — add an info section **below** the grid instead, as plain H2 questions (not labeled "FAQ") answered in natural human language that naturally reincorporates the keyword a few more times (e.g. "How long does the temporary tattoo last?" / "Can I shower with a temporary tattoo?"). For pages with only a few/near-duplicate SKUs, treat the whole collection page almost like a blog post: mini-blog + FAQ block underneath the grid, letting AI pull from existing owned material (e.g. the owner's book).
- **FAQs**: build as collapsible rows/content blocks (Shopify theme editor has a native "collapsible row" component — no custom dev needed). Roll out to **collection pages first** (broadest reach, far less labor) before individual product FAQs, which become a huge project at full-catalog scale. Two payoffs: preempt purchase-blocking questions (conversion) and feed AI/long-tail search, since people now type full-sentence queries, not 3-4 word ones ("Everyone's not typing in just like four words anymore... the more context you can have on the page, the better").
- **Product naming — real searched terms over invented/brand names**: type the plain physical/functional description into a keyword tool (ignore the cute/branded name), find the phrase with real volume and low/no competition, and rename the **actual product title** (not just alt text) to that phrase. Case examples: a custom hat maker's "cream beaver fur" hat under a made-up name → found ~50-100 monthly searches with no competition for the plain material description. Jackie's Irish-language candle names → repositioned as "[Scent Descriptor] Soy Candle" (e.g. "Vanilla Soy Candle," append "blend" if multiple notes present), Irish name moved to the end; SEMrush showed the vanilla-soy-candle-type query at ~**30 searches/month** ("if you sold 30 candles a month, you'd be very happy"). Lead the title with the real keyword, append the invented/brand name at the end — never the reverse.
- **Duplicate content across near-identical SKUs/locations**: for identical descriptions reused across near-duplicate contexts (e.g. an escape-room game description reused for an Anaheim booking page and a Connecticut booking page), differentiating just the **title tag and meta description** per instance (e.g. "Booking — Anaheim" vs. "Booking — Connecticut") can be sufficient — body copy can stay similar in this kind of case. (Contrast with the stricter "genuinely unique body copy" rule for full location pages — see Tradeoffs.)
- **Making plain-text CTAs clickable** (e.g. "Book your free consultation" written as static text, not a link) — not an SEO fix, but an easy conversion win to flag and fix.
- **Multi-niche stores** (e.g. one shop selling tennis, paddle/padel, and horseback riding apparel together) create real keyword-research friction — each niche needs its own research and doesn't share an audience — but can still work under a unifying frame (all "sports"); Matt calls it explicitly "a good thing and a bad thing."

## Local & Multi-Location SEO

- **One dedicated page per city/county/service area** — never optimize two pages for the same topic/keyword. Build a "Locations" nav tab with one page per town, each targeting exactly one town's keyword variant.
- **Keyword-first phrasing**: head term first, geography second — "Sell your house fast, Delaware, Ohio," not "Looking to sell your Delaware home quickly." Matt's diagnosis for location pages that aren't ranking is usually "they're not different enough or you're not targeting the right keyword."
- **Genuinely unique body copy per location page** — not the same template with only the city swapped; this is the real cannibalization risk (multiplying pages isn't itself the risk — duplicate/templated copy across them is).
- **City-size sizing rule** for whether to target a whole city or go neighborhood-level: a ~500,000-population city (Hamilton) has roughly **15,000 small businesses** — enough to justify the whole city as the geo-modifier. A 3-4 million population metro (Toronto) is too big — target a **neighborhood** instead, since people won't travel across a huge city for a local service.
- **Hyper-local terms can show 0 recorded search volume** in keyword tools even when legitimate — small town names are under-tracked, not un-searched; don't discard a term solely because it shows 0, especially paired with very low difficulty.
- **Local-service non-local-traffic tradeoff**: don't force-localize every blog post to your own city; broader-reach content (attracting visitors from outside your service area, who'll never convert) still helps because it lifts total traffic/keyword-ranking breadth, which is itself an authority signal that helps rank the searches that DO matter locally. "If I'm getting traffic from five towns over, somebody's not going to hire me. That's totally fine. That is helping my website show up higher in my local area because I'm getting more traffic and I'm ranking for more keywords and I'm helping more people."

## Site Health, Technical Cleanup & Black-Hat Recovery

- **Black-hat/programmatic spam cleanup workflow** (full sequence):
  1. Check Google Search Console for which spam pages are getting clicks/impressions.
  2. Any getting **zero clicks** → move to **DRAFT** status (never delete — keeps the option to restore).
  3. Because spam pages are typically internally cross-linked via plugins, drafting them removes the links along with the pages.
  4. Rerun the site audit tool afterward — surfaces any remaining broken links pointing at now-drafted pages.
  5. Resume a normal cadence of **1-2 new posts/week**.
  - **Explicitly do NOT**: manually 404 the pages, set up redirects for them, or notify Google/request a recrawl. "If your SEO is already terrible, then you can't really do worse." / "Let's say you're at zero and you can just delete a bunch of shit, and then hopefully that brings you in the right direction."
- **Fixing site-health/red flags at a sustainable pace**: batch **5-10 fixes per week** rather than clearing everything at once — fixing one instance of a systemic issue often auto-resolves it site-wide (e.g. a theme-level setting). Explicit burnout-prevention framing: "Don't try to optimize and fix all the issues on your website. Fix 5 to 10 a week, and you'll be astounded at how much progress you make and how not stressed you are about it because you're not burning out."
- **Audit-flag triage**: if a flagged page is genuinely traffic/ranking-irrelevant, don't burn time on every flag — but public back-end taxonomy/utility pages should still be made non-indexable regardless of traffic, since Google associates low-quality indexed pages with the whole domain. Broken-link flags pointing at social platforms (e.g. LinkedIn) are usually just bots being blocked — safe to manually validate or ignore.
- **Orphan pages auto-created by forms/quizzes** (e.g. a "find an expert" quiz that auto-generates a public page per answer-combo): these get indexed and flagged as orphans even though never manually created. Fix via a ChatGPT/Claude prompt: *"here's my situation, here's my theme, here's what I'm doing, all this stuff — how do I prevent these pages from existing but still have that information on the front end."*
- **Diagnosing an orphan page**: test whether it's reachable from the homepage without pasting the direct URL; if only reachable via a buried subcategory nav, that's the likely cause. If unresolved, escalate to a technical call — could be a canonical/duplicate-URL-version issue depending on platform.
- **Categories/tags**: on a typical business site, tags "most situations they do not make sense" — each spins up a thin, content-less orphan page. Use categories instead: **5-10 categories total** for a whole site, **1-2 categories per post** (often just 1), never leave a post "Uncategorized." **Legitimate exception**: a resource-library-style site that structurally needs tag/category-based on-site search/filtering — in that case, **no-index the thin tag/category pages** rather than remove the functionality (Google ignores them anyway, but audit tools keep flagging them); consider trimming excess tags later.
- **JavaScript-heavy WordPress sites**: install **WP Rocket**, use its one-click minimize/minify/defer-JS toggles; if a toggle breaks something visually, just untoggle that one. (Billing reportedly shifted from one-time to yearly.) **GoDaddy** has no equivalent JS-optimization plugin option — "there's nothing you could do with GoDaddy."
- **Platform migration** (Wix/Squarespace → WordPress, etc.): keep every URL identical, plus the same heading tags and content, even if visual design changes. If a URL structure must change (e.g. `/blog` → `/news`), set up 301 redirects mapping old → new for every page.
- **Repositioning/rebranding without losing SEO equity**: don't rebuild from scratch — rewrite/simplify existing pages in place, keep old blog URLs live (Google is still crawling them if indexed), and link new/revamped pages FROM the older already-indexed posts so Google discovers them faster.
- **Site redesign/relaunch discovery speed**: post about the new site on social media and email people the link — more real interaction/traffic signals help Google find and crawl it faster. If the old version never ranked, don't bother preserving anything — just start fresh.
- **No platform is "more SEO-optimized" than another** — WordPress has the most/easiest options, Shopify comparable, Wix/Squarespace slightly fewer options but still fully rankable. "There's no one platform that is better optimized than another platform." **WordPress.com** (the hosted blogging service) is the one real exception — it's a blogging service, not suited to a full SEO-optimized business site; migrate to **WordPress.org** instead (content can be imported over). **SmugMug** is also flagged as SEO-unfriendly for photographers — templated image URLs (pattern like "image12345") get canonicalized/treated as duplicates by Google; **Ghost** was named positively instead for clean image handling and alt-text support. **GoHighLevel (GHL)** as a website host is workable but not the personal recommendation unless already tied into your CRM — better dedicated hosting exists.
- **Crawl budget**: no official Google documentation on how it's calculated. Working theory: sites showing real traffic, time-on-page, and multi-page sessions earn progressively more crawl budget; sites with thousands of unvisited pages risk having crawl budget throttled.
- **Schema markup / "optimizing specifically for AI search"** is largely unnecessary as a separate initiative — ranking well organically on Google tends to carry over into AI answer engines since they lean on similar signals. "What we've found is if you rank high on Google and you do all that work to get there, you'll likely rank in all these other AIs too." **Index Now**: "kind of like BS... I don't think it really does anything" — don't rely on it.
- **A site must be live to run real on-page optimization** — if still mid-build with developers, fill out the optimization worksheet (focus keyword, title tag, meta description) now and hand it to the developers so the page launches already optimized.

## Backlinks & Off-Page

- **Quality over quantity, illustrated non-literally**: ~10 backlinks is "not a lot" in absolute terms but meaningful starting from zero. Not all backlinks are equal — a New York Times backlink might be "worth" 100 units of trust vs. a "shop next door" backlink worth 1%: **"You can have a hundred 1%'s, guys, but that still doesn't equal the New York Times backlink."**
- **Sequence**: write content → optimize pages → THEN pursue backlinks. Don't chase paid/bought backlinks early. Backlinks/directories training is gated at **Level 5 ("Expert")** inside Insiders.
- **Easiest first backlinks for a local business with no budget**: join local organizations (chamber of commerce, etc.) with member directories — get a business profile page listed there, that's a backlink.
- **HARO (Help A Reporter Out)** — free; ~3 emails/day of journalist topic requests; workflow: paste the whole email into AI to identify which requests are relevant to your niche. Useful for backlinks/press but "can be overwhelming" — use it, don't obsess over it.
- **Check referring domains, not raw backlink count**, when benchmarking a competitor (see Keyword Research section) — proves whether ranking a term requires backlink-building at all.

## Content Strategy, Cadence & Timeline Patience

- **Blog cadence target: 1-2 long-form posts/week**, ~1,000-1,200 words called "perfect" length. If only one/week is sustainable, that's fine — prioritize patience/consistency over volume.
- **New sites**: expect **zero visible ranking movement for the first two weeks to two months** — Google needs months of consistency (e.g. weekly publishing) before trusting a new site isn't a spammer. "Don't feel discouraged because every website that launches, like the first two weeks, the first two months, you're not seeing anything."
- **Most people quit SEO after ~3 months** without seeing results — the long game wins. Case in point: Andrea (worship flags) already ranks #1-2 for her broad head term and attributes it to sheer tenure ("I've just outlasted everybody").
- **Half-committed effort has little value regardless of timing**: "Starting now versus starting in January — if you're not doing it right, if you're not fully doing it, it's not going to make that much of a difference." Full commitment matters more than starting early but half-heartedly.
- **Content relevance rule**: every piece of content should directly serve the site's core promise. Tangential-but-related topics (e.g. general nutrition content on a fitness/self-defense site) should be folded into an on-topic post as a mention, not published standalone — dilutes Google's read of what the site is about otherwise.
- **Content architecture for multi-practitioner/directory sites**: don't write content about each listed expert's individual specialty — pick ONE overarching "vertical" term as the site's umbrella topic, treat sub-specialties as sub-topics beneath it. Keeps Google's read of site purpose coherent. ("Your site is about connecting podcasters with... people who are guests... Everything on your website should be like, talking about podcasts or podcasters or how to get on a podcast." / "I don't give a shit what type of guests you have and you're not writing content for people, looking them up.") Caveat: a directory doing SEO on behalf of its listed experts (to earn backlinks from their sites) is a different goal than direct organic acquisition for those experts individually — this profile-page approach won't drive new visitors to an expert unless they're already independently well-searched.
- **Editing/republishing old posts alongside new-post cadence is not spammy** — standard practice — but should be prioritized: check Google Search Console for posts with high impressions but zero clicks and optimize those first, rather than blanket-redoing every old post ("because you could just be wasting your time").
- **Podcast-specific microsites**: keep minimal — homepage, booking/"work with us" page, featured guests page, an embedded always-updating sample (e.g. Spotify playlist embed). Don't build per-episode content pages.
- **Google requires written text** to pull data into search results — video/audio can supplement (better UX/multimedia) but can't replace text for search visibility. "Blogs help Google understand what your website is about."
- **Interpreting Search Console metrics**: don't over-index on "average position" alone — it's an aggregate across every keyword and every ranking area, so a move like 28 → 25 is a weak/noisy signal by itself. Rising impressions with flat/lagging clicks is a sign of mid-funnel progress (more keywords ranking, just not top-of-page-1 yet), compounded by AI Overviews suppressing clicks on informational content generally — don't panic, keep producing content. "Ranking" includes **both** the local 3-pack/map pack **and** organic — even page 3-4 counts as ranking, just poorly, and one tends to feed the other.

## Tradeoffs & Nuance

- **Audit-tool severity vs. business relevance**: a page being "unimportant" doesn't excuse leaving a public back-end taxonomy page indexable — those are worth fixing regardless of traffic; but not every flagged issue on a low-relevance page needs fixing.
- **Backlink volume vs. quality**: absolute counts are meaningless without source authority — one high-authority link can outweigh many low-authority ones combined.
- **Cadence vs. capacity**: 1-2 posts/week is the target, but match it to what's actually sustainable — consistency beats speed.
- **Video/audio vs. text**: valuable UX supplement, never a substitute, since Google's indexing depends on parseable written content.
- **Platform choice is mostly a non-issue** for SEO — the real exceptions are structurally templated hosts (SmugMug's canonical/duplicate URL patterns) and blogging-only platforms (WordPress.com) that can't support a full SEO-optimized business site; GHL is usable but not ideal purely as a host.
- **Rebuild vs. revamp-in-place**: rebuilding risks losing accumulated SEO equity (indexed URLs, backlinks, rankings); revamping in place while preserving old URLs and cross-linking new content from them is the safer path even during a substantial positioning pivot. This flips, though, if the old site never ranked at all — then there's nothing to preserve and a clean rebuild is fine.
- **Refreshing old content should be prioritized, not blanket-applied**: check GSC for high-impression/zero-click posts first rather than redoing every old post ("you could just be wasting your time").
- **Duplicate-content tolerance is context-dependent and shows some tension across calls**: one case (near-identical descriptions across Anaheim/Connecticut escape-room booking pages) was resolved by differentiating only title tag + meta description, leaving body copy similar; but the general location-page rule (Mark's real-estate case, and the broader "avoid duplicate/near-duplicate content" guidance) demands genuinely unique body copy per page, warning that near-duplicate templated copy is the actual cannibalization risk. Read these as: low-stakes/minor-variant pages can get away with lighter differentiation, but a page meant to independently rank for its own local keyword needs real unique content.
- **Tangential-but-related topics** (general nutrition on a fitness site, invented-name products, "custom" as a catch-all attribute) dilute topical focus and confuse Google's read of site purpose — fold the adjacent info into on-topic posts rather than deleting outright or publishing standalone.
- **Programmatic/AI-scaled content**: "some people swear by this" even though most of the community considers mass unedited publishing non-viable — the acceptable use of AI (graphics, drafting assistance, single-post interview-style generation) is clearly distinguished from the unacceptable use (mass unedited publishing at unrealistic volume).
- **Geographic/traffic-source mismatches** (Analytics showing a different city than the actual service area) aren't inherently a problem if inquiries/calls still convert — the real risk to check for is accidental keyword contamination (wrong city name in titles/meta/H1s), not the traffic-source data itself.
- **A directory/marketplace's SEO efforts on behalf of listed experts** (to earn backlinks from those experts' own sites) serves a different goal than direct organic acquisition for those experts individually — won't drive new visitors to an expert unless they're already independently well-searched.
- **Tool-suggested competitor benchmarks** (word count, "should rank #1/#3") are directional, not prescriptive — filter through your actual current ranking position before acting, or risk breaking a page that's already performing.
- **Impressions-up-clicks-flat reassurance is partly unverifiable for most site owners**: the Google feature showing AI Overview appearance counts isn't available to most clients yet ("I have one client who has it. The rest of them don't, which is super annoying."), so the funnel-progress read can't currently be confirmed with hard data for most people.
- **The two platform search mechanisms trade off differently**: the literal top search bar is precise but brittle on multi-word intent; the AI help button handles natural questions better but is limited to what's already in the platform's own content archive.
- **FAQs pay off for conversion and AI/long-tail SEO but are labor-intensive at full-catalog scale** — sequence collection pages first, individual products only selectively.
- **Multi-niche stores** create real per-niche keyword-research friction but can still work under one unifying frame — "a good thing and a bad thing."
- **Title/meta-description length overages are low priority** relative to broken links, empty headings, or missing alt text — worth fixing (every controllable variable helps) but not urgent.
- **URL slug changes are high-risk even when unambiguously "correct"** — weigh existing organic traffic/sales against 301-redirect execution risk; sometimes leaving a flawed-but-ranking URL alone is safer, and even a correct redirect doesn't always fully preserve traffic.
- **Naming competitors in content is generally a trust signal** to Google but is client-specific — honor an explicit no-competitors preference rather than override it.
- **Meta-description length guidance is a moving target**: the old 140-character rule shifted as Yoast changed its cutoff, and Google increasingly rewrites descriptions on its own regardless of on-page settings — reducing how much character-count precision actually matters. Two tools can also disagree on the same live page's character count with no resolution — the CMS back end is the real source of truth, not a third-party checker.
- **Deciding how broadly to apply a keyword theme uncovered through research** (e.g. "microwavable heat cap" born from a 90-volume seed) depends on how much of the actual catalog fits it — site-wide, one collection, or a single product, based on whether most/all relevant products genuinely share the attribute.
- **Matching a top-ranking competitor's tactics isn't automatically safe** — if they're winning via keyword-stuffing/hidden text on a near-zero-competition term, replicating it risks a penalty on your own site when it wasn't even necessary for them to rank in the first place. Same logic applies to copying a mobile/desktop dual-H1 hack — correlation with a #1 rank isn't causation.
- **Duplicate/rendered content across responsive screen sizes** is judged contextually: tolerated if you already rank #1, risky if you don't.
- **Renaming products for SEO can conflict with an owner's pride/branding attachment** — keyword research wins, but get explicit client sign-off before overriding a preferred name rather than doing it unilaterally.
- **"Level plus one" is proportional matching, not a license to maximize** — a small, deliberate increment over what's already proven to work, never "use the keyword 100 times."
- **Client-interview workflows trade specificity for client time**: per-post interviews are higher quality but cost more of the client's time; batching multiple topics into one call (or multiple transcripts into one ChatGPT upload) sacrifices some specificity for dramatically less back-and-forth — most useful for hard-to-reach clients.
- **On-page SEO tooling itself is not fully reliable**: extensions can be blocked for no clear reason on certain platforms (Shopify), and audit tools can significantly overcount word count/keyword usage by scraping hidden popups and widgets — always spot-check manually (plain word counter, page-source search) before acting on tool output.
