# HeyTony Insiders — Diagnostics Synthesis (Group 1)

*Source: 15 WDIR ("Why Doesn't It Rank") live-audit/work-session calls, Matt Diamante, HeyTony Insiders — 01.05.2026 through 17.07.2026. Round-robin format: members bring a live win, question, or underperforming page/site; Matt diagnoses live using Search Console, Semrush/HeyKeywords, and page-audit tools, then assigns a small fix.*

---

## 1. Core Diagnostic Philosophy & Governing Rules

- **If a page is ranking — for anything, even a keyword other than the one you intended — leave it alone.** Stated as close to an absolute rule in nearly every call. Applies even when the page violates every other rule below (duplicate H1s, keyword stuffing 122×, hidden overlay text, zero backlinks, thin content). "If it is ranking, don't touch that page. Even if it's not ranking for the exact keyword that you want to go for or think it should rank for." Matt's own SEO-in-Hamilton service page: built once, minimally optimized, never touched again, hit **#1 after ~1 year** via blog posts + internal linking + anchor text alone — "I asked my team, did anybody touch this page? ... All we did was internal link to it." His own website-design page held **#1 for ~2 years on 600-800 words**, then got outranked once competitors escalated content — *that* moment (not before) is the trigger to redo the competitive audit.
- **If a page is NOT ranking:** wait **3 months**, then **6 months** (doing internal linking with descriptive anchor text in the meantime) before touching the page's actual content again. If still not ranking after 6 months, redo the full competitive audit — check who ranks #1-3, note their word count/topics/structure, then match/rework the page.
- **"Set and forget" has an expiration date.** A page holding #1 for years on relatively little content can quietly lose that position once competitors escalate — long-held rankings still need periodic re-auditing, just never preemptively.
- **Freshness-gaming behavior (Google's patent-level pattern):** when a page is newly published or updated, Google will sometimes purposely suppress ("tank") its ranking temporarily, then wait a few days to recheck whether the publisher panics and makes a flurry of changes — that panic-editing pattern itself reads as a spam/gaming signal. Rule: once a page is live, give it **about a month** before going back to re-update it. Don't knee-jerk react to a ranking dip within days or a week of any edit — that's expected noise.
- **Checking data daily is fine; acting on daily noise is not.** "You can look at it every day, but don't make actions on it every day — because that's how you lose money, that's how you lose credit, that's how you lose sanity." CJ's parallel: "Don't look at your credit score every day. Instead, do the right thing every day... and then 30 days later, without you looking, it's changed and you can buy a house." Applies identically to Search Console/GA, credit scores, and investment accounts. A member who checks rankings 2+ times a day was told directly to stop over-checking and over-requesting indexing.
- **Timelines:** SEO takes **3-6 months** of consistent work minimum; real inflection points can land as late as **month 12-14** even when monthly gains looked flat before that. Ranking within **2 weeks is "magic,"** not the norm. No industry-standard "healthy" CTR or impressions-to-clicks ratio exists — don't benchmark against a mythical universal number.
- **Diagnostic default when something isn't ranking:** "Most of the time, if you're not ranking, you're either doing too little or too much." Both under-optimization (thin content, missing keyword) and over-optimization (excess length, excess keyword repetition, too many pages) are equally common failure modes — always resolve by comparing directly against the actual top 3 competitors (§6), never against a fixed absolute rule.
- **Don't chase 100%/perfect scores on any audit tool** (Page Audit, Yoast, accessibility checkers). **80%+ on a Page Audit tool is fine** — 100% doesn't guarantee a #1 ranking, and members with near-perfect scores rank no better than members who don't chase the score. Matt keeps his own site near-perfect specifically because prospects scrutinize *his* site for credibility as a paid provider — that's a different bar than what a typical client needs to hit.
- **Fix the fundamentals before adding more tactics/channels.** "Your website's never going to be done... fix the big stuff" — a broken/incomplete site should be repaired before adding location pages, social channels, press releases, or new SEO tools.
- **Google and AI care about niche authority, not SEO polish.** "Google isn't looking for SEO experts and properly optimized, perfect sites to rank... they're looking for experts in specific niches." Don't over-index on technical perfection at the expense of genuine subject-matter depth.
- **Ranking position isn't the only success metric.** A lower-ranked or "invisible-to-every-tool" business can be fully booked (a therapist blogged consistently for ~12 months, doesn't show as "ranking" in Semrush or any tool for his target terms, yet runs a 3-month waitlist when actually searched) while a heavily-reviewed #1 competitor can lose the sale over UX friction. Don't equate rank-tracking data with real-world business results.
- **Fact-check any AI's SEO advice before trusting it.** When an AI explains "how to rank in AI search" it's paraphrasing what humans already wrote online somewhere — not automatically true. Validate against (a) what's actually working for real accounts in the community, (b) human logic ("would I, as a human, respond to content this way?"), and (c) a second reputable source, before acting on an untested claim. "It's kind of like talking to a very good friend with a good opinion" — treat any single AI's SEO take as one opinion, not gospel.
- **Always Google your own target keyword before committing to it.** One member built an entire strategy around "WordPress web design" as her keyword, then Googled it and found the results were all about the WordPress *software*, not web-design services — she'd built around the wrong keyword variant. Do this check before writing, not after.
- **A/B test rather than argue with what's clearly working for a sloppy competitor.** If a scrappy/spammy page is outranking a "correct" one, that's a live experiment result, not a rule to relitigate — note it, don't panic, and consider deliberately testing the same approach on a low-stakes page.
- **"If the way you're thinking about things is 'I want to rank on Google because I want people to pay me money' — that's kind of the opposite of how we should be thinking."** Write to genuinely help searchers; ranking and revenue follow.

## 2. Google Search Console — Core Diagnostic Workflows

GSC is the primary diagnostic instrument used live on nearly every call. Recurring workflows, merged into their most complete form:

- **Per-query / per-page position breakdown:** Search Results (Performance) report defaults to clicks/impressions only — click **"Position"** on the graph to add average position. Scroll to the query table, click a specific query row to filter, then click into the **Pages** tab to see exactly which URL(s) rank for that query and each one's average position. Matt's own demo: querying "Hey Tony" surfaced **61-62 different pages** ranking somehow for it (average position ~3.3-3.4 blended), with his homepage alone sitting at **1.4** and a stray unrelated page around position 10 — used to show harmless keyword overlap is normal, not cannibalization.
- **Why "average position" looks wrong vs. a manual search:** the page-level average position is a blend across *every* query variant that page ranks for. A keyword you type by hand can sit on page 30 while the page's overall average looks fine because other phrasings rank #1-2. Case: a page showed GSC average position **27** for its target phrase, yet didn't appear in the first 15+ pages of a manual incognito search for that exact phrase — resolved entirely by walking through the query-level breakdown instead of trusting the blended average. Matt's own "SEO [city]" page: overall average position 24, but per-query breakdown ranged from **13.5-18.6** (page 2) up to **~35** (page 4-5) depending on exact phrasing.
- **CTR/impressions math (why click-through rate naturally looks bad as a site grows):** the **top 3 organic positions capture roughly 90% of all clicks**; the rest of page 1 splits the remaining ~10%; page 2 gets roughly **1-2%** — effectively zero. A single page can rank for **hundreds to 1,000+ keyword variations** at once, and most individual keyword rows will show **zero clicks** — this is normal, not a failure. Ranking for *more* (often lower-position) keywords will mathematically drag average CTR down as a site grows; that's expected, not a red flag. Reference case: one page carried **1,000+ ranking keyword variations, 37,000 impressions, 162 clicks**, per-keyword average positions from **2.7 to 40+**, overall page average position **7.9**. Another member: **~55,000 impressions, only 139 clicks**, homepage average position ~25.
- **Diagnosing a CTR/impressions plateau (the "10X Your Traffic For Free" method):** filter Search Console to one specific page, sort the keyword table by **impressions** (not clicks), find the keyword(s) with the most impressions but poor position, and rework/add that exact keyword into the page's actual content.
- **Mining GSC for content-gap ideas generally:** in the Performance report, sort by impressions (not clicks) to surface topics/queries you're showing up for but not getting clicked — that's your next content brief. Also check which existing pieces of content carry disproportionate traffic and lean further into that angle.
- **Insights report (newer GSC feature):** shows clicks trending up, impressions, top pages by clicks, and pages trending up/down — use it to catch pages losing traffic early and decide whether to update them, or to trace a sudden traffic spike (set a custom date range bracketing the spike, then check the Pages tab for which page drove it and the Queries tab for what was actually searched — often branded terms: company name, coach's name, product name, event name — rather than service keywords).
- **Auditing your own top pages for hidden problems:** sort by top pages/clicks with no filters — Matt found his own unfinished "author page" (literally just "Matt D.," unformatted) pulling **167 clicks**. Run this pass on your own site periodically.
- **Links report:** External links / Internal links / **Top Linking Sites** (click "more" for the full list and which pages they link to) — but treat it as an undercount, not a source of truth (see §12).
- **When GSC only reports at country level and you need city-level data:** switch to Google Analytics/GA4 for acquisition-by-page detail instead. Prompt: *"How do I find acquisition for a specific page in Google Analytics/GA4."*
- **Diagnosing keyword cannibalization:** Search Console → click into a specific **Query** → **Pages** tab → see every URL ranking for it and each one's average position. Minor overlap (many pages showing somewhere between position 15-70+) is normal noise, not real cannibalization. Only worry when a *second* page seriously competes with your actual target page for a real (non-branded) keyword's position — then consolidate or 301-redirect the weaker page into the stronger one. (False-positive check: two pages that *look* duplicative on the surface may target genuinely distinct intents — verify before consolidating; see §8.)
- **HeyKeywords' semantic grouping caveat:** near-synonym terms (e.g., "photography" vs. "photographer") will show similar volume because the tool treats them semantically — don't worry about cannibalization between near-synonyms; use them interchangeably in copy.

## 3. Diagnosing Indexing Failures

- **Standard manual-indexing workflow:** Search Console → **URL Inspection** → paste the URL → if it returns "not found" or "not on Google," click **Request Indexing**. If it doesn't take right away, repeat the request every 2-4 days until it resolves (also useful for forcing a metadata refresh on a stale time-sensitive page, e.g. a contest page with an outdated date). Don't request a recrawl on a page you're still actively editing — wait until it's actually finished.
- **New-post indexing timeline:** anywhere from **~2 hours to up to a week** is normal — don't panic if it's not indexed at 24-48 hours. Standard practice: link the new URL from at least **3 older/existing posts** at publish time, then wait roughly a week before checking/requesting indexing manually. Sitemaps auto-update and auto-resubmit to Google on every publish/change — **never manually resubmit a sitemap.**
- **New post not indexing — don't fixate on one post.** Keep publishing on your normal cadence. To jump-start it: send the link to a few people directly, or share to email/social, to generate an initial traffic/engagement signal. "This is going to seem counterintuitive. Forget about it. Move on. Continue writing new blog posts."
- **Getting a page unstuck from indexing purgatory (a resolved 3-week "can't find robots file" GSC error):** wrote 2 new articles with direct backlinks into the stuck page, added 2 internal links from older articles, made ~7 total page-level changes, then requested indexing again every 2-4 days until GSC finally returned "indexing requested" instead of the robots error.
- **Fixing a GSC sitemap error reading "HTML" instead of XML:** ask ChatGPT/Claude "how do I find my Yoast sitemap, here's the link to my website," or just try swapping "html" for "xml" in the URL, or try `sitemap_index.xml` directly — then submit that XML link to Search Console.
- **The single biggest indexing rule: "If I can't get to it from your homepage, Google's not going to index it."** Content reachable only via more than ~3 clicks from the homepage is effectively invisible/"buried," even if technically linked. Homepage/nav depth target: reachable in roughly **1-2 clicks**. Delete unlinked/orphaned pages that do nothing (e.g., location pages built via AI pre-launch with zero internal links) rather than leaving them as dead weight; going forward, build pages in small batches, each one properly linked into nav/footer at creation time.
- **Fixing indexing gaps generally:** internal-link to any page/post that isn't indexed, and drive external traffic to it (social, email list) as an additional signal.
- **Reading GSC's "Not indexed" reasons selectively:** most exclusion reasons (duplicate-without-canonical, blocked, paginated/feed/author pages) aren't worth chasing — focus specifically on pages that are genuinely supposed to rank and aren't.
- **After a fix (e.g., adding alt text), wait roughly a month** before re-checking/re-crawling — also account for possible caching delay before assuming a fix failed.
- **A gallery/portfolio page showing impressions but zero keyword queries isn't necessarily broken** — sometimes Google just doesn't pass query data through for image-heavy, low-text pages. Only add text (~400-500 words) if there's real, confirmed search demand for that specific project; otherwise leave it and give it time.
- **Slug-change safety check:** if a page is (a) not ranking, (b) not getting traffic, and (c) has no backlinks — all three "no" — it's free to change the URL slug. If it IS already indexed/ranking, you're "probably safe" but should weigh it against the fact that a changed URL starts over: unrelated to any existing authority.
- **Never leave a partially-rebuilt site live with only some pages migrated.** Google reads missing pages that used to exist as deleted content — worse than shipping a full, even unoptimized, duplicate of the old site. When migrating to a new CMS/platform: duplicate all content, URLs, and heading tags identically to the old site, and launch the *entire* duplicated site at once, not a subset. On-page optimization (internal linking, meta descriptions, title tags) happens *after* launch, not before.

## 4. Diagnosing Ranking Drops, Plateaus & Post-Edit Behavior

- **Diagnosing a ranking/traffic drop after edits:** open the page/post in WordPress → **Revisions** panel, compare to prior versions, cross-reference the date the drop started against your edit history, and revert if a specific change lines up with the drop. Always save/keep the current version before reverting in case the revert doesn't help.
- **A ranking drop after a publishing gap is not necessarily "Google punishing you for not posting."** It's more that pogo-sticking (users clicking back to search results) signals the content/position isn't right, so Google re-sorts — still, don't create gaps; be consistent. Case: a **2-2.5 week** blogging gap (medical emergency) was followed by a drop from page-1 position #4 down to **#6-7** — resolved simply by resuming the normal cadence, not by editing the page.
- **Recovering from a competitor-driven ranking loss:** check what competitors changed (new backlinks, updated content) when they overtake you, and respond in kind; meanwhile keep publishing low-competition/low-difficulty blog content since almost nobody competes for that volume — ranking on many secondary keywords builds trust signal for the site as a whole on top of chasing the money-page keyword itself.
- **Protecting a high-traffic legacy post instead of touching it:** a 2022 blog post (~200-300 words) that drives **80% of total site traffic** and ranks #1 for several keywords, but for informational ("how to learn X") rather than buying intent — don't rewrite or restructure it, even though its intent doesn't match what you want to sell, and even though ~20 competitor posts have since copied/expanded on it. Instead bolt on a popup or nav-menu CTA ("get our course here") linking to the offer, leaving the ranking post itself untouched. The post functions as topical-authority proof to Google; touching it risks the whole site.
- **When a head term is simply too competitive to win** (e.g., a hyper-competitive local head term for a small business), **stop trying to optimize the homepage for it.** Redirect effort into blog posts and easier long-tail variants instead, and make sure the Google Business Profile is fully filled out. Diagnostic tell: even some of the actual top-ranked competitor pages for that term don't have an H1 tag at all — a sign the term is being won on raw domain authority, not on-page correctness, and isn't a fair fight to keep chasing head-on.
- **A genuinely low-difficulty keyword (e.g., Semrush difficulty score of 7) shouldn't need backlinks to rank.** If it's not ranking despite low difficulty: manually review what the other top-ranking pages for that exact term are doing content-wise, and increase on-page usage of the exact target phrase (including placing it in an H2) rather than assuming a technical problem.
- **Consolidating dated/annual content instead of letting old years compete with new ones:** create ONE landing page with no year in the URL as the permanent money page for that topic; redirect all old dated URLs (2024, 2025, etc.) to it; update the title tag, H1, and in-body year references to the current year; and at each annual renewal make a real content change (cite/quote an updated source, not just swap the digit). Never bolt an "this is expired, go here" intro paragraph onto old dated pages — delete/redirect them. Rule: if content updates yearly, never put the year in the URL in the first place — it signals to Google the page is tied to that specific year. (Consolidating does mean accepting a real short-term hit — the old dated URL drops out of the index and its equity doesn't automatically transfer — traded for a cleaner long-term structure.)

## 5. Diagnosing CTR, Impressions & Cross-Tool Traffic Mismatches

- **GSC vs. a platform's own analytics (e.g., Shopify) will never fully reconcile** — treat mismatches as expected: GSC pulls directly from Google, the platform counts/attributes independently. Go to GSC for "what Google actually says," go to the platform for "what it says," and don't expect them to match.
- **Google Analytics traffic includes every channel** (social, ads, events, PR mentions), not just organic search — a "drastic" GA traffic drop doesn't necessarily mean an SEO problem. Check Search Console specifically for the organic-only trend before worrying; a member's GA numbers dropped hard week-over-week purely because a *non-organic* source (e.g., a lapsed campaign) fell off, while her actual organic rankings held page-1.
- **Backlink-count tools disagree with each other and with GSC by wide margins for the identical domain** — one live example: GSC's own Links report showed only **325** unique referring domains, HeyKeywords showed **2,700 backlinks / ~1,100** referring domains, and Semrush showed **~1,300** referring domains — all for the same domain, checked back-to-back. None should be treated as a definitive count; direction/trend matters more than any single absolute figure.
- **Troubleshooting low-impression blog posts:** first confirm the posts are actually indexed — if yes, that alone is a real win, not a failure. Then check Google Analytics for referral traffic specifically from `chatgpt.com` / `claude.ai` / other LLM domains, since a blog post can be doing real work (AI citation, internal-linking context feeding a money page) that raw impressions/clicks never show. In a highly competitive niche (many competitors who are themselves SEO-savvy), the fix is getting **more specific/niche** with topics, not concluding that blogging failed — e.g., a "how to add custom icons in Avada" post (paired with a matching YouTube video) kept performing long after publishing specifically because every other tutorial online on that exact task was bad.
- **A "buried" theory for rising impressions but flat/declining CTR:** when asked, Claude suggested the page might simply be positioned too low in the SERP for its impression volume to convert to clicks — a plausible explanation worth checking against the page-level average-position data (§2) rather than assuming content is broken.
- **A visibility/traffic drop is not automatically an SEO failure.** Zero-click search behavior (users getting their answer directly in the SERP/AI Overview without clicking through) can explain declining clicks even while actual search visibility holds steady or improves — this reasoning applies to a business's own traffic dips and, per Matt, to his own recurring imposter-syndrome moments about his agency's numbers.

## 6. The Competitor-Benchmark Method

This is the single most-repeated diagnostic-and-fix technique across all 15 calls — used for word count, keyword frequency, and heading count alike. **Never optimize to a fixed absolute number; always benchmark directly against whoever currently ranks #1-3 for your exact target keyword, then match or slightly exceed it.**

- **Word count:** Google the exact target keyword, pull up the actual top 3 ranking pages, and match their word count or add roughly **100-200 words** more (not a fixed program-wide number). A program-cited baseline of **750-1,500 words** (later refined to **800-1,000**) exists only as a rough starting anchor — the real rule is competitor-relative. Evidence the fixed-number approach is wrong: real competitor pages for one exact keyword ranged from **~500 to 2,500 words** across the same SERP, proving word count itself isn't the ranking driver. Over-optimization (excess length) is one of the most common failure modes seen live: a page with far more words/information than the actual top rankers was told to **trim down**, not add more; conversely, a page shorter than its top-ranked competitor was told to add depth. Zero-volume-and-zero-difficulty keywords are the one exception — **~400-500 words** is plenty there, no need to go in-depth.
- **Keyword frequency/density:** split the target phrase into its components (e.g., a location term and a service term) and count each **separately** across the top 3 competitor pages, rather than only counting the literal full phrase. Feed those counts into a spreadsheet, then match or slightly exceed the top competitor's frequency ("**plus one**") — don't just copy their wording, write in your own voice/expertise. Case: top competitors used a service term ~10× and a location term ~4× on pages ranging from ~500 to 2,500 words; guidance was ~800-1,000 words with matching frequency, not a fixed formula.
- **Heading count:** use a browser extension to pull your page's H1/H2 outline, compare it against the heading structure of the pages currently ranking above you, and use **one more H2 than your top competitor has** — you don't need to reverse-engineer a "correct" number, just slightly exceed what's already ranking. (Diagnostic gotcha: even some top-ranked competitor pages for a highly competitive term turn out to have **no H1 tag at all** — evidence the term is being won on domain authority, not structure; see §4.)
- **Ignore a page-audit tool's overall composite "score" entirely when working a money page** — read only three things: (a) how many times the target keyword is used vs. competitors, (b) total word count vs. competitors, (c) keyword density vs. competitors — then be patient for rankings to catch up. "Ignore everything else. It's there. It's like nothing. Zero."
- **Verify competitor data by eye, not just by tool.** Tool-reported competitor word counts and keyword usage can be wrong or include hidden/duplicated content (e.g., a review-carousel plugin duplicating testimonial text can inflate a real page to 3,000-4,000 words that aren't genuine content) — sanity-check anything unusual before using it to set your own target. Strip a reviews/testimonial plugin if it's the actual source of an inflated word count.

## 7. Heading & H1 Diagnostics

- **Book-analogy structure:** H1 = book title (states the page's main topic/target keyword), H2s = chapter titles, H3s = subheadings within a chapter — you can drop back down to a new H2 after an H3, but never skip a level going *down* (H1 straight to H3, or H2 straight to H4/H6). Skipping backward/*up* a level (H3 back to H2) is fine. Avoid H4/H6 entirely in practice.
- **A page must have exactly ONE H1**, positioned at the very top, containing the actual target keyword in natural, readable language (not "gobbledygook"). Rewrite the H1 to match how people genuinely search — one case: "romantic storytelling wedding photography in Toronto" (the business owner's own phrasing) rewritten to "Timeless wedding photography in Toronto" to match what people actually type ("wedding photographer Toronto").
- **The two-path decision when a second heading-level element exists on the page:**
  - **If the "extra" heading is real topical content** (a genuine subheading of the page's subject that just got mis-tagged as H1) — **downgrade it to H2.** Don't delete the content, just correct the tag level. Case: a wedding-ring blog's second heading, an actual subtopic ("What Wedding Band Style(s) Couples Like"), had been styled as a second H1 — corrected to H2, content untouched.
  - **If the "extra" heading is NOT real content** — a hero/lead-magnet box, a CTA ("Inquire Now"), a testimonial quote, a tagline, a logo, or a specific client/name callout — **strip the heading tag entirely and convert it to plain, styled paragraph text.** Don't demote these to H2 either; they aren't topical subheadings at all, and leaving them as any heading tag confuses what the page is actually about. Go through every H1/H2/H3 on a page one at a time and ask "does this support what the page is about?" — anything that's pure styling, branding, or a CTA fails that test and gets demoted to plain text.
- **Platform-specific duplicate-H1 sources to check for:**
  - **Kajabi:** a blog-index title (e.g., "Relationship Clarity Blog") sitting above every individual post's own H1 — convert the index title to H2/paragraph text so the real post title is the page's first and only H1.
  - **Avada (and similar page builders):** duplicating an entire block to show separate desktop/mobile copies is the actual bug — this loads the same content (and heading) twice on every pageview. **Don't duplicate the block; use per-breakpoint design settings on the same element** (a different font-size, image size, or background image per breakpoint is fine; the text/heading itself must not be duplicated as a second element). Claude Code fix: copy the block's default-editor code from the WP backend, paste into Claude Code, prompt *"make this mobile friendly in the same blocks without duplicating the content."* (Caution: asking a general-purpose AI the same question without giving it this framing can return the *wrong* fix — one member's AI suggested simply renaming the mobile heading so it "isn't called H1," which doesn't actually solve the duplication.)
  - **Generic "too many H1s" flags from an audit tool** often lack specifics — install a dedicated heading-audit browser extension that lists every H1 individually. One case found **13 separate H1 tags** this way, firing from the logo, signage graphics, and animated display elements that a generic tool had only flagged as a vague count.
- **Sequential heading structure matters for AI/LLM readability too** — since there's no way to know how deep any given AI crawler actually reads into a page, put the direct answer to the page's core topic right at the top, and keep the heading hierarchy clean and sequential all the way down.
- **Cut dropdowns/accordions for FAQ or service content** — convert them into always-visible H2 headers + plain paragraph text underneath, so AI crawlers and Google can read the content without needing to trigger the toggle open. Matt's stated belief: content hidden behind a toggle is probably weighted less effectively by AI/search, even though he stops short of a hard rule ("fine in some cases, not every case") — he's actively moving his own site away from heavy toggle/dropdown use.
- **Never use hidden text** (font color matching the background) to stuff a keyword invisibly — a classic black-hat trick; Google devalues/ignores it, and duplicate H1 tags hidden this way are a cloaking pattern to remove on sight the moment you spot it.

## 8. Duplicate Content, Cannibalization & Consolidation

- **Consolidating two near-duplicate pages:** if two pages aren't indexing and target the same or very similar keyword/near-duplicate content (e.g., "Interior Design Services" vs. "Full Service Interior Design" vs. "Interior Design Studio"), consolidate them into one page.
- **False-positive check before consolidating:** two pages that *look* duplicative on the surface may actually target genuinely distinct keywords/intents — verify via real keyword research before merging (case: a state-mandated "catalog/designations" page and a "continuing education" listing page looked similar but targeted "real estate continuing education" vs. "online real estate programs" respectively — correctly kept as two separate pages).
- **Blog listing/category pages must never show full post content** — only a thumbnail image and an excerpt, with a click-through to the full post. Showing full content on both the listing page and the individual post page gives Google two pages with identical content, and it will deprioritize/fail to index one of them.
- **Category pages for blog content will basically never rank on their own** — don't bother adding meta descriptions/focus keyphrases to them. Exception: **Shopify collection pages behave differently and can and do rank** — worth optimizing those the same way you'd optimize a money page.
- **Cross-posting a blog post to social/other platforms without triggering duplicate-content risk:** don't repost the exact target-keyword content verbatim elsewhere. Post a summary with your own added framing and link back to the full post (Medium, LinkedIn, etc.) instead of the full text.
- **Turning a blog post into a YouTube video is fine and not duplicate content** — as long as you're adding more/different information rather than just reading the blog text verbatim, and it isn't an AI-generated video (YouTube is actively cracking down on AI-generated video specifically).
- **Blog-vs-money-page cannibalization guard:** money pages shouldn't link out to blog content as "a feature" — link to at most 2-3 genuinely essential resource pages, and let blog posts link **to** the money page, not the reverse. Blog posts don't always need to rank or drive direct traffic themselves — sometimes their only job is passing internal-link authority to the money page.
- **Only use 5-10 categories total** (each a real content "vertical"), and **avoid tags entirely** — every tag spins up its own thin/near-empty archive page the moment you stop posting about that exact tag term, wasting crawl budget. Only spin up a dedicated tag/category once there's enough volume of content to justify it. Category maintenance cadence: you should be able to add at least one new piece of content to a given category roughly **every 2 months** — only commit to "pillar" categories you're confident you'll keep filling.
- **Don't touch sitemap-plugin config beyond excluding tags** — keep categories included in the sitemap, and otherwise leave whatever the plugin auto-generates alone.

## 9. Site Hygiene, Technical Cleanup & Migration

- **Pruning dead pages:** in Search Console, find pages with zero impressions/zero indexing/zero traffic and remove them as useless. Delete unused/orphaned pages an audit tool surfaces (e.g., a leftover test page) if nothing links to them and you're not using them.
- **Cleaning up broken/404 links:** run a site-wide audit tool to surface every broken link, then work through them in small batches (roughly **10/week**) rather than trying to bulk-fix or bulk-delete — the links point to different destinations, so there's no single bulk action.
- **Image optimization, in priority order (don't try to convert everything at once):** (1) money pages first, (2) new blog posts as you publish them going forward, (3) then circle back to older posts that have impressions in GSC but aren't ranking well.
- **WebP conversion workflow:** Canva cannot export WebP directly — export PNG/JPG from Canva first, then run it through a separate free WebP converter (Google "convert JPEG/PNG to WebP"). Photographers with Lightroom should use its built-in preset export to output WebP directly. For bulk-converting hundreds/thousands of existing images, use a plugin (e.g., **Imagify**) rather than manual conversion.
- **Website-speed fix sequence (least to most disruptive, in order):** (1) audit and cut down plugin count first; (2) install/configure a caching plugin (e.g., **WP Rocket**); (3) run an image-compression tool (e.g., Imagify, or a caching plugin's paid image add-on) to convert images to WebP; (4) only change the theme if the above doesn't get the score high enough, since a theme change forces a full visual rebuild; (5) for more advanced fixes, ask Claude Code directly how to make the site faster. **Keep WordPress plugin count to 15 or fewer** — consolidate/remove old or unused ones even if they seem harmless; they still load and cost speed.
- **Making internal links visible:** underline them and give them a color meaningfully different from body text so they visually "pop" — a real usability defect if links blend into normal text.
- **Checking internal links per page with no dedicated free tool:** use GSC to see which pages have few or no incoming internal links, and prioritize linking to those. Not usually needed if you're already doing weekly blog posts each linking to 3 other pages + 1 money page.
- **External-link rule in practice:** on money pages/homepage, external-link to your own legitimacy profiles (Chamber of Commerce, Trustpilot, Glassdoor, or similar) rather than a competitor or unrelated site, and whenever you state a stat/claim ("50% of businesses do X"), hyperlink that exact phrase to the actual source/study — instead of a separate "References" list. When citing supporting research/psychology angles, don't link to direct competitors — use neutral third-party sources.
- **Homepage vs. money-page keyword targeting:** if you're not actually trying to rank your homepage for a given keyword, remove that keyword from the homepage title tag (use your name/brand instead) and put the "[keyword] — [name]" pattern on the dedicated service/money page that IS targeting it.
- **"Near me" keywords cannot be optimized by literally putting the words on the page** — Google infers proximity from the searcher's own location automatically. If "near me" is already a top referring keyword organically, leave the page as-is; don't try to engineer it further.
- **Meta description target length: 120-150 characters.** Every page's H1, meta description, and product name/description should be unique across the site — never reuse or duplicate them page to page. Don't allow blog comments — "the only thing you'll get on blogs is spam."
- **Preserving SEO equity during a platform migration or full redesign:** for any URL currently getting organic traffic, or any URL with existing backlinks, keep the exact URL and keep the on-page content the same — visual design/branding/colors can change freely, content and URL structure cannot. Don't change everything site-wide in one pass (e.g., adding H1s across every legacy page at once) — it causes Google to visibly "adjust" across the board; make structural changes incrementally instead. If you can't preserve the exact URL structure during a migration, at minimum keep the content near-identical to avoid a traffic collapse (a documented case: a mid-migration platform switch with redirects still saw traffic "falling through the floor" and lost backlinks as linking sites dropped links rather than update them to point at a redirect).
- **Never redirect a redirect.** Point straight to the final live URL — don't stack a second layer of redirects on top of existing ones.
- **Recovering lost backlinks after a bad migration:** proactively email the sites that used to link to your old (now-dead or redirected) URL, tell them it moved, and ask them to update the link to point directly at the new URL — don't just rely on the redirect to preserve the relationship/signal.
- **Finding quick-win keywords during or after a redesign:** in GSC, find queries where a page ranks positions **11-20**, then make small optimizations (add a paragraph, fix the H1) to try to push it onto page 1 — this is the fastest lever for a traffic increase.
- **Diagnosing crawler/audit-tool access failures:** check the site's `.htaccess` file for rate-limiting or bot-blocking rules; ask the host (or Claude/ChatGPT) how to add the needed user agents to the allowlist. A tool or bot that worked fine via an old URL but fails via a new login/URL setup is a strong sign of an `.htaccess` or rate-limit issue, not a content problem — "if our tools can't crawl your site, how many other tools and AI can't crawl your site?"
- **Never publicly name or link a community member's/client's winning site, even with explicit permission** — treated as a real security risk (a known target invites nefarious action like bad backlinks against the site).

## 10. Location & Service Page Architecture

The single most fully-worked-out structural framework across these calls.

### Core rule
- **One page = one keyword = one service = one location, always.** Never build a page for the same city targeting two different services — "you're not even getting a slice" of either keyword by trying to own both. Menu master pattern: Home / About / Contact / Buy [product] / **Services** (one page per service) / **Locations** (one page per city); on each location page, target only the ONE service that's easiest to rank for in that specific city, then just mention the other service in passing (an H2 or plain text — "I also do emceeing — looking for an emcee? I do that too").
- **Home-base/flagship exception:** your main/home-base location can carry a separate page per service (e.g., "Chicago Newborn Photographer" AND "Chicago Family Photographer"), since that's your flagship market. Every OTHER (satellite/suburb) location gets only ONE service page — never stack multiple services under one satellite location. Choose which service to target per satellite location via keyword research on whatever's actually easiest to rank there.
- **Sequencing:** finish building ALL location pages first (each targeting one service) before moving on to blog content.

### Scaling large or multi-location service areas
- **Prioritize smaller towns/suburbs over the big nearby city.** Almost nobody else targets them, and searchers type their specific city/town, not a regional abbreviation (a Toronto-area photographer was told to drop "GTA wedding photography" from her flagship page entirely and just use "Toronto wedding photographer," then build individual pages for Hamilton, Stouffville, Mississauga, Oakville, King City, etc. — small towns called out by name as near-zero-competition). Neighborhood-level pages within a big city work the same way (e.g., "junk removal beaches Toronto," "junk removal Leslieville" for named sub-areas people actually search).
- **A location page usually cannot outrank a genuine local competitor's homepage** (their de facto money page, and typically their most-backlinked page site-wide) without committing real backlinks specifically to your page — the practical workaround is retargeting smaller/adjacent towns where you're not fighting a dedicated local homepage, rather than fighting head-on for the big city term.
- **Avoiding combinatorial explosion (many locations × many services/event-types):** don't build every location × service combination "just in case" (one case would have been 66 pages: 25 cities × ~6 event types). Instead build one page per location with sections/buttons for each event type/service, and run keyword research to check whether any *specific* combo genuinely gets searched — only build a dedicated combo page where real volume exists (roughly 5 of 66 combos were judged worth their own page in one case).
- **Service-area hierarchy instead of a flat link-dump:** for a wide service area (multiple counties/regions), don't publish one giant page listing every city as a raw link — "it's useless, people don't really need that." Instead: top-level page lists only the main sub-regions/counties → each links to a dedicated page for that county/region, listing its top towns → each town gets its own page, which can list smaller neighborhoods within it. One case: this hierarchy replaced a flat list with roughly **10-20 pages** total.
- **Zero-volume keywords are still worth a page.** Roughly **15% of Google searches are brand-new every day** and rising — historical zero-search volume isn't proof of zero future demand. If a keyword shows zero volume AND zero difficulty, ~400-500 words is enough; don't skip the page just because a tool shows 0.
- **Rollout cadence:** unique copy per location, not a template with only the city swapped. Ship roughly **1 new location page per week** (2/week if aggressive) — a 12-location build finishes in ~3 months without burning out. Large SKU/product catalogs follow the same batching logic (~20 items/week rather than all 400 at once) so lessons from the first batch inform the rest. After 3-4 months, check whether the pages are actually ranking; if not, simplify toward the more templated/duplicate style some competitors use and re-test.
- **Location pages must be genuinely unique content per location, not a template with only the city swapped** — though if literally all your local competitors are doing the swap-only approach, that itself signals low competition, and either approach might work; test unique content for 3-4 months first, simplify only if it fails.

### Content specifics for a location page
- Delivery-time specifics for that place (e.g., "same-day delivery within 6 or 12 hours"), named local landmarks/venues (funeral homes, wedding venues, parks), and seasonal color commentary (a park is great May-September but hotter mid-summer, with prep tips).
- For non-delivery/service businesses that can't say "we deliver here" (telehealth, remote services): anchor each city page in genuinely local hooks instead of repeating the core pitch — nearest hospitals/urgent care, local grocery stores/farmers markets carrying relevant products, local gyms (potential partners), local hiking trails or 5K routes.
- Interview-style method to generate the copy: ask yourself a few location-specific questions ("What do you like most about this location? How's the lighting? If it's rainy, can we still shoot here?") and turn the answers directly into page copy (not a blog post).
- **Location page vs. blog post, the dividing line:** static/evergreen info that should rank for the money keyword = a page. A specific one-off question/topic = a blog post.
- **Avoiding duplicate content across local pages:** check your top 3 competitors' local/city pages for the same keyword, note their word count, and write the same length or ~100-200 words more (see §6).
- **Multilingual/bilingual local pages:** don't just translate — do fresh keyword research *in that language* (the top keyword is often different from a direct translation), then write using the natural in-language keyword in context. Applies to Spanish for bilingual-practice pages, and French/German for a Swiss business ("three chances to rank three different audiences"). Warned specifically for Quebec French: "AI translation will not cut it in Quebec" — validate via a private/incognito browser window set to French to see what the SERP actually looks like.
- **Sessions/gallery pages as an alternative** when a full location page per suburb isn't feasible: build a "sessions" page/gallery for specific venues you've actually shot at (e.g., "Ancaster Mills sessions") — attracts search traffic and doubles as a gallery to show prospective clients from their actual venue.
- **Navigation:** put a "Locations" hub link in the header/footer → hub page lists every location → each location name links out to its own individual page. Get new local pages indexed faster by linking to each one directly from the homepage (a real "areas we serve" section with real links), not only buried in the footer — footer-only links index slower.

### Seasonal money pages
- Build a dedicated page per key season (Black Friday, Christmas, Valentine's Day) instead of a blog post. Keep the URL live and indexed year-round, but pull the nav link when out of season — Google still knows the URL exists. Before/during the next season, either restore the nav link or point a timely seasonal blog post at it so Google can keep crawling it in-season.

### Diagnosing a page/site that isn't ranking at all despite the basics being right
- Check: (1) GSC signed up + sitemap submitted, (2) Google Business Profile connected to the site, (3) backlinks/citations in place. If all three are present and it still isn't ranking (not even page 10), the honest read may simply be **"a matter of trust"** — accumulated authority the newer/thinner site hasn't earned yet, not a fixable technical bug.
- Check the actual on-page H1/H2 wording against real search phrasing, not the business owner's preferred marketing language (see §7's Prito rewrite example).

## 11. Money Page, Homepage & CTA Rules

- **Money pages are broadly defined:** location pages, product pages, service pages, and collection pages are all money pages — apply the same discipline to all of them.
- **Money-page linking rule:** money pages should link out to your own blog content and to authoritative external sources, and should always include a link/CTA to book or convert — even if the page itself isn't the final conversion step.
- **CTA placement:** don't stop at one CTA at the top and one at the bottom — add 2-3 more spaced through the body content, so a reader convinced mid-page can act immediately instead of scrolling back up or down to find contact info.
- **Quote/inquiry forms on ecommerce or service pages:** keep one full dedicated "Get a Quote" page, but also add a small inline quote form (name, phone, email, short message) directly on each individual product/service page — mirrors what competitors already do and gives Google more on-page conversion signal.
- **FAQ answers should link through to money pages**, including linking straight into a specific answer/section on the money page via an anchor/hashtag URL, not just to the top of the page.
- **Links opening in a new tab vs. the same tab:** change them to open in the same tab — a UX call, not an SEO rule ("why are we opening up so many tabs... most of us have like 50 tabs open at all times").
- **Keep both a "Contact me" CTA button AND a separate "Contact" item in the main menu** — not either/or.
- **Reviews belong on every money page** (not just a dedicated reviews page): hand-paste 2-3 real reviews directly into the page copy (not a carousel plugin — see §13), rotating which reviews appear on which page so the same text isn't repeated everywhere.
- **No auto-rotating image sliders or carousels anywhere**, especially as a page's hero. Reasoning given repeatedly: a slider doesn't auto-advance (most visitors never click to see slide 2+), and every page load still has to load every slide's image even if nobody sees them — pure cost with no reliable benefit. Replace with one static hero image, or an alternating layout (two photos stacked, or text-left/photo-right then text-right/photo-left blocks).
- **Cut vague, generic brand copy that does nothing for SEO or the reader** ("every woman is uniquely beautiful," "beautiful holidays, effortlessly handled") — Matt's test: if an unrelated business in a different niche could reuse the exact line verbatim, it's too generic to keep.
- **Naming/labeling calls:** call the pricing page "Pricing," not a euphemism like "Investment" — people search "[service] pricing," not "[service] investment." Avoid cursive/script fonts for anything that needs to be read quickly — "any friction is friction," especially for readers who never learned cursive.
- **Don't duplicate the same section (e.g., testimonials) twice on one page "just in case" people don't scroll far** — consolidate or reposition instead.
- **Testimonial page-matching:** put location-specific testimonials only on the matching location page (e.g., city-A reviews go only on the city-A page) instead of mixing testimonials from every city together on every page.

## 12. Backlinks — Diagnostics & Policy

- **Backlink tools disagree with each other and with GSC by wide margins for the same domain** — treat the trend, not the absolute number, as signal (see §5 for the specific 325 / ~1,100-1,300 / 2,700 example).
- **Never disavow or remove a competitor's backlink to you.** A competitor linking to your page is a trust signal, not a threat — "that's like your competitor saying [you're] good." Confirmed live via GSC's Top Linking Sites report multiple times.
- **Toxic/"suspicious" backlink flags from an audit tool (e.g., Semrush) should NOT trigger default removal/disavowal.** Only intervene defensively if the site has zero traffic, isn't ranking at all, or appears shadow-banned. Otherwise, the fix is adding MORE good backlinks to outweigh the bad ones, not subtracting the flagged ones — Matt has seen clients remove flagged links on their own initiative and lose traffic as a direct result, with no way to know in advance which "toxic" link might actually be propping up rankings.
- **Guest-post / backlink placement structure:** the topic doesn't need to be closely aligned to your niche or your local service area at all — the backlink is the point, not rankings on the host site ("we don't care if this site ranks really well... we're just doing this for a backlink"). If the host site's own on-site search is unreliable (can hide relevant content), don't trust it — Google-search the domain directly with `site:[domain].com [rough topic]` to find real indexed content to pitch into. First person is fine if it's your natural voice. Only **ONE** link back to your own site, placed in the **top half** of the article. Anchor text should be specific and descriptive/geo-qualified (e.g., "clean comedian, Boca Raton"), not a vague brand/book name — unless you deliberately want traffic routed to that specific offer. Bonus move that signals quality to the host site: also link to one of the host's own existing internal articles, not just your own site.
- **Backlink outreach phrasing:** never say the word "backlink" to a non-SEO person or organization (a chamber of commerce, a local publication) — they won't know what it means and you'll get blank stares. Ask instead: "Can I include a link to my business [on your site/directory/profile]?"
- **Automatic backlink sources that need no extra "claiming" step:** Chamber of Commerce / association membership (once listed, it's an automatic backlink — you can also add their logo to your own site noting the membership), event listings (posting to Eventbrite auto-creates a backlink), and a third-party directory that already lists/ranks your name even without sending you the booking directly (reach out and ask them to also link to your own website).
- **Backlinks are more likely to land on a blog post than a money page** — most people are simply more likely to link to genuinely useful informational content than to a commercial service/product page; blog posts still benefit the site as a whole even when the link doesn't land on the homepage.
- **Two brands/sites in the same location offering overlapping services:** pick ONE site to own the shared keywords — whichever one is already ranking — rather than competing with yourself. Don't run active SEO on multiple owned sites simultaneously (burnout risk, and cannibalization risk if the sites are in a similar niche) — treat separate sites as fully separate SEO projects. Basic backlinking between your own multiple sites is fine "a couple of times," but prioritize backlinks from sites you don't control.
- **Google's "Preferred Sources" feature** (newly discovered): a page titled "Make search your own with preferred sources" lets a user manually designate trusted sources for their own search results. As of these calls there's no known auto opt-in mechanism — even paid ads pointed at the feature still require the user to manually click to add the source — an open, unsolved growth lever.
- **Domain-wide authority governs every subdomain built on it.** A spam-damaged or low-authority root domain will drag down an otherwise-clean subdomain (a nanny-vetting platform's clean new subdomain was still at risk from the main domain's history of bot-posted forum spam). Recovering from embedded spam (bot-posted backlinks via an old forum/community feature) can take **6-12 months** to fully clear from Google's index/algorithm even after the spam source is removed. Two remediation paths: (A) leave the main site as-is, wait it out, see if authority recovers as spam signals age out; (B) register a brand-new domain, migrate the good content over (not the spam), and abandon the old domain. Matt's rule of thumb: "unless you're in love with your domain, buy a different domain and switch things over." Either way, keep blogging 1-2×/week and keep building legitimate backlinks in the meantime.
- **Backlink asks inside a shared community must stay topically relevant** (e.g., a comedian's site shouldn't link to an unrelated nanny-vetting platform just because both are members).

## 13. Reviews, GBP & Local Trust Signals

- **Never review-gate.** Don't run an NPS or 1-5 rating pre-screen before deciding whether to send someone a review-request link — this is explicit review gating and against Google's policy; Google can **ban the entire Business Profile** if caught. If review requests run through an automation (e.g., a CRM workflow) that pre-screens sentiment before sending the link, that also counts as gating. Always send review requests as a **direct, unfiltered link straight to the Google Business Profile.**
- **Avoid in-person QR-code review requests** (e.g., a table tent at a restaurant) — Google doesn't want reviews solicited while the customer is still on-site/being watched, since that biases toward artificially positive reviews. Texting/emailing the link later, once the customer is home, is the Google-approved pattern and yields more honest reviews. Real risk: a customer or competitor can report an in-person QR-code ask and get an entire franchise account/profile shut down.
- **Review pace matters more than volume for professions that can't ask clients directly** (therapists/counselors): ask supervisors/colleagues for character-testimony reviews instead, at roughly **1 new review/week** rather than dumping a bank at once — steady pace signals legitimacy and compounds; **~6 months of 1/week** can outpace a competitor who has more total reviews but has gone stagnant.
- **Never use a review-carousel plugin.** Found adding roughly **1,000 extra words** of markup on some pages (hurting load time) and bad UX besides — users scroll past carousels rather than clicking through them ("does anybody click through the carousel and it's like, give me more reviews? No"). Hand-paste 2-3 real reviews as plain text on each money page instead, rotating which ones appear where (see §11).
- **GBP profile strength can drop** (100% → 80%) even without you removing anything — Google nudges toward "complete to 100%," which routes to Google's paid business bundle (~$27/month); clicking "complete" alone doesn't restore 100% for free.
- **Never click "Apply All" on Google's recommended edits** — applies to both **Google Business Profile** recommended edits/services and **Google Ads** recommended changes. A past client did this on both and both rankings/traffic and ad spend went sideways as a result.
- **Regularly review and delete irrelevant auto-added GBP services** (a real case had "telemarketing" and "magazine/newsprint printing" auto-added despite the business doing neither) — treat these as bad trust signals, not harmless clutter.
- **GBP name changes tied to a DBA/press release are safe to run in parallel with the release** — if Google declines the name-change verification, the profile simply reverts to the old name; it does not go down or get suspended in the process.
- **Geotagging photos for GBP:** upload to a geotagging tool (e.g., Geo Imgr), choose a plausible spot on the map for the target service area (doesn't have to be the literal shoot location), download the geotagged file, then upload to GBP posts/photos. To rank a wider area, vary the geotag across a grid of locations in the target zone rather than tagging every photo to the same point. Real phone photos with location services on are usually already geotagged via EXIF — no manual tagging needed. Never falsely geotag a stock photo to a location it doesn't plausibly belong to; for stock photos, strip existing embedded location metadata instead (re-save through Photoshop/Canva). Cost: roughly ~1 minute/photo. **Genuinely disputed even among practitioners** — ChatGPT itself calls it "one of the most overrated SEO tactics," while Matt's agency reports real correlated results; his actual position is outcome-based, not mechanism-based (Google may strip the metadata entirely on ingestion) — test on 1-2 clients, keep it if it correlates with results for that client's competitive situation, since the cost is low enough to do regardless of uncertain payoff. Fundamentals still come before geotagging: more reviews, a fully completed GBP, regular photos, a good website connected to the GBP.
- **Local directory listings:** submit to **Bing Places** (import directly from your GBP — "takes like two minutes") and **Apple Business Connect/Maps** (more involved — requires adding a TXT record to your DNS; the record must include "www" if your site uses www, or verification fails).
- **Map-pack strategy (the full recurring pattern, confirmed across multiple cases):** post to GBP at least **twice a week**; geotag every photo posted with the target keyword in the file/metadata; include a CTA/click-to-call link and a website link on every post; chain posts together (grab the share link from your *previous* GBP post, paste it into the description of the new one); build service-area pages on the website (one per neighborhood/service category), each linked to the matching GBP service category; ask for reviews continuously. One case sustained this for ~1 year across two locations and sits in/near the top-3 map pack as "a very consistent lead generator" — note that a much older, completely inactive competitor (no posts, ~1 review/week, decades in business) can still outrank this effort "by a mile," attributed mainly to longevity/authority and a larger review base rather than any single tactic gap.
- **Diagnosing a dropped map-pack position:** check whether you changed anything recently; compare review count AND review *velocity* against top competitors (velocity can beat a competitor even if their total count is higher); confirm citations are built; confirm NAP (name/address/phone) is present and crawlable in the site footer, linking to the GBP; check whether GBP-listed services match/exceed the real website (a mismatch can look like profile padding); and factor in that Google periodically updates its own map-pack algorithm independent of anything you did.
- **Linking all active social media handles to the GBP listing:** when someone searches the business name directly, GBP can surface "recent posts on social media" pulled from those linked accounts (observed on mobile).
- **Local citation/GBP data-integrity monitoring:** a Google-Cloud data connector (e.g., Coupler.io) pulling GBP + GSC + GA into one place can surface anonymous or fraudulent suggested-edit activity hitting your Business Profile that you'd otherwise miss.

## 14. Press Release Playbook

- **Full build process:** gather recent customer reviews (screenshots are fine, no need to transcribe) → upload the screenshots directly to Claude → prompt it to write a press release weaving in roughly **5 of the reviews** around the target keyword phrase (e.g., "[Business] is a top-rated [service] in [City]") → submit through a distribution service (AB Newswire ≈ **$80/release**; add-on packages available, e.g. +$30 for two additional newspapers via a different service).
- **A press release doesn't need real news.** Frame it as "[client] is a top-rated business in [industry]," then talk about their reviews and why they're highly rated — no actual newsworthy event required. Aesthetic polish doesn't matter either: "AI doesn't care what it looks like," and a release that "looks a little cheesy" can still drive real pickup and citation.
- **Book-launch press release structure:** announcement title = topic keyword + "book" + author framing (e.g., "How to Build a Bookkeeping Business: Book Launched by Brand New Author [Name]") — rationale: people searching the topic keyword are also plausible searchers for a book on that topic. Follow-up review article title = topic keyword + "book" + accolade (e.g., "...Book Gets Five-Star Reviews").
- **Amazon #1-bestseller PR play:** check your Amazon category/micro-category ranking daily after launch — it can take only a handful of sales to hit #1 in a narrow micro-category. The moment you hit #1, screenshot it immediately since the position is often fleeting, then issue a press release announcing the #1 bestseller status and keep reinforcing that specific claim in follow-up content so it feeds AI answer engines too.
- **Never publish the press-release text on your own site.** The release outlets are already linking to you; hosting the same copy yourself creates a reciprocal-linking pattern to avoid. Share it on social media and your email list instead.
- **A press release + a GBP name change can run concurrently** — submit the release as soon as it's ready (no need to wait for a specific day of the week); change the GBP name at the same time as long as you're holding DBA ("doing business as") paperwork in case Google asks for re-verification.
- **Tracking your own pickup:** don't wait for the distribution service's report email (it may not arrive, or lands in spam) — just Google your own name/business directly to see which sites picked it up. Use a never-before-used photo in the release so that whenever that exact image resurfaces elsewhere (Google Images, other sites), you know it's a press-release pickup, not an organic mention.
- **Posting a press-release win on social:** frame it as a "why we started this business" personal narrative, not a bare announcement. Post to Facebook and ask friends/family to actually *share* it (not just like) — one case saw roughly **10× normal views** (~2,000 vs. a usual ~50) this way.
- **Proactively pitch local media directly after distribution — don't expect them to find it on their own** ("there's so much noise" for a passively-distributed release to surface locally): go to the local publication's website, find their contact form/press or media contact specifically, and reach out: "I was featured in [national outlet] — I'm local to you, would love to chat, do an interview, or have you repost this." Use Claude/ChatGPT to draft the outreach if unsure what to say. National distribution services typically do NOT include small local/regional newspapers in their own network — local pickup requires this manual step.
- **Etsy/platform-specific press release:** if sales/traffic are coming from a third-party platform (Etsy) rather than your own site's SEO, run a press release aimed specifically at that platform/niche instead of only optimizing the website (e.g., "Funny tennis T-shirts on Etsy").
- **Three distinct purposes of a press release**, worth pursuing simultaneously: (1) leverage for direct local-media outreach you couldn't otherwise get; (2) AI Overview/answer-engine visibility (gets treated as "in the news," reinforcing topical relevance — AI descriptions have been observed pulling language directly from a client's own press release when asked "who's the best X in [city]"); (3) shareable social-proof content that lets you talk yourself up without sounding self-promotional (a publication calling you "the best" lands differently than saying it yourself).
- **Reviews-aggregator microsite (an unproven, secondhand tactic, explicitly untested by Matt himself, and publicly disputed by at least one other SEO practitioner):** buy a domain like "[BusinessName]Reviews.com," build a genuinely simple, plain one-page site (white/cream background, black text — doesn't need to match your brand), and paste in testimonials/reviews pulled from every source (Google, Facebook, direct client feedback — doesn't have to be all positive). **Reviews must be written out as real text, not posted as screenshot/JPEG images** — feed screenshots into Claude/Claude Code and instruct it to transcribe the review content as real text while building the page. Add ONE link to it from an already-indexed blog post on your main site to seed its authority. Build time: "shouldn't take more than a couple hours" via Claude Code. Theory: AI models may pick this up as a positive reputation signal — cheap enough to test either way ("if it works, it works. If it doesn't work, it doesn't work").

## 15. AI/LLM Citation (GEO/AEO) — Mechanics, Tactics & Tools

### How citation actually seems to work
- **No confirmed correlation between organic rank #1 and AI Overview citation.** Manual testing (5-10 queries) plus a broader AI-agent check found *no* correlation — you can rank #1 and not be cited, or rank #1 and be cited. Re-running the identical keyword search on 3 separate occasions, the same site was cited consistently only around **~30%** of the time (self-corrected down live from an initial ~45% estimate) — AI Overview sourcing is unstable/session-to-session, not a fixed lookup.
- **Working theory of the mechanism:** Google's AI finds a piece of content, writes an AI Overview based on it, then looks for other sources that back up what the Overview already states.
- **A 100+-AI-Overview study's conclusion:** citation isn't primarily about formatting, FAQs, or schema markup — it comes down to whether your content (a) **agrees with the general consensus** the AI Overview is already stating (if it says "50% of people do X," your page should state that same baseline claim) and (b) **adds your own additional expertise on top of that baseline claim.**
- **AI doesn't independently crawl the entire internet.** ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, etc. run searches against Google/Bing and synthesize an answer from roughly 5-10 of those existing results — they don't have their own separate crawl of everything. Standing claim: **"If you rank on Google, you will get recommended by AI."** Google's own crawl depth has also tightened: bots (including third-party tools like Semrush) used to see the top 100 search results; now they can only see/crawl the **top 10** — a "massive change" for downstream AI visibility tools.
- **Schema markup is not believed to move AI-citation odds beyond what's auto-generated by the platform** (Wix/WordPress/Shopify's own default schema) — custom schema is not implemented for any client, ever, on this basis. (Caveat: one case *did* show custom schema correlating with new AI Overview/side-panel appearances, but the causal credit was given to content specificity/consistency, not the schema itself — an acknowledged ambiguity even in a case being used as a positive example.)
- **AI "visibility scoring" tools (Semrush and similar) are methodologically weak/self-referential.** They have an AI generate hypothetical search prompts, then use AI again to crawl and check whether you show up in the synthetic answers — "a very backward system" that doesn't reflect what real users actually type or what AI itself weighs. **AI-referral traffic (sessions actually arriving from chatgpt.com/claude.ai/perplexity as a source) CAN be tracked in Google Analytics** — a different, genuinely measurable thing from unproven "AI visibility" scores.
- **Write for humans, not "for AI."** AI evaluates content based on how valuable it is TO humans — framing content as written to please an AI directly is the wrong mental model; optimizing for genuine human usefulness is what carries through to AI citation too.
- **Answer the target question immediately at the top of the content** — the single most consistent AEO instruction across every call, echoed identically whether the source is Matt's own experience or an AI explaining "how do I show up in AI search" back to a member who asked directly.

### Crawler & infrastructure policy
- **Never block AI crawlers, even when they're overwhelming your server.** "I firmly believe we should not be blocking AI from crawling our sites because how else are they going to recommend it?" Cloudflare has a one-click "block AI bots" toggle — don't use it. Reacting to falling click traffic by blocking AI crawlers is called "the stupidest thing" people are starting to do.
- **If AI-crawl volume is genuinely crashing your hosting, fix it with better hosting + a Web Application Firewall (WAF), not by blocking bots.** Documented case: adding FAQ schema to a small one-person-business site triggered **100,000+ simultaneous bot hits** (legitimate crawlers — Apple, Meta, etc., not attackers), crashing shared hosting for all 4 sites on that account at once (~$300/year total hosting spend); Cloudflare's AI-bot-block setting was already on and did NOT prevent the crash. Fix: move from a shared plan to cloud/VPS-tier hosting (~$30/month via a provider like Crockweb) plus a proper WAF. If bots are already flooding/indexing your site, **feed them more, don't throttle them** — publish more often, not less.
- **Private AI chats are not fed into public ranking or citation systems.** If they were, "it would be so easy to game the system." What your own chat history with Claude/ChatGPT does do is build a private context/database about your business that produces better, more specific outputs in future sessions of yours — a completely different mechanism from public ranking influence. AI's citation/recommendation engine draws only from what's publicly crawlable: your website, social media profiles, newsletters — not private chat logs.
- **Using multiple AI tools to draft content does NOT itself improve external AI-search visibility** — visibility comes from what's public on your site, not which tool wrote the draft. Multiple tools CAN meaningfully improve final content *quality*, though: draft the same questions separately in ChatGPT and Claude, then feed ChatGPT's output into Claude and ask it to identify anything the ChatGPT version did better that Claude's own draft missed, merging the best of both — costs "a couple of minutes extra."
- **Don't fully automate content creation or posting, even with agentic AI, even though it's technically possible.** Platforms can detect automated content — YouTube has announced AI-made videos get labeled as such and can detect this without creator disclosure; Google and Facebook can "tell" when things are automated too. Spend roughly **1 hour/day** being intentional about content instead (use AI to brainstorm/rewrite/generate images, not to generate final video wholesale) — every platform is pushing video specifically because real video is comparatively hard to fully automate.

### Local AEO content method
- Find a specific, narrow question your customers actually ask (test case used live: "best hot sauce pairing with a Pinot Noir") and publish content on your own site that explicitly answers it AND names your actual product/service. This is what lets AI Overviews/ChatGPT associate your business with that exact query — general topical presence in your niche isn't enough if the specific answer content doesn't exist on your site. Confirmed live: a follow-up "where can I get that locally" query excluded otherwise well-known local businesses that hadn't published that specific pairing content, even when they were the obvious real-world answer.
- **Personalized-search / narrow-expertise content strategy (forward-looking, not an immediate ranking win):** premised on Google/Gemini being positioned to win the AI race via existing calendar/Gmail/search-history data, meaning search is trending toward heavy personalization. Tactic: write blog posts answering very specific, narrow customer questions with real expertise, not generic service descriptions (example: not "we fix tires, we change tires" but "how to check tread depth on Michelin X-Ice tires using a coin/dime test"). Matt is explicit that today's version of this content "might not do much" immediately — it's an investment that pays off more as search personalization increases.
- **Top-of-funnel + bottom-of-funnel content both remain necessary** under the AI-search model: if AI trusts and cites your informational/blog (top-of-funnel) content, it's more likely to also recommend your conversion/service (bottom-of-funnel) pages as the actual solution — you still need both content types, not just one.

### Testing AI citation directly
- **Isolated single-channel test methodology** (deliberately restricted to ONE platform so you can actually attribute cause): post in a Facebook group only — not simultaneously to X/LinkedIn/anywhere else. Engagement/reactions don't matter for this test; the only failure mode to avoid is the post getting removed/unapproved by group moderators.
- **CJ's proven backlink-timing sequence** that got AI to answer with his business by name within 6 hours of a program launch: (1) roughly 2 months before launch, publish a blog post about the upcoming program and build its landing page as a placeholder — don't link it yet; (2) on launch day, publish a new post specifically about the launch; (3) immediately go back and add a backlink from the original (placeholder-era) post to the correct new program page — double-check it resolves to the right URL; (4) result — 6 hours later, an incognito search for the program name returned the business as the sole direct answer, plus one adjacent party and the relevant regulatory body.
- **Live cross-platform citation test (Matt's own demo):** write a "who's the best [service] in [city]" post naming your business at #2 or #3 with reasons why it should top the list → post the identical content simultaneously to your Facebook business page, X, and LinkedIn company page (skip hashtags on LinkedIn) → check indexing with the `site:` search operator → force/accelerate indexing via a free tool (e.g., primeindexer.com — create a project, paste the URL; observed indexing in about 7 days) → recheck the target query days later to see whether the post surfaces or gets cited. Comments reportedly do NOT get pulled into AI systems at all — a public post's negative comments don't hurt an AI-visibility test; only public-facing content (public groups/posts) counts, personal/private profiles reportedly don't.

### Social & platform-specific notes for AI visibility
- **Connecting social profiles as GSC properties** (see also §2): once connected, reports show Total Clicks/Impressions for your social posts/videos separately from your website, plus a "top performing content" breakdown and a queries report. Confirmed accepted platforms: Instagram, TikTok, X, YouTube (Threads uncertain). Feature rolls out unevenly/unpredictably per account — not having it yet isn't user error; check back periodically. Live numbers observed: **~56-63 clicks and ~130,000 impressions in just the first 2 days** after connecting a social profile; a separate later test showed a site's overall clicks at ~3,000/month with YouTube alone adding **~1,500 more clicks** in the same period once connected, with one single video responsible for **1,400** of those clicks and surfacing for **320 different keyword variations**.
- **Post the identical piece of content to every platform** rather than tailoring per platform — reaching even a 200-view minimum on each of 10 platforms is 2,000 people/day for free; platform-specific versions aren't worth the added time. Transcripts (not just captions) get indexed/cited — a keyword spoken but never typed in a caption still drove a click from search.
- **Bing Webmaster Tools** already has a live "grounding queries" report showing which AI-answer seed keywords/queries you're getting citations for and which specific pages are winning those citations — sign in directly with a Google account to import Search Console data.
- **Reddit and Quora carry real ban risk** (auto-ban/mod-ban on Reddit for posts lacking genuine engagement; a Quora account has been flagged/banned before) even though old content on both platforms — including years-old Quora posts — performs well in AI-cited answers.
- **LinkedIn needs professional framing, not looser social-platform tone.** Content has to "make sense in the business world" even for a comedian or apparel brand — Instagram/Facebook tolerate a much looser, personal tone for the same underlying business. Skip hashtags on LinkedIn entirely — per a podcast tip, LinkedIn's system reportedly treats hashtags like title tags and pulls attention away from the actual post.
- **YouTube is the second-biggest search engine**, owned by/connected to Google, and Google pulls information directly from video transcripts. Long-form video is currently comparatively hard to fake with AI (a real-person/business trust signal), though that advantage is visibly eroding as AI video generation improves. Bing/Yahoo/other engines are believed to be "a step behind" Google on the AI-search shift; Google's May-2026 keynote previewed an AI Mode interface replacing the 25-year-old type-a-keyword search bar with one that expands/refines as you type, more like prompting than searching.
- **A whole content category can lose organic demand overnight when users adopt an LLM to self-serve the underlying task** (e.g., people asking ChatGPT to "write me a dating profile" instead of searching for a paid copywriting service — confirmed industry-wide via a cross-competitor traffic check, not one business's isolated problem). The proposed response isn't more traditional SEO — it's a service-offering pivot (sell a productized "AI skill" that does the task, or reposition as coaching: "still not getting results using AI — here's why").

## 16. Reputation Defense

- **Countering a defamatory competitor "vs." page that's outranking you for your own brand name:** (1) send a cease-and-desist; (2) consider trademarking your business name to restrict the competitor's use of it; (3) outproduce them — if they have 1 comparison article, publish roughly **10** pieces of content across different sites; (4) accept there is currently no clean mechanism to get an LLM to retroactively discount or correct a false claim it's already citing as fact; (5) an escalation option Matt explicitly does NOT endorse: publicly expose the manipulation (post content stating the competitor is "hacking Google," show his real review count against his public claim, show a business-registry lookup proving true ownership behind a false ownership framing, make a video about it since the competitor isn't making videos); (6) the cleaner alternative: standard reputation management — keep any rebuttal professional, then bury the issue under a wave of positive content.
- **Reframing being attacked/hated on online:** don't engage publicly. Check whether the criticism is actually true to who you are — if not, dismiss it as a hater and move on. Applied example: after someone made a 30-minute video bashing a 30-second clip, the response was to DM the critic privately, thank him for the attention, invite him to share what's actually working for him, and propose a collaboration instead of a public fight — "I want to turn that hater into an ally."

## 17. Content Strategy, Production & Social

- **Blog cadence:** **2 quality posts/week** is the standard baseline. Build a bank of posts ahead of time (write more than you need) and schedule them out so illness/vacation/a busy stretch doesn't break the cadence — don't ramp volume up then cut back down, since that pattern looks like gaming the algorithm to Google.
- **Weekly content/keyword workflow (avoid overwhelm, avoid wasted research):** don't research keywords far in advance — trends and phrasing shift, and the research gets wasted. Instead, each week: use a question-finding tool, type in your category/what you sell, pick 1-2 keywords or questions to target that week, write the post, move on.
- **Avalanche/snowball technique:** post #1 might bring 2-3 visitors/week; post #2 the next week stacks on top (~6/week); then ~12; then ~24 — traffic compounds post over post, and it builds trust/topical authority with Google that helps harder keywords rank later. Deliberately target low-volume/low-competition long-tail keywords first to "own" a niche term (e.g., one case ranked #1-3 for a deliberately low-search-volume phrase rather than competing on a saturated broad term) — most competitors ignore these because they chase volume instead, and winning them signals to Google the site can be trusted with traffic before higher-difficulty terms become realistically winnable.
- **Niching down a saturated keyword space via platform-specific how-tos:** instead of competing on a broad, crowded term, write technical how-tos tied to specific platforms people actually search (e.g., "how to set up DMARC/DKIM in [specific email platform]"), then a natural follow-up post per platform.
- **Ecommerce keyword strategy for a genuinely unique product with no direct competitor:** name the product/page around the generic, descriptive term a buyer would actually search rather than an invented brand/proper name, then use the real product/brand name secondarily. Build a supporting blog post or collection page around the broader concept that can mention your product and even others'. If a similar product from someone else is already popular, build a "[competitor product] alternative(s)" page listing your own.
- **Writing informational content in a space dominated by big authorities** (e.g., a small ecommerce brand writing about a medical/health topic against a major hospital-system authority): still write it — if the reader finds the answer on your site, they stay in your ecosystem regardless of whether you can technically out-rank the bigger authority for that query.
- **Refresh vs. new post:** refreshing an already-indexed/ranking page is often faster to get re-evaluated by Google (it's already indexed, and if it already has substantial content that's less net-new work) — plus an old URL may already carry backlinks and internal links a brand-new URL won't have. Still create a new page/URL when the old one clearly isn't the right home for the topic.
- **Capturing tacit expertise as content:** record a subject-matter expert's natural spoken explanation of something (the specific reasoning, comparisons, and context an expert gives in person) rather than only writing generic copy — that "train of thought" explanation format is described as increasingly valuable, differentiated content.
- **Blog pillar/hub structure:** build a hub post listing multiple named methods/approaches with a one-line description each, then publish a separate, fuller post per method, linking from the hub post out to each individual method post. The hub must be a real blog post (not a static page) so it can link out to other posts.
- **Homepage FAQ + blog linking:** a core recurring question can get a permanent short-answer spot on the homepage with a "see full breakdown" link to the fuller blog post — reserve this for genuinely core, high-frequency questions, not every post.
- **Borrowed lead-magnet positioning (using someone else's course content as a lead magnet, e.g. a licensed PDF):** never repurpose someone else's content as your own — make your own version. Give it an explicit, justifiable dollar value ("$100 value — free") and be ready to defend the number (either you actually sell it at that price, or bonuses genuinely stack to it) — don't invent an unrealistic figure. Collect an email address in exchange for the download (that's the actual point), follow up via email automation/tags once they opt in, and promote the underlying course/offer across socials to drive signups into that funnel.
- **Differentiating on an IDX/MLS-feed real estate site where listings themselves can't be differentiated:** win via blog content and homepage personality — local restaurant/coffee-shop/venue reviews with your own photos and opinions, blog posts about relocating to specific areas, neighborhood breakdown pages. On social, post yourself talking inside houses (not scripted "tours") to show personality rather than just square footage.
- **Turning a live Q&A/interview into content without doing the work twice:** answer the blog-generation interview questions via voice mode or chat (don't record it) to get the blog post out. If you *also* want a video/YouTube asset, make it a genuinely separate, more engaging piece (e.g., walking around the actual location on camera) rather than trying to force one flat Q&A recording to serve both jobs.
- **Funnel/offer teardown method for researching a competitor's or a guru's course:** judge by what they actually DO in their funnel, not just what they claim in marketing — check whether they use an order bump, check whether they have a back-end/upsell offer, and sign up TWICE with two different email addresses (one where you actually buy/opt in, to see the real follow-up sequence; one where you reach checkout and abandon, to see retargeting/abandoned-cart emails and ads) — compare messaging, length, and quality between the two paths.
- **Finding what social content actually works:** post consistently for a long stretch to gather data, then go back and deliberately "overanalyze" the videos that performed best — identify the specific format/hook/setup that worked, then replicate that pattern in future posts. Seed early engagement by asking friends/family to like/comment occasionally (fine sometimes, **not on every post** — constant outside-traffic funneling stops the platform's algorithm from distributing organically on its own).
- **Cross-posting workflow:** post the identical piece everywhere rather than tailoring per platform (use a platform's built-in auto-cross-post toggle where available, e.g., Instagram → Facebook/Threads). Turning one blog post into many social posts: treat the blog post as "macro" content covering many points, then break each individual point out into its own "micro" post/video (one tip per video, not the full explanation) — test which specific points resonate, then produce more content around those.
- **Social cadence rules:** progression from **1 post/day for the first 3 months, then 3 posts/day for the next 3 months** is one documented ramp used to generate enough data to see what works; volume framing is roughly **~20 posts/week** as a target, treated as a practice/volume game — "sometimes they knock 1 out of 20 out of the park." Video-length heuristic (one creator's own derived rule, explicitly not guaranteed to work every time): **as close to exactly 31 seconds as possible**, holding attention through at least the **first 15 seconds**. **Minimum viable cadence for someone who finds camera work draining: once a month is legitimate** — "some is better than nothing"; someone for whom camera work is natural should lean into the channel much harder. **AI avatars/AI-cloned presenters kill trust** for personal-brand content — one data point showed AI-avatar-only videos capping around **12 views** before going dead, versus a typical **1,000-2,000 views/video** (one outlier hit 200,000) once the creator appeared on camera themselves.
- **Judging whether an on-page/content change worked:** wait roughly **2-3 months**, using that window to keep publishing and internally linking to the changed page/post — treated as helping "the ecosystem," not passive waiting.
- **Reframing the paid-ads question:** no fixed SEO-vs-ads ratio exists — if ads are profitable, keep running them; scale back only as organic rankings climb for the same terms. Value is highly niche-dependent: an urgent/low-consideration purchase (e.g., an emergency repair service) can convert well even at a tiny daily spend, while a considered/referral-driven service (e.g., therapy) may not convert from cheap ads at all — though a client may still justify the spend for competitive local visibility rather than lead volume. A search-driven sales spike is not "going viral" — that term describes algorithmic feed-push to people who weren't looking, while search success is intent-driven (someone actively looking); don't conflate the two.
- **Embedding social content on-site:** embed Instagram posts and YouTube Shorts on blog/website pages — increases time-on-page (a positive signal to Google) and lets visitors consume content in their preferred format. Skip embedding TikTok (may require the viewer to be signed in to view). YouTube embeds carry extra weight since Google owns YouTube. Never build a plugin/widget that auto-pulls your latest social posts onto the site — slows the site for no real value; let content exist once as a real blog post and link to/from the social version instead.

## 18. Accessibility, Legal & Trust Pages

- **Accessibility self-test:** on Windows, open the search bar, type "narrator," turn Narrator on, and let it read your site out loud — if it doesn't read what's actually on screen, that's a concrete bug to fix. Do this in addition to (not instead of) running an automated accessibility checker.
- **Reading-level and structure for the widest audience (including screen-reader users):** write at roughly an **8th-grade reading level** with no industry jargon — wider comprehension is a wider ranking net, not just an accessibility nicety. Alt text convention: treat every image like a captioned figure in a nonfiction book ("Figure 5.8") — describe what the image actually is.
- **ADA lawsuit exposure (US):** the Americans with Disabilities Act mainly obligates a "place of public accommodation" with **15 employees or more** — solopreneurs and small shops generally aren't held to the same standard. Threat letters are frequently sent by opportunistic lawyers running what amounts to a shakedown — don't panic and don't just pay; get advice first. Conformance has tiers (Single A, Double A, Triple A); your actual legal obligation depends on which level applies. (Canada note, raised but unverified against statute: a "fully accessible" obligation was described as kicking in around **50 employees**.) ADA-related lawsuit claims cited in the community as running **$100,000-$150,000**; a third-party accessibility widget (e.g., accessiBe) was described as costing roughly **$300-400/year** — "way less than one hour of a lawyer."
- **PageSpeed Insights' new "agentic browsing" score:** paste a URL at pagespeed.web.dev and look at a new section scored out of 3 — covers LLMs.txt presence, "Web MCP" (agent tool-use support, not relevant for ~99% of businesses), cumulative layout shift, and accessibility-tree well-formedness (sequential heading order). A low score here does **not** currently affect ranking in AI Overviews or ChatGPT — it only reflects whether an AI *agent* can crawl/operate the site, distinct from ranking.
- **llms.txt:** visit `yourdomain.com/llms.txt` to check if it exists. If the site runs Yoast SEO (WordPress or, via a dedicated plugin, Shopify), it's generated automatically — no manual setup or hosting needed. Not mandatory and no confirmed ranking impact yet, but low-effort and expected to matter more as agentic/AI crawling grows.
- **Legal/trust pages must be real, crawlable text on your own domain — never left as an iframe embed from a third-party document tool.** Copy the actual policy text out of the embedded tool and paste it directly onto your own page (Claude/ChatGPT can help rewrite the copy for readability, but verify legal compliance separately — "I'm not a lawyer"). A missing or iframe-hidden privacy policy/terms page "legitimizes the business" when it's real text, and its absence can actively hurt paid-ad delivery (platforms like Meta/TikTok want to see a genuine terms/privacy page and may show ads to fewer people without one).
- **SEO for gated/login-walled content:** make sure search-engine crawlers/bots can still reach and index gated content even though human users hit a login wall — look up the specific allow-pattern for your platform (other sites do this: "click this button and it'll load the rest of the page"). Also ensure all user-submitted content inside a gated area stays genuinely unique.
- **Duplicated meta descriptions across many blog posts (a common Yoast-template bug):** check the Yoast meta description field on individual posts/the category template — the likely cause is a template auto-inserting the same site-title boilerplate into every post's description field; fix inside Yoast, don't touch the URLs.

## 19. Security

- Every high-value account (domain registrar, hosting, primary email, banking) needs **2+ administrators** — never let a single person be the sole holder of credentials.
- Use a **hardware security key (e.g., YubiKey)** on important accounts — must be physically touched to complete login. Own at least **2** (losing your only key locks you out of everything).
- Don't reuse the same email address across banking and lower-security consumer accounts (streaming services, etc.) that are more likely to be breached — that shared email becomes a single point of failure for account takeover, a risk affecting the large majority of people.

## 20. Platform-Specific Gotchas

- **WordPress + Yoast SEO:** fix an auto-appended long title/H1 via Settings → Content Types → Posts (repeat for Pages) → remove the "Site title" variable auto-appended after the separator in the title template field. Reserve the **focus keyword field for money pages only** — skip it for blog posts, since the plugin's logic is built for short commercial phrases, not long-tail natural-language questions, and will flag a genuinely good long-tail blog title as "too long" incorrectly; title tag and meta description fields are fine to use on every page type. Advanced → Media Pages toggle turns off individual staff/attachment photos getting indexed as standalone pages. Auto-generates the llms.txt file and the XML sitemap with no extra setup. The Revisions panel is the go-to tool for diagnosing a ranking drop after an edit.
- **Kajabi:** a blog-index title auto-set as H1 above every post's own H1 — convert the index title to H2/paragraph text (see §7). Unused JS/CSS and large image payloads are largely unfixable on this platform (no developer-level access).
- **Avada (WordPress theme/builder):** duplicating a block for separate desktop/mobile display is the classic bug source for both duplicate H1s and doubled page content — fix via per-breakpoint settings on one block, never by duplicating the block (see §7).
- **Squarespace:** "unused JavaScript/unused CSS" audit flags are not fixable — no developer-level access on this platform. Safe to delete an unlinked orphan page an audit surfaces. Claude Code has essentially nothing to work with here — "there's not really any code on Squarespace." Canonical/trailing-slash bugs on the homepage can be genuinely hard to resolve and may need specialist/technical escalation rather than a live-call fix.
- **GoHighLevel (GHL):** has two separate ways to build a blog (a regular page vs. a dedicated "Create Blog Site" feature) — mixing them creates orphaned duplicate pages; fix by cloning the correct version to the intended URL and deleting the old orphan. Core Web Vitals issues (Cumulative Layout Shift, Total Blocking Time) are typically **unfixable** on GHL — no code/server-level access. Fine for SEO fundamentals overall, but it sacrifices CSS/site-speed control compared to WordPress.
- **Shopify:** collection pages CAN and do rank (unlike blog category pages) — worth optimizing them like money pages. Product pages in a large, thin-vendor-data catalog should rely mainly on genuinely unique spec differentiators (carat, clarity, cut, etc.) in the title/H1 rather than needing a full unique keyword phrase each; put the broad competitive keyword on the collection page instead. GSC vs. Shopify's own analytics will never fully reconcile — expected, not a bug (see §5).
- **Showit:** runs a separate sitemap from its WordPress blog integration — submit **both** sitemaps to Google Search Console.
- **Wix / Google Sites:** fine if you don't want to spend time building/maintaining a site yourself, but one documented case on Google Sites specifically "was not going to rank" regardless of effort — switching to WordPress and ranking shortly after was the resolution; treat platform choice as a real, potentially blocking variable, not a neutral detail.
- **Old WordPress installs on a legacy page builder (e.g., WP Bakery):** don't assume a rebuild or platform change is needed just because it looks dated — if it's already ranking/converting, the fix is usually just a plugin update, mobile-responsiveness repairs (images cut off/sideways), and removing defunct elements (a leftover dead social-icon link), not a rebuild.
- **Zenoti (or similar CRM/booking platforms for med spas, etc.):** auto-generates service-listing pages that Google can independently index separately from the business's own domain, even with blank metadata by default. Audit for this, fill in meta descriptions/keywords via ChatGPT for the top profit-driving services, and decide whether the CRM's pages are helping (leave them) or actively competing against your own site's equivalent pages (in which case, ask the vendor about a noindex setting, or get a custom domain/subdomain for the booking system so it lives under your own domain).
- **Don't build a website directly on Claude Code as the platform itself** — use WordPress as the underlying base, and use Claude Code to help build individual page layouts/components within it. Claude Code has real, demonstrated use inside WordPress (rebuilding a locked/default theme page, fixing Avada responsive bugs) but essentially none on Squarespace.
- **Claude Code page-build workflow (general):** tell it which theme/page-builder you're using, give it the content/info for the specific page, point it to an existing page whose layout you like as a visual reference (or attach a full-page screenshot via a tool like the "Go Full Page" Chrome extension), and ask it to build that page using the supplied information — paste the resulting code into the site and expect to need some manual touch-ups. Always back up the site first before letting an AI tool touch theme/functions files directly — "backups are your friend."

## 21. Tools & Resources Reference

- **HeyKeywords (hey-keywords.com)** — Matt's own keyword/backlink tool, positioned as a scaled-back Semrush alternative pulling from similar underlying data; **~$30/month** vs. Semrush's **~$150/month per user** (motivated by Matt's own team's Semrush spend of ~$2,000-3,000/month). Domain overview (backlinks/referring-domain counts), keyword research (volume, variations, competitive score, semantic grouping). Known gaps: per-page backlink lookup has returned 0 on pages independently confirmed to have links (a recurring, acknowledged tool limitation); the "Topic cluster" feature has had bugs; the referring-domains view only lists top domains, not the full list.
- **Semrush** — cross-check/primary keyword and backlink tool for many members: keyword volume/difficulty, Authority Score, backlink audits ("toxic" link flags — don't act on these by default, see §12), competitor/keyword-overlap analysis, site health score, and a "traffic cost" metric (dollar estimate of what the same organic traffic would cost via Google Ads — useful client-facing win stat).
- **Ubersuggest** — alternate keyword-research tool; also used to catch duplicate-metadata issues across many pages at once.

## 22. Prompt Library

Exact or near-verbatim prompt wording used live across these calls:

- **Getting unstuck on ANY unfamiliar platform/tool:** *"How do I do this in [Go High Level / Shopify / whatever platform]?"* — or paste the link to your site and ask, *"How do I change this?"*
- **Screenshot-driven troubleshooting (the most repeated pattern in the entire corpus):** take a screenshot of your current screen/error, upload it, and ask *"Where do I click? What do I do? Where do I go from here?"* paired with the explicit instruction *"Treat me like an idiot"* / *"Talk to me like I'm an idiot"* / *"Slow it down."*
- **Debugging AI-built code/integrations:** *"I'm getting this error, I don't know what to do, look into this and tell me the solution, then go fix it."* — repeat as new errors surface.
- **Setting up a CLAUDE.md project file:** *"I need you to make me a file that has this stuff in it — all these rules"* — have it include setup steps for any new project, security measures to apply by default, and an instruction to look at other existing projects for patterns before building something similar.
- **Extracting relevant info from a long transcript:** *"Knowing that I already post on social media and generally have a sense of what will/won't do well, tell me all the information in this that would be helpful and relevant to me, and give me a breakdown of how it's specifically relevant to me."*
- **Segmenting a spreadsheet into real output files:** *"Tell me who — how many people bought upgrades, how many people did this — then make me two separate spreadsheets with those lists, make them CSV files."*
- **Reining in a digressing AI:** give narrow prompts with the exact output format wanted (e.g., "give it to me in 10 points," not "give me options"); if it drifts, explicitly say *"No, we're working on this, tell me this specific thing, stay on track."*
- **Generating a privacy policy/T&C conversationally:** *"I need a privacy policy, ask me questions"* / *"Tell me what I should include and then ask me questions to get that information from me."*
- **GA4 lookups:** *"How do I find acquisition for a specific page in Google Analytics/GA4."*
- **Avada/CSS mobile-responsiveness fixes:** state the exact platform ("I'm using Avada in WordPress"), describe the problem, and have it fix responsive image ordering and text-block edge-overflow; the more specific Claude Code version: *"Make this mobile friendly in the same blocks without duplicating the content."*
- **Claude Code page-build from a reference:** *"Here's the default page, here's the code for that, I want it to look like this"* — paired with a full-page reference screenshot.
- **Claude Code WordPress page build from scratch:** tell it which theme you're using, give it the content/info for the page, point it to an existing page whose layout you like as reference, and ask it to build that page in your page builder using the supplied information.
- **ChatGPT image generation for a blog's featured image:** upload the full blog text and prompt *"Make me an image for this blog post, here's what it's called."*
- **Multi-AI cross-check for content quality:** draft the same questions separately in ChatGPT and Claude, then feed ChatGPT's output into Claude and ask it to identify anything the ChatGPT version did better that Claude's own draft missed — merge the best of both.
- **Humanizing an AI-drafted blog:** draft in ChatGPT first with fairly detailed responses (so it "doesn't sound super AI-ish"), export/create a file of "everything ChatGPT has learned about you," then feed that plus the blog drafts into Claude and let it refine/rewrite so it reads even better.
- **Getting unstuck on content ideas generally:** talk it out conversationally — *"Hey, I'm really stuck here, what can I do, do you have any ideas?"*

## 23. Thresholds & Numbers — Quick Reference

- **CTR math:** top 3 organic positions ≈ **90%** of clicks; rest of page 1 splits the remaining ~10%; page 2 ≈ **1-2%** (effectively zero).
- **Timelines:** SEO results in **3-6 months** minimum; real inflection points as late as **month 12-14**; **2 weeks with no movement is normal** ("magic" if faster). Wait **~1 month** before re-editing a freshly published/updated page. Wait **3 months, then 6 months** before touching a non-ranking page's content again. New-post indexing: **~2 hours to ~1 week** typical.
- **Word count:** competitor-relative always (§6); rough anchors seen: **750-1,500** (program baseline), **800-1,000** (a specific niche's guided target against a 500-2,500-word competitor spread), **~400-500** for a zero-volume/zero-difficulty keyword page.
- **Meta description:** **120-150 characters.**
- **Nav/click depth:** reachable within **1-2 clicks** ideally; **>3 clicks** = effectively invisible/"buried."
- **Categories:** **5-10 total**, no tags; refill a category roughly **every 2 months** or don't commit to it.
- **Plugin count:** **15 or fewer.**
- **Blog cadence:** **2 posts/week** standard baseline; **1-2/week** absolute floor for a struggling/thin site.
- **Reviews:** **~1 new review/week** is enough for professions that can't solicit directly (colleague-testimony strategy) — compounds past a stagnant higher-volume competitor within ~6 months.
- **GBP posting:** **at least 2×/week** minimum for a serious map-pack push; one aggressive test case ran 6×/day.
- **Press release cost:** roughly **$80/release** (AB Newswire); **+$30** for 2 extra newspaper placements (add-on).
- **Accessibility/legal risk:** ADA obligation mainly applies at **15+ employees** (US); Canada's "fully accessible" threshold cited around **50 employees** (unverified); ADA-related lawsuit claims cited at **$100,000-$150,000**; accessibility widget **~$300-400/year**.
- **Security:** **2+ administrators** on every high-value account; own at least **2** hardware security keys.
- **Backlink tool spread for one identical domain:** GSC Links **325** referring domains vs. HeyKeywords **~1,100** referring domains/**2,700** backlinks vs. Semrush **~1,300** referring domains.
- **AI Overview citation consistency:** roughly **~30%** across repeated searches of the same keyword in one test.
- **Google crawl depth:** bots/tools now generally see only the **top 10** search results (used to be top 100).
- **New-query growth:** roughly **15%** of Google searches are brand-new every day (cited as the reason zero historical volume isn't proof of zero future demand).
- **Location-page rollout pace:** roughly **1 new page/week** (2/week aggressive); large SKU/catalog batches at roughly **20/week.**
- **Social cadence:** **1×/day → 3×/day** ramp (3-month blocks) is one documented progression; **~20 posts/week** as a volume-game target; minimum viable cadence for camera-averse creators: **1×/month.**
- **Video-length heuristic (one creator's own, not universal):** **~31 seconds**, holding attention through the **first 15 seconds.**
- **HeyKeywords vs. Semrush pricing:** **~$30/month** vs. **~$150/month per user** (Semrush).
- **ChatGPT Advertiser Program:** charges triggered every **$25** of spend; ~48-hour approval.
- **AI-bot crawl-crash case:** **100,000+** simultaneous bot hits crashed shared hosting costing **~$300/year**; fix hosting tier **~$30/month.**

## 24. Case Studies

- **The bookkeeper in St. Petersburg, FL (same business across two separate calls, ~2 weeks apart):** first session — on-page keyword-density research split the target phrase into a service term (used ~10× by competitors) and a location term (~4×) across competitor pages ranging **~500-2,500 words**; guided toward ~800-1,000 words with matching-plus-one frequency. Second session, roughly two weeks later — a heading-tag check revealed even some top-ranked competitor pages for the head term **don't have an H1 tag at all**; the term was judged too competitive to keep chasing on the homepage, and the pivot was to stop optimizing the homepage for it, shift effort into blog posts + easier long-tail variants ("bookkeeping small business," "near me"), and finish out the Google Business Profile.
- **A Toronto wedding photographer/filmmaker:** wasn't showing up even on page 10 of Google despite GSC signup, sitemap submission, and a connected GBP — diagnosed as likely "a matter of trust" (accumulated authority) rather than a technical bug. Homepage was targeting two keywords at once (photographer + filmmaker) using invented marketing phrasing ("romantic storytelling wedding photography in Toronto") instead of real search phrasing ("wedding photographer Toronto") — rewritten to "Timeless wedding photography in Toronto." In a later check, GSC showed a blended average position of **27** for the exact target phrase despite not appearing in the first 15+ pages of a manual search — resolved by explaining the page-vs-query average-position gap.
- **A moving company, two locations, ~1 year of consistent map-pack work:** posted to GBP 2×/week minimum, geotagged every photo with the target keyword, chained posts via share-links, built service-area pages linked to matching GBP categories, and asked for reviews continuously (3-4 new reviews/week) — sits in/near the top-3 map pack as "a very consistent lead generator." A much older, completely inactive competitor (no posts, ~1 review/week, decades in business, 3× the review count) still outranks it "by a mile" — attributed to longevity/authority rather than any tactic gap.
- **An 8-figure cosmetics/haircare manufacturer (in business since 2009):** paid a directory/lead-gen service roughly $1,000+/month for ~10 years until its pricing model changed; left, built its own site over ~6 months (fixed a homepage that had never even stated what the company makes, started blogging) — reported more leads in the first week on the new site than from the old paid directory service. Nearly all ranking keywords are branded (business-name variants); only 2 backlinks total, both unintentional (from a competitor's own "top 5 manufacturers" list). Demonstrates: brand recognition + paid lead-gen can substitute for organic SEO for years, but the upside of finally doing SEO can be immediate.
- **A Hamilton-area realtor branded around a physical trait ("the bearded realtor"):** ignores all SEO advice entirely, yet ranks inside Google's AI Overview for a highly specific branded query — sources cited were social posts (a colleague's Facebook post about him, his own Instagram posts), not his website at all. Organic results below were dominated almost entirely by the two people's own faces, with almost no other competitors visible on that results page — used repeatedly as proof that social-content signals alone can win AI/organic visibility without a traditional SEO program.
- **A therapy/counseling practice that blogged consistently for ~12 months:** doesn't show as "ranking" in Semrush or any other tool for its target terms at all, yet runs a 3-month waitlist when actually searched by a real prospective client — illustrating that rank-tracking tools can completely miss real-world lead generation.
- **A dating-profile-writing service (~40,000 profiles written historically):** a prior SEO vendor had keyword-stuffed the site; a rewrite doubled conversions — then industry-wide traffic collapsed simultaneously (confirmed across the client's competitors too, not an isolated problem), attributed to searchers going directly to ChatGPT/Claude to generate their own dating profiles instead of searching for a paid service. The business owner has screenshots showing his brand surfacing inside ChatGPT answers, but that hasn't converted to revenue, and he's resistant to using AI himself to help fix the funnel — "something still has to tip back over somehow."
- **A real-estate continuing-education program (recurring case across multiple calls):** consolidated a dated "Annual Commission Update [Year]" page (still ranking #1 for a certification process that had since expired, generating confused inbound calls) into one evergreen URL with redirects from every old dated version; separately used the CJ backlink-timing sequence (§15) to get an AI answer engine to name the business by name within 6 hours of a new program's launch; and, in a later call, lost ranking ground on a top keyword after competitors responded with their own changes — the fix was matching their moves while continuing to publish low-competition content in parallel.
- **A construction/custom-builder company that lost its #1 ranking (dropping to roughly #250) years earlier** after a URL migration mishap, then relaunched on a hosting-company template that was "an absolute disaster" and had to be rebuilt via a freelance marketplace in ~2 days: landed a ChatGPT-sourced referral from the North American representative of a premium international cabinetry manufacturer after 5 years of trying to land that distributorship — a deal estimated near **$5 million** if it closes, requiring a **$50,000-$75,000** co-funded warehouse/showroom build-out.

## 26. Tradeoffs & Nuance

- **A technically "broken" page should stay broken if it's already ranking.** Duplicate H1s, keyword stuffing, thin content, zero backlinks — none of it matters once a page is genuinely ranking and converting; "fixing" it for correctness risks breaking what's actually driving the business. The one carve-out: UX/CTA polish is still fair game, since that doesn't touch the content/structure signals that could shift rankings.
- **Word count, heading count, and keyword frequency all cut in both directions** — the same underlying rule ("match competitors") means sometimes the fix is trimming excess and sometimes it's adding depth, depending purely on what the actual top-3 pages show. There is no universal "more is better" or "less is better."
- **Geotagging, custom schema, and the reviews-microsite tactic are all genuinely disputed, low-cost, outcome-based bets** rather than settled mechanisms — Matt's own stated position on each is some version of "the cost is low enough to test regardless of whether we can prove why it works."
- **Toxic/suspicious backlinks and freshly-flagged competitor links are asymmetric risks:** audit tools flag them confidently, but removing them has real observed downside (traffic loss) against unproven upside (no confirmed harm from leaving them) — the asymmetry argues for leaving them alone unless the site is already failing outright.
- **AI citation and organic rank are decoupled, and AI citation itself is unstable session-to-session** (~30% consistency across repeated identical searches in one test) — a single "why did a competitor beat me in the AI Overview" comparison is unreliable; look for patterns (e.g., curated list-page sourcing) rather than one-off snapshots.
- **Chasing 100% on any audit/scoring tool is a trap** — 80%+ (Page Audit) or a "fantastic" 90% (site health) is enough; the incremental gap between "good" and "perfect" rarely changes rank and reliably wastes time that could go to publishing or backlinks. The exception is a paid SEO provider's own site, which faces a higher credibility bar from prospects than a typical client site does.
- **Enterprise/corporate SEO clients pay far more than small businesses but bring execution paralysis** (red tape blocking recommended changes from ever shipping) and, in Matt's framing, deliver less real personal/economic satisfaction than small-business work — an explicit reason for prioritizing small-business clients emotionally, not only financially.
- **A whole content category can lose organic demand overnight to LLM self-serve** — the response isn't more traditional SEO, it's a service-offering pivot (productize the AI task itself, or reposition around "still not getting results with AI — here's why").
- **Location pages: fully unique content per location is the safer long-term bet**, but Matt concedes duplicate-with-swapped-locale-name pages are what many real competitors are doing and reportedly ranking with in low-competition niches — the resolution is to try the harder/unique approach first, give it 3-4 months, and only simplify toward the templated approach if it fails.
- **Scaling a community or business can directly conflict with personalized service depth** — raising a price specifically to slow new-signup volume (rather than to extract more from existing members) is presented as a deliberate lever to protect the depth of 1:1 attention existing members get, at the explicit cost of growth speed.
- **Full content/posting automation is technically available now but deliberately not used** — the tradeoff is detection risk (platforms increasingly label/flag AI-made content) and authenticity loss, weighed against real time savings; even members using AI heavily for drafting still manually edit before publishing.
