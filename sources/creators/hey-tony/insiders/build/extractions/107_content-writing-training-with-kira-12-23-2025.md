# Content Writing Training with Kira — 12.23.2025

## Tactics & step-by-step how-to
- Keyword-testing method ("throw spaghetti at the wall"): take a seed phrase, punch several worded variants into a keyword-research tool one at a time, and compare volume + difficulty side by side rather than trusting any single AI-suggested phrase. Demoed live in Semrush: "we buy junk cars, Dallas, Texas" (vol 20 / difficulty 10) vs. "junk car buyer, Dallas" (vol 210, higher volume and lower difficulty) — picked the second because higher volume AND easier.
- For service-area pages (no physical location, multiple cities/neighborhoods): test differentiating phrases per page (e.g., "we buy junk cars, Dallas, Texas" / "we buy junk cars, Dallas" / swap "junk" for another word people use) in the keyword tool and keep whichever variant wins on volume+difficulty for that page — don't assume the AI's first suggestion is optimal.
- Long-tail keyword discovery method: start typing a question stub into the keyword tool ("is roll on perfume...", "what roll on perfume...", "where roll on perfume...") and read the autosuggested keyword-idea list; add/remove words repeatedly ("perfume ingredients" → "what ingredients perfume" → "how ingredients perfume" → "why ingredients perfume") until one shows real volume.
- Keyword Gap tool workflow (used live in Semrush) for "I have no idea what to write about": pick 2-3 competitor domains in your niche, run Keyword Gap against your own domain, then look at the "Missing" category (keywords competitors rank for that you don't) — those are your next content targets. Ignore "Shared" keywords (both already rank — targeting those again would be cannibalization).
- Diagnosing a zero-traffic site (case walkthrough): (1) eyeball the site design first for confusion signals (competing CTAs, hard-to-read layout) that could be inflating bounce rate; (2) confirm whether a real keyword-research tool was used at all when the content was written; (3) check how many keywords the site currently ranks for as a baseline; (4) use the keyword tool to find high-volume/low-difficulty terms in the niche, including terms adjacent to the core service (e.g., general "skin" questions for a skin clinic) purely to build topical signal even when they won't convert directly; (5) for already-published service pages, run the Hey Tony Chrome extension page-check (title, meta description, keyword-in-title match, word count, external links) before deciding whether to rewrite pages or just add new blog content.
- Page vs. blog optimization is a different action: "optimizing a page" = editing that specific page's on-page elements (title, meta, body); "adding blog posts" = net-new pages that add fresh traffic/topical coverage. If existing service pages already check out (keyword in title, decent word count, external links present), prioritize writing new informational blog posts over further tweaking existing pages.
- Focus-keyword/title alignment check (used live via the Hey Tony content analyzer on Carl's contractor blog): if the tool's stated focus keyword doesn't appear in the actual title, that's a flagged disconnect — fix by either changing the title to include the keyword or changing the keyword to match search intent. Also make the URL slug match the primary keyword.
- Reframing a negative-intent keyword into a positive-intent one without losing the original content: Carl's blog was built around "bad contractor" (a phrase nobody actually searches), but the underlying pain-point content (price that was never real, vague scope, disappearing act/silent treatment, quality issues noticed too late, permit/licensing risk) was kept and simply nested as H2 subheadings/body under a new positive-intent focus keyword/title ("What does a contractor do?").
- Secondary-keyword generation prompt pattern: ask the AI for a batch of secondary keywords tied to your primary keyword, then only use the ones that naturally fit the piece — no obligation to work every suggestion in.
- Building topical authority without cannibalizing: differentiate near-duplicate topics by changing the *content format/angle*, not just the wording — e.g., one page structured as "12 ways to use honeysuckle perfume" (listicle) vs. another structured as "what's in honeysuckle roll-on" (ingredients/explainer).
- For local/location pages specifically: don't just swap the city name in an otherwise identical template — spend real copy talking about the location itself (why Dallas needs this, what's specific to Houston) so each city page is substantively different content, not a keyword with a city name pasted in twice.

## Rules / dos & don'ts
- One primary/focus keyword per page or blog post — never two pieces of content (a page and a blog, or two blogs) targeting the exact-match same keyword. That is the actual and only definition of keyword cannibalization.
- Different locations = different keywords by definition — "we buy junk cars, Dallas" and "we buy junk cars, Houston" are not cannibalization, no matter how similar the surrounding content is.
- Topical authority (many pages in a similar keyword family) is fine and is not cannibalization, as long as (a) no two pages exact-match the same keyword, and (b) the content itself is meaningfully different, not just re-skinned.
- Focus keyword must appear in the title and the meta description; URL slug should match the primary keyword.
- Don't create blog categories when you're just starting out — wait until you've published roughly 10-20 blog posts; categories will become obvious in hindsight, and boxing yourself into categories too early can itself become a reason not to write.
- When starting out, prioritize quantity over quality to build the habit/"get the reps in" — quality-vs-quantity flips once you have a base of content.
- Volume + difficulty are the two metrics that matter in a keyword-research tool: volume = estimated monthly searches (tools are estimates, never exact); difficulty = how hard to rank (color-coded green=low/easy, red=high/hard). Target high volume + low difficulty.
- It's fine to write a piece for a near-zero-volume keyword (e.g., ~20/month) if the difficulty is very low, or if it answers a real recurring customer question — you won't get much traffic, but it teaches Google what your site/business is about and adds to perceived expertise/resource depth.
- Don't chase volume/difficulty numbers for secondary keywords the way you do for the primary keyword — Kira's personal rule is to skip that scoring for secondary/variant keywords entirely (see caveat under Tradeoffs — she flags this as her own approach, not confirmed as Matt's).
- No universal cap on blog posts per week — depends entirely on the operator's capacity; the goal is sustainable output, not a fixed target, and posting a genuinely bad post to build the habit beats not posting at all.
- Historical/industry-level rule of thumb: after AI made mass content production possible, sites that pushed to ~500 pages/week saw quality (and rankings) drop — Google respalliating against low-quality mass output is described as expected, not a bug.
- Agency (Kira/Matt's) client cadence benchmarks: packages run at either 2 blogs/week or 3 blogs/week, i.e., roughly 8/month or 12/month.
- For a solo operator with limited bandwidth, a workable cadence is ~1 blog/week, optionally paired with a short "teaser" post earlier in the week (e.g., publish the full blog Friday, teaser Monday).

- **Mangools** ("Mangoes") — keyword research tool; gives a limited number of free searches per day.

- **Ubersuggest** (Neil Patel's tool) — mentioned as another affordable keyword-research option.

- **Hey Tony Site Audit Tool** — flags on-page technical issues (used live for an accessibility question: insufficient button color-contrast ratio, button name not accessible).
- **Hey Tony Chrome Extension** — page-level SEO checker; shows title, meta description, focus-keyword match, word count, and external-link presence for a given page.

## Case studies / examples / results
- **D — junk car recycling/buying business.** Brand-new domain, no backlinks, no domain authority, no Google Business Profile, single service, 7 service-area cities with 3 neighborhoods each (~21-24 location pages under consideration). ChatGPT had recommended one root keyword ("junk car buyer") repeated across every city/neighborhood page, differentiated only by geography. D pushed back with three concerns: (1) is this cannibalization? (2) is this a sound strategy for a zero-authority new site specifically? (3) won't 21-24 pages hammering the same keyword look manipulative to Google? Kira: confirmed geography-based differentiation is not cannibalization as long as exact-match keyword differs and content differs; endorsed the topical/location-authority approach; live-tested Semrush and found "we buy junk cars, Dallas" (vol 210) beat both "we buy junk cars, Dallas, Texas" (vol 20 / difficulty 10) and D's original higher-difficulty "junk car buyer" root (D cited difficulty ~14, described as "higher end of the low," vs. a stated low-end floor of 6). Did not directly address whether zero domain authority should change the strategy.
- **Fragrance/perfume examples (Kira's own hypothetical brand, used repeatedly for teaching)** — Keyword Gap demo: "Fragrance X" (95,000 ranking keywords) vs. competitors "Perfume Direct" (11,000) and "Perfume.com" (108,000); the gap tool surfaced "mini perfumes" as a Missing-category opportunity at 18,000 volume / 28 difficulty, called a strong target (high volume, low-ish difficulty).
- **FK — static/aesthetic clinic website** (site referenced in chat, transcribed inconsistently as "library.com" / a ".uk" domain; US-domain business operating for a UK client base). Live-published for over a year, blogs and pages built by a separate web developer who did use some keyword research, but the site was ranking for only about 3 keywords total. Kira's live diagnosis: flagged the homepage as hard to read with two competing calls-to-action (possible bounce-rate driver); recommended getting on the Web Design for SEO call for design-specific help; then demoed keyword discovery — rejected "how to know if a mole is cancer" (high volume but wrong/oversaturated angle) and "mole" itself (pulled exterminator/animal results, wrong intent), pivoted to broader "skin" queries (e.g., "how to get hair dye off skin") as topically-adjacent content to build authority even without being a direct service. Checked an existing "anti-wrinkle injection" service page via the Hey Tony Chrome extension: title had the keyword, meta description flagged as "a little long," keyword volume 110 with low/easy difficulty, word count 1,270 (called "a good word count"), external link present — page judged already reasonably optimized. Recommendation: since existing pages check out, prioritize writing new informational blog posts (using the keyword tool for volume/difficulty) over further page-level edits.
- **Carl — Heritage Custom Kitchens Remodeling** (contractor, solo operator). Blog drafted earlier in a working session with Matt but not yet live (waiting on web designer); Carl used the free Hey Tony content analyzer and found a mismatch: stated focus keyword "bad contractor," but the actual title was "The problems with hiring a contractor that no one talks about from the inside" (keyword absent from title). Kira reasoned people search from a positive angle ("good contractor," "what to look for in a contractor"), not "bad contractor," then found via Semrush "what does a contractor do" at 3,600 volume / 24 difficulty — called this "delicious," easy-to-rank-for and high-volume. Recommended keeping all the original pain-point content (the price that was never real; the vague scope that turns into landmines; the disappearing act/silent treatment; quality issues only noticed too late; permits/licensing risk shifted onto the homeowner) but restructuring it as H2s/body under the new positive-intent focus keyword and title, with the slug matching the new primary keyword.
- **Kira's own agency content performance** (self-reported, no client named): currently posting 3 blogs/week for a client and reports 7 of the last 12 monthly blog posts ranking on page 1 of Google.

## Tradeoffs & nuance
- Kira explicitly flags her secondary-keyword approach (skip volume/difficulty scoring entirely, just check topical relevance) as her personal habit, not a confirmed team-wide rule — she says she'd want to check with Matt on whether he sees it the same way.
- Topical authority via many similar pages walks right up to the line of cannibalization visually (same core keyword repeated dozens of times across a site) — the distinction Kira draws is entirely about exact-match keyword uniqueness plus real content-angle variation, not about the number of pages or how similar they look at a glance.
- D's specific worry — that a brand-new, zero-authority, zero-backlink domain might need a different keyword strategy than an established site — was not directly resolved; Kira endorsed the topical/location approach generally without addressing whether thin authority changes the calculus.
- Kira predicts secondary keywords may become a less meaningful technique within roughly two years, as Google/AI gets better at detecting genuine topical coverage rather than needing surface-level keyword-stuffing signals to be told a page is "about" something; she frames this as speculation, not settled fact.
- Choosing a keyword isn't purely volume-maximizing — a low-volume keyword is worth writing if the difficulty is trivial or if it's a real, recurring question from actual customers, because the payoff (Google/AI understanding your expertise, site depth, dwell time) isn't purely traffic-denominated.
- Blog vs. social-media content is framed as an intent-and-personality match, not a strict either/or: platforms where people are actively searching (Google, and by this framework Pinterest/YouTube too) suit purchase/research-intent content (blogs); platforms people browse to kill time (scrolling while waiting, etc.) suit entertainment-intent content (social/short-form video). Overlaid on that: a "journaler"/writer personality fits blogging/newsletters better, while someone comfortable "blabbing" on camera fits social video better — and content can cross over (a blog can become a video script; a podcast can become a blog).
- Posting cadence has no single right answer — it's bounded above by quality collapse (the post-AI "500 pages/week" example) and bounded below by the risk of never shipping at all; the right number is whatever an individual operator can sustain without burning out, adjusted upward once capacity/help increases.
