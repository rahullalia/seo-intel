## Tactics & step-by-step how-to

- **Generic "why doesn't it rank" diagnostic sequence Matt used on every site this call:** (1) confirm the literal target keyword string actually appears on the page, (2) pull keyword variations + difficulty/volume in Semrush and compare the current target against easier variants, (3) check internal-link anchor text pointing at/away from the page for topical dilution, (4) for local businesses, check Google Business Profile, directory listings, address visibility, and reviews, (5) open whoever currently ranks #1-3 and diagnose what they do differently (depth of content, structure, local signals).
- **Keyword selection:** pull keyword variants in Semrush's keyword tool, prioritize by difficulty (KD) score, and go after low-KD keywords first — these can rank with zero backlinks, so they're the fastest path to traction before investing in link building.
- **Verifying a suspicious/flagged backlink is real:** open the linking page's View Page Source and Ctrl/Cmd+F for your own domain string — if it isn't in the code, the backlink tool's data is stale or wrong (this is exactly what happened to Susan; see case studies).
- **Cross-checking backlink-tool data:** compare Semrush's referring-domains list against Google Search Console → Links → "top linking sites" as a second source before trusting either alone.
- **Handling a flagged "link farm"/toxic-backlink warning:** identify the specific domains actually driving the pattern; check first whether you personally own/control any of them (old PBNs, abandoned old sites) — if you do, log in and manually delete the outbound links yourself rather than disavowing.
- **Getting a bad link removed from a site you don't own:** contact the site owner directly, name the exact page/link, and ask for removal; expect some owners to demand payment (quoted example: ~$50/link) and others to comply free; follow up to confirm it's actually gone.
- **After any link removal (own PBN or third-party outreach):** wait — Semrush/Google re-crawl lag means the tool and your rankings won't reflect the change immediately.
- **Checking spam score:** use a free spam-score-checker tool (paste domain → "Check Spam") since Semrush itself doesn't surface spam score directly; same tool also returns Domain Authority, Page Authority, dead-link count, and domain age in one pass.
- **For a local business with no web presence at all:** prioritize getting a real Google Business Profile live before anything else — it alone can outrank fully built competitor websites (see DND Towing case).
- **For rank-and-rent sites specifically:** plan for a longer timeline unless/until you can establish a real address + GBP eligibility path; don't assume the same playbook as an owned local business.
- **Bilingual sites:** keep language versions on subfolders, not subdomains, if the goal is for Google to treat the whole thing as one site — a subdomain gets treated as a separate site (confirmed live on Manny's FR-main/EN-subdomain setup).
- **Auditing internal anchor text sitewide:** if a page links to the homepage (or any other page) using the exact keyword you want a *different* page to rank for, that anchor text is telling Google the wrong page is about that topic — audit and correct anchor text to match the actual intended target page per keyword.
- **Site architecture for service businesses:** build standalone, dedicated service pages and standalone area/location pages — don't bury area-served content inside an "About Us" page or similar; only build a location page where there's confirmed search volume behind that location name.
- **Before building a location/suburb page:** check search volume for that specific town/neighborhood name first. Separately, figure out how people in that specific market actually search — by hyperlocal neighborhood name or by county/city — and prioritize accordingly (in Scott's DC-area case, town name signals income level, so granular pages for the wealthier towns were worth prioritizing).
- **Redesigning a page against top competitors:** open whoever ranks #1-3 for the target keyword and note specific structural/content choices (content depth, use of both the acronym and the fully spelled-out term, one single clear CTA, whitespace, locality named immediately above the fold) — adapt those elements, don't copy-paste their actual content.
- **URL cleanup:** strip "near me"/"near you" and unnecessary category-folder slugs out of URLs; use a flat pattern like `service-city` (e.g. landscape-lighting-montreal) instead of nested category + "near me" strings.
- **Avoiding self-cannibalization across owned/related sites:** when two sites you control could target the same keyword, don't let them compete head-on — assign each site a distinct keyword target and connect them via internal backlinking/recommendation copy instead (e.g., a hub site publishes "top 10 restaurants in [area]" and links out to/recommends the client restaurant site, rather than both targeting "restaurant [area]" directly).
- **Managing a multi-site portfolio:** keep one spreadsheet (or one Notion database) per site, not one shared master sheet across every property — track each site's money pages and blog-post keyword targets independently so keyword targeting doesn't collide across the portfolio.
- **Spot-checking LLM/AI-search visibility:** run your exact target query directly in ChatGPT and compare who it recommends against who ranks organically in Google and who appears on authoritative directory/list pages (e.g. a .gov business list) — gives a read on what currently feeds AI-assistant recommendations for that niche.
- **Merging duplicate Google Business Profiles:** if you have login access to both duplicate listings for one location, contact Google directly through the Business Profile interface to request a merge and specify which listing to keep (also possible to flag one as a duplicate via GBP's own reporting flow, but Google acting on it isn't guaranteed).

## Rules / dos & don'ts

- Never put "near me" or "near you" literally in on-page copy or in URLs. It does nothing for rankings — Google geolocates the searcher/device automatically. Matt: this is "amateur SEO," a sign whoever built the site "did not know what they're doing."
- Big brands (Yelp, Amazon, Home Depot) can rank while breaking normal SEO best practices, purely on domain authority/trust — don't use them as a model for "what good SEO looks like."
- `.gov` domains carry outsized built-in authority (only real government entities can register them) and are very hard to outrank — if a `.gov` directory lists businesses in your category, get listed on it.
- A Google Business Profile with zero website can still outrank fully built competitor sites in local results (DND Towing: ranked with no website at all, just a GBP + Facebook page, 2.2-star rating).
- Missed/unanswered phone calls hurt local rankings — Google reads poor call responsiveness as a bad user-outcome signal.
- One keyword per page — every page on a site should target exactly one keyword.
- Never let two sites you control target the same keyword with the same tactics — you likely end up ranking on neither.
- A Google Business Profile's location must match the property/business's actual physical address, even when the target searchers live elsewhere (e.g. a Mexico vacation rental targeted at US/Canada bookers still needs its GBP pinned in Mexico) — local pack/map visibility is driven by where the pin sits, not where the searcher is.
- Hidden or auto-collapsing on-page text (e.g. breadcrumbs that flash and then disappear on scroll) is bad for Google — never hide navigational/text elements from the crawl-visible state.
- If your niche has both a spelled-out full term and a common acronym (e.g. "International English Language Testing System" vs. "IELTS"), use both forms somewhere on the page — searches happen in both forms.
- Don't lead a page with an aggressive, too-early conversion ask (e.g. "35% off, sign up now") before the visitor has enough context about what's being offered.
- A page should have exactly one call-to-action color, visually distinct from everything else on the page — don't run 3-4 competing accent colors plus multiple simultaneous CTAs (e.g. "call now" + "download your free report" + more).
- **Never disavow backlinks.** Matt, verbatim: "in all the years that I've been doing SEO, we have never disavowed links... my advice is never disavow links. Never." It's impossible to know which toxic-looking link may actually be propping up your rankings; clients who disavowed after a scary Semrush/GSC notification have seen rankings drop as a result.
- Spam score benchmarks: should stay under roughly 11%, ideally around 1%; 1% is effectively the practical floor (you can't hit 0%). Anything meaningfully above that range, go audit the backlink profile.
- Older domains are generally easier/faster to rank, but a brand-new domain isn't automatically doomed to a multi-year wait — it can rank quickly depending on keyword difficulty, content, and local signals.
- Low-difficulty keywords can and do rank with zero backlinks — prioritize these before spending time or money on link building.
- Don't build a location/suburb page just because the town exists — verify search demand for that exact town name first.

## Tradeoffs & nuance

- Rank-and-rent local sites are structurally harder to pull off now — Google increasingly expects local trust signals (GBP, directories, address, citations) that a pure lead-gen operator may not be able to fully obtain. Matt's framing: "not impossible," but a longer, harder path than it used to be.
- Big-brand exceptions (Yelp, Amazon, Home Depot, `.gov` listings) mean "copy what the top-ranked result does" isn't universal advice — some pages rank purely on domain trust, not technique, and shouldn't be used as a content/structure template.
- ChatGPT's live business recommendations favored 5-star-rated businesses but did not appear to weight for review volume/count — a business with fewer total reviews surfaced ahead of one with more, purely on star rating.
- Prioritizing by keyword difficulty means an obviously relevant head term (e.g. "Boston Towing") may not be worth chasing yet — pursue a realistic easier variant first and treat the harder term as a longer-run target.
- Backlink acquisition is framed as expensive, slow, and adversarial ("everybody wants money because everybody knows the game") — the reason given for prioritizing low-KD keywords that can rank without any link building, not a claim that links never matter at all.
- Removing a bad link via direct outreach isn't free or guaranteed goodwill: some site owners demand payment (quoted ~$50/link in Matt's own case) rather than remove it for free — the "clean" alternative to disavowing can still cost money.
- Small-town/suburb landing pages are a case-by-case call: worthless with no search volume behind the town name, but genuinely valuable in markets (like DC's suburbs) where hyperlocal names carry income/status signal that broader county names don't — the same tactic can be right or wrong depending on how that specific local market actually searches.
- Tool-reported local rankings shouldn't be trusted at face value: Semrush's local-pack simulation diverged from a live, in-market Google search in Scott's case, reportedly because of a too-broad default location setting — always sanity-check local/map position against a real search from the target geography.
- Disavowing vs. direct removal: disavowing is framed as strictly worse — even a link that looks objectively bad on paper (high spam score, "link farm" warning) might be contributing positive ranking signal, so the recommended path is always identify-and-remove-at-the-source (if you control it) or negotiate-removal (if you don't), never disavow.
