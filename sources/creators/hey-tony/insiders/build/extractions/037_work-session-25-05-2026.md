## Tactics & step-by-step how-to

- **Standard blog-writing workflow (live demo):**
  1. Find a question to answer: go to app.heytonyinsiders.com → click **Tools** → **Blog Idea Finder** → type what you sell (demo used "Nail Salon") to surface candidate questions. Alternative source: **findquestions.com**.
  2. Paste the interview-starter prompt into your AI, quoted: **"I'm writing a blog post answering this question [paste the question]."** It interviews you, asking questions one at a time.
  3. Answer the interview using voice mode. Matt's own stack: **ChatGPT** for this step specifically, because "they have the best transcription service or the best voice to... or voice to text."
  4. Copy all your answers into **Claude** and prompt (quoted): **"Write me this blog post using this information"** — it drafts the post.
  - Matt: "if you want to use something else, you want to start with Claude, go for it. Use whatever ones you want" — stack isn't mandatory, Gemini/ChatGPT/Claude are interchangeable for the draft step.
- **Alternative topic-ideation method for a maturing blog (Marita, 23-24 posts in):** compile an overview of every post title written so far, feed it to an LLM (she used Gemini that day) with the prompt, quoted verbatim: **"here, my blog post I write so far and please made me three suggestion as what makes sense and what is useful for me and the community as to write next."** Goal: pick topics with more consistency/relevance instead of randomly; got three good suggestions back and wrote one that day.
- **Diagnosing keyword cannibalization with the Keyword Conflict tool (live demo on Laurie's site):**
  1. Enter the page (Matt just used the homepage here) into the tool; it scans the whole site and lists other pages competing for the same keyword.
  2. Cross-check the flagged URLs against **sitemap.xml** and **robots.txt** to see if they're real indexed pages — on Laurie's site, the flagged "competing" URLs weren't in either file.
  3. Root-cause found by inspection: the flagged URLs were date-based slugs (e.g. resolving to `.../202605/22`) auto-generated from each blog post's publish date, which simply redirect back to the homepage — not real content pages, but the tool still flags them because each blog post technically links to one.
  4. Fix: remove/turn off the date link entirely ("we just need to remove the link from this page… turn off the date, you don't need the date on there"). Relay the fix instructions to the site builder (Leanne) via the call recording.
- **Debugging a mystery CSS border (Avada/WordPress, live demo on Marita's site):** inspect element on the container, then each column; check computed margin, border-box, padding, and border-position values layer by layer. Matt's own attempt got as far as isolating it to a `social wrapper` div with padding/border computed to 0 — couldn't pinpoint the true source live. When stuck: post in the community forum and tag **Denroy** (needs site access to dig in); **Leanne** can also help.
- Matt's own note on where SEO time actually goes: "the writing part of SEO at this point, it doesn't take that long compared to finding images, doing the internal linking, and all that other stuff" — implying the bottleneck to plan around is imagery + internal linking, not drafting.

## Rules / dos & don'ts (keep every threshold, number, specific)

- Don't panic if a new post ranks well immediately (Bertha's posts hit #1-2 on page 2 in week one) and then dips in week two/three — normal; Google is still settling where the page belongs. "Keep writing, keep doing the internal linking and have faith, have patience."
- Don't start going back to re-optimize old posts too early — keep writing new content until roughly **30, 40, 50 posts** published (Matt's threshold, given to Bertha who has ~10 posts and writes 1/week), because indexing/traffic takes time to show up.
- Google shows only **one page per website per keyword** in results — so don't target your homepage's primary keyword inside your blog posts; use secondary/variant keywords in the blogs instead to avoid self-cannibalization.
- If a page's slug/link redirects to the homepage (e.g. a leftover date-based archive link), remove the link outright — it will still get flagged by cannibalization-scanning tools as a competing page even though it isn't real content.
- Data-privacy guidance (one member's industry rule, real estate–adjacent): turn off Gmail's connected-inbox setting, or use a separate/secure email address, if sensitive documents (e.g. real-estate transaction paperwork) pass through it — because Gmail content can now feed AI personalization.
- Matt's own counter-stance on the same privacy question: don't overcomplicate it — "you could make a different email address. But let's not over complicate. Let's just keep living our lives, guys."

## Tradeoffs & nuance

- Google's new AI-mode personalization reportedly draws on Gmail, calendar, and contacts (who you're emailing, upcoming events) — a real exposure risk for anyone routing sensitive documents through Gmail (real-estate transaction paperwork was the example raised) — but whether disabling connected-inbox settings actually helps or hurts the quality/relevance of your own personalized AI results is an open, unconfirmed question nobody on the call could answer. Matt: "I don't know if it helps hurts or if there's anything in the notes."
- The same agency simultaneously runs "literally everything" through Google Workspace (keyword research sharing, Looker/Data Studio reporting) while being wary of Gmail-driven AI personalization — Matt's resolution is to not worry about it since nothing sent to clients is inherently sensitive.
- Every keyword tool disagrees with every other one (Semrush vs. Ahrefs vs. Hey Keywords) because each uses its own estimation methodology/data sources — "none of them match up with each other anyways" — so treat any single tool's volume number as directional (does this keyword get real usage, what are the variations), not as ground truth.
- Hey Keywords trades precision for usability: Matt states outright its numbers won't match Semrush, but the pared-down ~4-5-option interface removes the analysis-paralysis that Semrush's breadth causes for less technical members (explicitly named as the reason Marita, AJ, and Jason all prefer it).
- Multiple own-site pages cannibalizing a keyword can feel harmless or even intuitively better ("isn't it better for me to compete with my own self than competing with another business?" — Marita's question) — but Google actively picks only one page per site to rank, so unmanaged cannibalization has a real, non-obvious ranking cost.
- Current ranking oddities (e.g., Marita's impressions on unrelated queries) may simply be transitional noise from Google's broader shift toward AI-mode-by-default over "the next, like, month or two," rather than a genuine signal of site health one way or the other.
