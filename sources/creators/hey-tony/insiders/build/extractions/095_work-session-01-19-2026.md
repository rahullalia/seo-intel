## Tactics & step-by-step how-to

- **Core weekly content-finding workflow** (recap for newcomers):
  1. Go to Answer the Public. Type in what you sell (Matt demoed "SEO"). No subscription needed.
  2. Go to Search Engines → Questions (or just go straight to Questions).
  3. Scroll to **page two or page three** of results before picking a question — most competitors never click past page one, so later pages are less contested.
  4. Grab a question you can answer (example used: "Which SEO plugin is the best for WordPress?").
  5. Paste the question into ChatGPT with the standard interview prompt (see Tools section) to get it to interview you before you write.
- **Diagnosing a "only homepage is indexed" problem (live audit, Thomas's site)**:
  1. Pull up the site in Semrush, switch to **root domain** view.
  2. Check **Authority Score** and **Referring Domains** (his: 127 referring domains).
  3. Check **Backlinks (all time)** — found 288,000 backlinks (moments later Matt restates the figure as 406,000; treat both as heard on the call, exact number unclear from audio, but the point stands: an extreme, disproportionate count).
  4. Site was also flagged/showing as "dangerous" in the tool.
  5. Identify the single referring site responsible for the bulk of the links (in this case, one of Thomas's own client sites, linking back via a footer credit).
  6. Remove that footer link — a concentrated, disproportionate backlink source from one domain reads as spam/gaming-the-system to Google and can suppress indexing of everything except the homepage.
- **Diagnosing a second site (same owner) that also won't index**: compare the two sites' backlink profiles. If the same backlinks point to both domains (near-identical referring-domain sets), Google may treat them as duplicate/spam and choose to index only one. Fix: strip the shared/duplicate backlinks pointing at whichever site you care less about ranking.
- **Reducing AI image-creation friction (Matt's live example)**: instead of generating every image with AI, generate only the **featured image** with AI (using prior images in the same chat thread as style reference), then fill supporting in-body images with **real screenshots** already sitting in client folders/reports (Semrush screenshots, Looker Studio report screenshots) — deliberately pick screenshots with **recent date ranges visible** (e.g., a December report referenced on a Jan 19 call) to visually prove recency of results.
- **Nick's current (clunky) image pipeline**, shared as the pain point being solved above: keep a saved prompt in ChatGPT with brand/design particulars pre-loaded → ask for 3-5 images (cover + inline) → download as PNG → convert to JPEG → compress via tinyjpg.com → upload → insert into blog. Article itself takes 15-20 min to write; images take 45 min-1.5 hrs.
- **Converting PNG to WebP**: open in Photoshop/Creative Cloud, save/export as WebP at **~70% quality** (Matt: barely any visible quality loss). If no Photoshop, Google "JPEG or PNG to WebP converter" for free online tools.
- **Avoiding repetitive real-photo blog images (Dr. Karen's question)**: upload a couple of your own reference photos into an AI chat (ChatGPT or Gemini) and ask it to generate "[subject] doing [action]" using those reference images for likeness/style. Alternatively, just take more photos on your phone — imperfect, off-the-cuff photography is fine and increasingly preferred over polished graphics.
- **Word-count reconciliation process**: don't trust one tool. Check the on-page count from at least two extensions plus a manual copy-paste into a word counter site, and treat the spread as a directional range, not a precise figure. If a tool's count is wildly higher than what you can visually count on the page, view the page source — look for hidden pop-up text, mobile-only text, or plugin-injected duplicate text.
- **Internal linking on a young site with few posts (Sandy's question)**: don't force internal links that don't exist yet. Publish content first (10-20 posts as a rough bar), link to what you naturally can as you go, and linking opportunities will accumulate on their own as the content library grows.
- **Local SEO layered into broad/national-style content (Jackie's hairdresser example)**: even when a blog post answers a broad question (e.g., "how does hair color work"), add a local CTA sentence with an anchor link inside the post — e.g., "If you're looking for a hairstylist in Denver, [link] give us a call today" — keep NAP (name/address/phone) in the site footer, and link out to your Google Business Profile/reviews from posts.
- **Choosing between two owned websites targeting overlapping audiences (Jessa's case)**: pick ONE site as the primary SEO/content focus; use the other site to link toward it (e.g., "if you'd rather do this yourself, check out our DIY course [link]") instead of publishing similar content on both and competing with your own name/authority across two domains.
- **Differentiating near-duplicate topics into separate posts**: change the angle/audience in the title, not just the wording, to make two posts genuinely different keywords (example: "what to do when my wife has sexual avoidance" vs. "...when my husband has sexual avoidance").
- **Testing AI-answer visibility**: after ranking organically for a topic, cross-post the blog link to LinkedIn and answer related questions on Quora/Reddit (with a link back) as an active experiment to see if it increases odds of being cited/mentioned in AI-generated answers, not just classic organic rankings.
- **Authenticity content for trade businesses (roofing example for Sandy)**: film ~30-second real clips of an actual team member on site — e.g., someone named "Jim" on a roof cracking/bending a shingle to show granule loss and explain why the roof needs replacing, or a short piece testing how long "15-year shingles" actually last — instead of faceless/stock content.

## Rules / dos & don'ts (keep every threshold, number, specific)

- Work on **one website at a time**. Don't split SEO effort across multiple sites simultaneously — you end up half-assing all of them (weaker internal linking, thinner execution across the board).
- If any single external/client site is responsible for **more than 100 backlinks** to your domain, remove your link from that site — concentrated backlink sources from one domain look like manipulation to Google.
- GoHighLevel does **not** support WebP uploads for courses/training video hosting — must be JPEG or PNG. Wix or Squarespace (one of the two, per the discussion) also doesn't support WebP uploads yet.
- Don't chase volume for its own sake — Matt doesn't worry about search volume on ultra-specific questions, even down to an estimated **~10 searches/month**, because that traffic is highly qualified intent.
- Higher-volume keywords are, per Matt's estimate, **"9 or 9.9 times out of 10"** more difficult to rank for than low-volume long-tail questions — even when the tool (Answer the Public) shows no explicit difficulty score.
- Cap publishing cadence at **2 blog posts/week**. Going faster risks getting sloppy or reusing recycled material ("oh, I already have something better").
- Don't fully trust word-count or keyword-density percentages from any single tool (extension vs. extension vs. manual count can differ by hundreds of words on the same page) — use as a rough guide, verify manually if a number looks implausible.
- Tools generally don't account for keyword variants (e.g., "web design" vs. "website design") when calculating keyword density — expect reported counts to be inflated versus true literal usage.
- Running two of your own websites after the same keyword/audience is a losing strategy — you split effort, risk duplicating content, and (per Matt) Google won't rank the same known person/entity twice for one query regardless of which domain it's on.
- A keyword being objectively "difficult" (e.g., "web hosting") is not, by itself, a reason a whole site fails to index beyond the homepage — don't misdiagnose keyword difficulty when the real blocker is a spam-looking backlink profile.

## Tools, prompts & resources

- **Answer the Public** — free tier used to find real user questions; type in what you sell → Search Engines → Questions (or Questions directly); dig to page 2-3 for less-contested questions.
- **ChatGPT interview prompt** (as dictated on the call, cleaned of stutter): *"I'm writing a blog post answering this question: [QUESTION]. Ask me any questions you'd need to know the answer to in order to write a helpful blog post using my expertise, experience, case studies, opinions. Keep it limited to 10 questions."*
- **AI featured-image prompt** (same pattern, cleaned): *"Using the images as a style inspiration, create me a featured image for this post. We don't need the title of the blog post on it. It should be something to do with [topic]."* — run in the **same ongoing chat thread** as prior image generations so the model has style continuity.
- **Semrush** — used for: root-domain backlink audits (Authority Score, Referring Domains, Backlinks all-time), plus pulling screenshots (impressions, other reports) to embed directly as blog images.
- **Looker Studio** — pulled client-report screenshots (with visible recent date ranges) for use as blog body images.
- **tinyjpg.com** — used to compress JPEGs after downloading AI-generated PNGs.
- **Photoshop / Adobe Creative Cloud** — used to export images as WebP at ~70% quality.
- Generic fallback: Google "JPEG or PNG to WebP converter" for free online conversion tools if you don't have Photoshop.
- **Ubersuggest** — named as a keyword-research cross-check tool to validate actual search volume/variations of a keyword ChatGPT suggests (ChatGPT itself has no access to Semrush/Ubersuggest data, so its keyword guesses aren't reliable for volume).
- **Word counter site** (referenced loosely as "word counter dot net" — Matt himself wasn't fully sure of the name) — used to manually verify true word count by copy-pasting all page text.
- **GoHighLevel ("High Level")** — used by Matt for hosting courses/training videos; doesn't accept WebP.
- **Gemini** — recommended alongside ChatGPT as "really, really good" for generating varied images from reference photos, to avoid repetitive real-photo blogs.
- **Page audit extension** — Matt's team's own browser extension for on-page word count; compared against...
- **Detailed SEO** — third-party browser extension, also used for word count; discrepancies between it, the page-audit extension, and manual word-counter checks (2000 vs. 1900 vs. 2400 words) were the live example discussed.
- **Google Business Profile** — recommended link target from blog posts (reviews) as a local-SEO trust signal.
- **LinkedIn, Quora, Reddit** — channels for redistributing blog content / answering questions with a link back, as part of the AI-citation visibility experiment.

## Tradeoffs & nuance

- AI image generation is far cheaper/faster than custom design work (Matt estimates $100-200+ per designed image otherwise), but it doesn't eliminate friction — format conversion, compression, and upload steps remain manual and are the actual bottleneck members complain about (Nick: 45 min-1.5 hrs on images vs. 15-20 min writing).
- Real/authentic photos and short phone-shot video are increasingly favored over polished AI or stock graphics — framed as a 2026 shift toward authenticity in marketing generally, not just SEO, even though it's a change from some members' (and Matt's own) older habits.
- Ranking organically and being cited in AI-generated answers are treated as two separate outcomes: Matt notes ranking #2 for a piece didn't get him mentioned in AI results, so social distribution (LinkedIn/Quora/Reddit) is being tested as a possible lever for AI visibility — explicitly framed as an active experiment, not a proven fix.
- Traffic from outside a business's service area still has value for a local business: it can't convert directly, but it costs nothing extra (hosting doesn't scale with visits in any meaningful way here) and builds engagement/trust signals with Google — so broad/national-style content is still worth publishing even for a local-only business, as long as local CTAs and NAP/Google Business Profile signals are layered in.
- Owning two sites/brands to double up on rankings for the same audience is tempting ("ranking #1 and #2 would be fantastic") but Matt frames it as usually backfiring — better to pick one primary and cross-link from the secondary.
- Keyword difficulty and indexing failure are separate problems that get conflated: Thomas assumed a hard keyword explained his lack of rankings, but the actual blocker (spam-looking backlink profile) had nothing to do with keyword choice — worth checking backlinks before reworking keyword strategy.
- Word-count and keyword-density tool numbers shouldn't be treated as hard rules — divergence between tools stems from real technical causes (mobile vs. desktop rendering, hidden pop-up text, plugin-injected text, failure to account for keyword variants), so manual judgment and spot-checking beat chasing an exact percentage or count.
