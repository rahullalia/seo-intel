## Tactics & step-by-step how-to

- **Cold-read test (the throughline of this whole call)**: look at your own money page as a total stranger would — does the H1 and the very top of the page tell you exactly what's being sold/who it's for, with zero scrolling? Matt ran this live on three different pages (Aaron's Yellowstone tour page, Samantha's workbook collection, Jorg's tennis-shirt collection) and all three failed it — vague H1s and buried offers were the #1 recurring problem of the call.
- **Diagnosing a non-ranking page, step by step** (demonstrated on Aaron's "Total Yellowstone, three days" tour page):
  1. Check whether the H1 actually communicates the offer (his didn't — "Total Yellowstone, three days" doesn't say tour, camping, hiking, etc., and is confusable with the *Yellowstone* TV show).
  2. Run the exact H1 phrase through a keyword tool to see if it has any real volume before assuming it's a good target.
  3. Check for a branded variant you may already rank for (Matt assumed Aaron's site already ranks for "Teton excursions, Yellowstone tours" as a branded term).
  4. Pull keyword variants people actually search and place the relevant ones into H2s/body copy exactly where the page already covers that content (e.g., since day one of the tour starts in West Yellowstone, use an H2 like "Day one: stay in West Yellowstone" rather than inventing new sections).
  5. Add an FAQ block addressing real customer expectations/objections (e.g., "Am I guaranteed to see a wolf during a wolf tour in Yellowstone?" → answer honestly: no, wildlife can't be guaranteed).
- **Auditing "why isn't this indexing"**: first check the publish date — a page only a few days old may simply need more time, not a fix (applied to Tatiana's "best video production company in New York" page, posted 4 days before the call).
- **Click-depth test for buried pages**: starting from the homepage, manually click through to the target page and count clicks. Footer-only links count as a path but are weighted lower than header/on-page links — if the only route is 3–4+ clicks deep through a footer link, that's a plausible reason a page isn't indexing/ranking well.
- **Diagnosing "crawled but not indexed" in GSC**: Search Console → Pages report → toggle to "Not indexed" → review the exclusion reasons. Ignore reasons like "not found with redirect," "duplicate without user-selected canonical," "blocked," or paginated/feed/author pages you don't care about indexing. Focus specifically on **"Crawled – currently not indexed"** as the reason worth investigating.
- **Checking for keyword cannibalization**: Google the exact target phrase and see if the client's own other pages are already ranking/showing for near-identical phrasing (found on Tatiana's site: a new page targeting "best video production company in New York" overlapped heavily with existing pages "professional commercial video production in New York" and "commercial video production in New York").
- **Checking search-intent match**: Google the target keyword and look at what's actually ranking. If the SERP is dominated by list/directory sites (e.g., Clutch, for "best video production company in New York"), a single-company "we are the best" page may not match the intent Google expects for that query.
- **Reformatting an evergreen page mistakenly published as a blog post**: if the page is meant to be a permanent, important page (not a dated post), rebuild it as an actual site page (e.g., a dropdown under the relevant service nav) instead of a blog post, and have other blog posts link into it rather than the reverse.
- **Fixing a collection/category page that buries the product** (done live on both Samantha's workbook collection and Jorg's tennis-shirt collection):
  1. Move informational/marketing copy below the product grid — in Shopify this content can be relocated down near the footer.
  2. Put products/images front and center on load, especially for mobile.
  3. Add real product photography (e.g., a person wearing the shirt) plus descriptive alt text on every image — alt text can also get picked up by search.
  4. Shrink oversized branding — a logo eating a third of the screen adds no value.
  5. Make sure the URL slug and the H1 target the same keyword/intent (Jorg's URL was "polo-shirts" while the H1 said "tennis shirts" — different search intents even though the products look similar).
- **Building category structure instead of one broad collection page**: create individual pages per subject × grade (or per product variant) instead of forcing one page to rank for 5–10 unrelated keywords at once (Samantha's collection page tried to cover math, reading, coding, writing, and financial literacy all at once). Let keyword-research volume decide which specific pages are actually worth building.
- **Vocabulary/naming test**: check whether the term you use for a product is the term buyers actually search — swap wording where the data disagrees with internal terminology (Samantha's team used "eBook," but "PDF" is what people search). Also swap industry jargon for parent/customer language — e.g. "four year olds" instead of "Grade 1," "essentials" instead of "basics."
- **Long-tail/question-based keyword method** (for niches that have exhausted head-term keywords, e.g. Tara's dumpster/bin-rental clients): use Answer the Public or the Insiders' "Reddit tool" to surface full-sentence questions people actually type, then use the literal question as the blog title/H1 and answer it thoroughly in the content — don't force keyword-first phrasing into the H1. Example given: for a bin-rental client, "Can I put a fridge in a dumpster?" → answer no, explain the disposal fees/environmental rules, link to proper-disposal guidance, and link back to the company's own service.
- **Reddit tactic — third-party posting**: create a personal (real-name, not brand) Reddit account and participate genuinely in relevant subreddits without self-promoting (most subreddits, and the SEO subreddit especially, flag your own links as spam). Get an actual third party — friend, family member, real past customer — to post the honest recommendation/link instead of posting it yourself.
- **Reddit tactic — "give first, reference later"**: post a genuinely valuable free asset (Aaron's idea: give away the full 3-day Yellowstone itinerary as a guide) to earn organic upvotes/karma, then return to that thread or account later and reference the business once it has credibility — framed as a "reverse" of Matt's usual Reddit-recommendation trick.
- **Reddit account setup**: sign up on desktop rather than mobile to be able to pick a custom username at signup (a community member's experience: mobile signup assigned a generated username with no easy way to change it; took 2–3 attempts to get it right).

## Rules / dos & don'ts

- Every page should target **one specific keyword** — repeatedly stated as the core rule (Samantha's collection page was fighting for math, reading, coding, writing, and financial literacy simultaneously on one page).
- Let keyword research dictate which pages you build, never the reverse — "we can create a million different pages, but if nobody's searching them ... we're just doing work for no reason."
- A page not ranking doesn't automatically mean it's failing the business — if it converts through another channel, it's still doing its job. Aaron's ~$5,800/group 3-day Yellowstone tour (also described as "$5,000, $6,000 for the group") sells almost entirely by phone after a visitor views the page, not through the page directly.
- Blog posts on a site with some authority can rank on page 1 with **little to no backlinks** — demonstrated with three zero-backlink #1-rankers on Samantha's exact keywords: Indigo, Scholastic, and "Russo Book," all ranking with zero links.
- Don't bury the product/CTA under a wall of marketing copy on collection/category pages — flagged as hurting both conversion and (per Jorg's own data) click-through once the page does get impressions.
- URL slug and H1/target keyword must match the same search intent — don't let a URL (e.g. "polo-shirts") diverge from what the H1 is optimized for (e.g. "tennis shirts").
- Important, evergreen pages should not be published as ordinary blog posts — they get absorbed into the "recent posts" feed and lose prominence; give them real nav placement instead.
- Footer links carry less ranking/crawl weight than header or in-content links, but a category page should still carry plenty of internal links out to individual product pages (every product listed on a collection page functions as one internal link).
- Reddit self-promotion breaks most subreddits' norms and risks a spam flag — every subreddit sets its own rules, and the SEO subreddit specifically was called out as strict about it.
- Paying for Reddit upvotes/posts via Fiverr was mentioned only as something people do — Matt explicitly said he hasn't tested it and isn't advising it.
- Indexing timing varies enormously and "depends on every single website": as fast as within an hour; Matt's own team has seen posts indexed, on page 1, and recommended by AI within 2–3 hours; other client sites with insufficient authority take up to ~2 months with no movement. (A community member separately noted it can range "from one hour to weeks... could also be months.")
- Keyword volumes cited live (US/Canada, exact tool not named on-screen): "Total Yellowstone" = 0 volume (confusable with the *Yellowstone* TV show — surfaces "Yellowstone Season 5, total episodes" instead); "Canada" (isolated) ≈ 30/mo; "math workbooks" ≈ 140/mo (page wasn't ranking for it); "grade 10 math workbook" ≈ 20/mo, flagged as high buying intent — though Samantha's business only sells pre-K through grade 4, so that specific example wasn't actually usable for her; "educational ebooks" = 0/mo; "ebooks for kids" ≈ 10/mo (variants: free ebooks, ABC's ebooks, counting ebooks, counting/opposites ebooks for kids). Jorg's "tennis shirts" ≈ 3,600/mo, currently ranking position 34.

## Tradeoffs & nuance

- A real differentiator can still lose to keyword-volume data: Jorg suggested "private" and "custom" as keywords for Aaron's tours since they're meaningfully different from a "unified for everyone" group excursion — Matt agreed it's a true distinction but held firm that keyword research, not the differentiator itself, should dictate the primary target phrase (the nuance can still live in the copy/FAQ).
- Ranking isn't the only success metric: Aaron's page converts mainly by phone after a visitor views it, so "not ranking" doesn't automatically mean the page is failing commercially — it's a candidate for SEO improvement, not proof of a broken asset.
- New-page patience vs. a real problem look identical at first: a page only 4 days old (Tatiana's video-production page) may just need more time to index, but the same "not indexed" symptom can also mean a genuine structural issue (wrong content format, buried placement, cannibalization) — the call repeatedly stressed diagnosing which one you're actually looking at before "fixing" anything.
- Reddit self-promotion is a genuine tension: posting your own link risks a spam flag (enforcement varies by subreddit, with the SEO subreddit called out as especially strict), so the workable paths are third-party/authentic posting, "give value first, reference later," or the unverified, not-endorsed option of paying for engagement on Fiverr.
- Footer links still count toward "lots of internal links" on a category page, but they're weighted below header/in-content links — useful for relocating supporting copy out of the way (as recommended for both Samantha's and Jorg's collection pages) without deleting it, but not a substitute for a well-linked primary navigation path.
- Terminology choice can pit internal/brand clarity against actual search behavior: Samantha's team used "eBook" partly to keep customers from thinking they'd bought a physical book, but real search volume favored "PDF" — and even the partial fix (adding "PDF" back into some listings) didn't fully resolve customers emailing to ask "where's my book" after downloading a digital file, showing the SEO-optimal term and the customer-clarity term can genuinely conflict.
