# 105 — WDIR: Why Doesn't It Rank? (26.12.2025 Replay)

## Tactics & step-by-step how-to

- **Matt's live "why doesn't it rank" diagnostic sequence** (run twice this call — on Anthony's compactor page and implicitly on Nick's site) — synthesized from his repeated actions:
  1. Pull the target keyword into the keyword tool, switch location to the correct country/region (he caught himself set to Canada instead of US), and check volume + keyword difficulty %.
  2. Check who currently ranks in the top 3 for that keyword and open their pages.
  3. Determine **search intent by content type**: is the top of the SERP full of service pages, blog posts, or job postings? Your page's content type must match what's already ranking, not just the keyword.
  4. Compare word count directly against the top-ranking page (not against "what feels thorough").
  5. Compare heading structure: count H1 tags (should be exactly one), check H2s actually support the H1's topic.
  6. Check the URL and meta description for the target keyword — keyword should sit toward the **front** of the meta description; URL should contain the keyword/location, not a vague truncated version.
  7. Compare images/visual clarity and overall page cleanliness against the top 3 — note if the ranking pages are simpler/cleaner and the client's page is visually noisier.
  8. Only after all of that, decide what to change — and never touch a page that is *already* ranking for its target term, even if it's "wrong" by these rules.
- **Keyword cannibalization check:** if a blog post and a service page both seem to target the same term, Google the term yourself and see whether the SERP is dominated by service pages or blog posts — write/keep the content type that matches, don't run both against each other.
- **Fixing a non-ranking page's URL:** if the current URL doesn't contain the keyword (e.g. `.../compactors` instead of `.../compactor-rentals-new-jersey`), and the page has no inbound backlinks, it's safe to change the URL and 301 redirect the old one.
- **Diagnosing duplicate H1/H2 tags on page builders (High Level, Wix, Squarespace):** open the page, look for a "desktop version" section and a separately-hidden "mobile version" section carrying the same heading text — this is caused by builders encouraging you to duplicate a section and hide one copy per breakpoint instead of building one truly responsive section. Confirm by resizing the browser/using the builder's device toggle and watching whether a second, differently-highlighted instance of the same tag appears.
- **Fix for the above:** there is no in-builder toggle fix — you have to rebuild the section as one truly responsive block (delete the duplicate, keep one, restyle for both breakpoints) rather than duplicating and hiding.
- **Testing heading hierarchy without breaking visual design:** you can make an H1 visually *smaller* than an H2 via custom CSS/font-size if you want the top tag to carry SEO weight without dominating the layout (Matt does this experimentally on his own web-design page).
- **GoHighLevel sitemap fix:** GHL sitemaps do not auto-update when you publish a new blog post. You must manually go regenerate the sitemap inside GHL, then go back into Google Search Console and resubmit/re-validate it.
- **Blog roll / blog index page structure** (asked by an unnamed attendee, answered using Matt's own blog page as the live example):
  1. Do **not** use a "filter" widget that appends a `?taxonomy=category-term-35`-style query string to the URL — Google doesn't treat this as a real, nameable page, and it doesn't visibly say the category name.
  2. Instead, place your 5–10 topic/category names as plain links at the very top of the blog page, each linking to its own real category archive page (e.g., a dedicated "SEO" category page, a dedicated "Website" category page).
  3. Below that, just show the full chronological list of blog previews (newest first) — don't repeat the category links again partway down the page ("don't over-design... don't try to do anything too crazy").
  4. Cap the chronological list at roughly the **most recent 50 posts** (Matt's own site currently shows ~50–100, but he'd tell this attendee to keep it to 50).
  5. Set this structure up immediately, even with a single published post — don't wait until you hit some post-count threshold.
- **Diagnosing "is my page truly responsive":** resize the browser window / switch device view live and watch whether content reflows smoothly (text shrinking/moving/reformatting) versus getting cut off or requiring horizontal scroll. A page can pass a "no duplicate H1" audit and still fail this responsiveness check — they are separate problems.
- **Discerning keyword intent when the SERP looks like the wrong audience** (Nick's voiceover-actor case: keywords pulling job-seeker content instead of hiring-client content): Google the keyword yourself, or run a SERP analysis in SEMrush, and read what content type/audience actually ranks. If the results are dominated by job postings, that keyword isn't a buyer-intent term for a service business — described as inherently manual, trial-and-error work.
- **Expanding a regional service business into new states:** don't decide the naming/domain question in the abstract — look at how an established, larger multi-state competitor in the same space structures their site (one domain vs. many), and default to copying whichever pattern is clearly working; test with a single new location page first to see if it drives traffic before committing to a new domain.
- **New/temporary business location:** create a separate Google Business Profile for it directly — go to Google, search "create Google Business profile," and follow the verification prompts.
- **Rewriting vague service-page headlines/subheads:** replace proprietary/branded product names in your main H1/value prop with plain, literally-searched language (Matt's own homepage pattern used as the model: lead H1 = "Level up your digital marketing," followed by a specific one-line description of what you actually do, then social proof/logos).
- **Tracking internal link equity to individual pages** (suggested addition to Marla's Notion system): add a column for how many other pages link to a given page — especially useful for "gateway"/navigational pages whose main job is routing traffic into filtered or dynamic sub-pages.

## Rules / dos & don'ts (keep every threshold, number, specific)

- **One H1 tag per page. Always.** Two H1s (or duplicated H1/H2 pairs from mobile/desktop duplication) confuses what the page is about to Google.
- **H2 tags must directly support/reinforce the H1's specific topic** — they are subtopics of the main topic, not generic value-prop lines. Matt ran through Nick's actual H2s against his H1 ("video marketing for small business") and rejected nearly all of them for not supporting it: "give me one hour and you get an entire month of authority content," "attention consultants," "you're wasting 40 hours a month on content nobody sees," "gut punchline," "what if one hour could create a month of high converting content" — none reinforce the stated H1 topic.
- **Match content type to search intent** — never publish a blog post for a keyword where the SERP is all service pages, and vice versa.
- **Don't keyword/service-stuff a single URL, title, or meta field** — Anthony's client had "trash compactors NJ / buy, rent or lease / commercial waste solution" all crammed together; correct approach is one specific keyword per field ("commercial trash compactors" — not just "compactors" or "commercial waste solution").
- **Meta description: put the target keyword toward the front**, not buried mid-sentence.
- **Only redirect/change a URL if it has no existing backlinks** — if backlinks point to the old URL, don't casually change it.
- **Word count: match the top-ranking competitor, don't maximize it.** Anthony's client page ran ~2,800 words versus the #1-ranking competitor's 823 — flagged as roughly 2,000 words of excess that buries the actual product info.
- **Blog categories: keep to fewer than 10** — Matt's own site currently exceeds this and he flags it as a problem on his own property ("we shouldn't have this... these categories don't help").
- **Never use a `?`-query-string category filter as your primary category navigation** on a blog roll page — it isn't a real, Google-legible page.
- **Leave a page alone if it's already ranking or converting, even if it breaks every rule above.** Direct rule, stated explicitly: "if Nick told me... this page ranks really well, I get a lot of business from this page... I'm going to say don't touch that page, don't try to fix it... I really don't care why they're ranking it." Applies to duplicate H1s, keyword stuffing, non-standard structure — anything.
- **Don't split one multi-service business across separate subdomains per service line** — flagged as unnecessary because every individual page can independently rank for its own keyword; no technical reason requires the split.
- **A "no duplicate headings" audit-tool pass does not mean a page has no problems** — Matt's tool only flags multiple H1 tags (or duplicate H2s if they're identical); it will not flag multiple *different* H2 tags, and it says nothing about responsiveness. Tal's site passed the heading check but still failed on non-responsive mobile/desktop layout.
- **Google has done mobile-first indexing since ~2016** — if content is visually cut off or non-reflowing on mobile, that's a direct negative regardless of desktop quality.
- **GoHighLevel does not yet support schema markup on individual blog posts** (works fine on any other page type) — Matt confirms GHL is actively working on this.
- **PR/press-release backlink services charging $40–60/post: no blanket verdict.** Matt hasn't personally tested them for clients; some SEOs he knows swear by them. His only rule: judge by results, not by reputation either way.
- **New-state/domain expansion decision: follow whatever's already proven to work for bigger competitors** in the same space rather than reasoning it out from scratch.
- **When positioning copy is vague, cut the branded jargon** (e.g., "Marketing Mountain," "Lead Forge," "Intellisites," "Radio VIP Day," "The Builder's Code") from primary headlines/value props — nobody searches those terms, and a first-time visitor can't tell what the business does. Exception: fine to use as internal product names *within* a page that already explains itself, or on an ad-driven landing page where the ad copy already set context.

## Tradeoffs & nuance

- **A technically "broken" page should stay broken if it's already working.** Duplicate H1s, keyword stuffing, non-standard structure — none of it matters if the page ranks and converts. Fixing it "for correctness" risks breaking what's actually driving business.
- **Mobile/desktop section duplication is a builder-encouraged shortcut** (High Level, Wix, and Squarespace all do it), not automatically fatal — but it's also not fixable from inside the builder; it requires rebuilding the section as genuinely responsive.
- **More words is not inherently better or worse** — the real signal is whether your length matches what's already ranking. Anthony's client's extra ~2,000 words weren't flagged as an automatic ranking penalty so much as a user-experience and clarity problem.
- **A clean result from an SEO audit tool is not proof of a healthy page.** Tal's site had zero duplicate headings and still had a serious responsiveness problem the tool can't see — the two checks are independent axes.
- **PR/press-release backlinks: no house position.** Matt hasn't tested them himself and won't say they're good or bad — his stance is purely outcome-based ("are you getting results?").
- **Elaborate SEO tracking systems (Marla's Notion build) are "SEO adjacent," not SEO.** Time spent designing the tracking framework is explicitly distinguished from time spent doing keyword research, writing content, or checking rankings — useful for the person if it keeps them moving, but not a rankings lever itself.
- **New-market domain/naming decisions have no universal rule** — the guidance both to Lux (multi-state expansion) and generally is to copy whatever's already proven for bigger players in the same space, testing with a single new page before committing to new domains.
- **Matt applies his own rules unevenly to his own site and says so on-camera** — his blog exceeds his stated "fewer than 10 categories" guidance and has a stray junk category from a team member, cited candidly while teaching the same rule to someone else.
