## Tactics & step-by-step how-to

- **The 30-day SEO plan (core deliverable of the day)** — 4 weeks, ~1 hour/week, same 4-task loop repeated each week:
  1. Publish 1 blog post that links to 3 other blog posts + 1 money page.
  2. Fix 5 serious "red" issues flagged by audit.heytony.ca.
  3. Fix that week's money page: title tag + meta description (treated as two separate sub-tasks so progress feels faster, even though conceptually "one task").
  4. Add 3 internal links from a blog post to that week's money page.
  - Repeat with a **different** money page and a **different** blog topic each week — 4 total money pages, 4 total blog posts, 20 total red issues fixed over 30 days.
  - Dates pre-filled by Matt: **March 16, March 23, March 30, April 6** (Mondays) — put in calendar, block the hour, show up.
- **Sourcing the 4 blog topics**: go to app.rwideas.com (free, available "until tonight," then folds into Insiders), type in what you sell, copy 4 of the customer questions it returns. Free fallback: answerthepublic.com. If a topic is already cached in the tool from earlier in the week, results appear instantly; otherwise there's a processing queue.
- **Sourcing the 4 money pages**: go to your own site's services/products section, grab 4 distinct service/money pages (e.g., Matt used Hey Tony's own "services" page as the demo), paste each into its own week slot.
- **Template mechanics**: the Google Sheet has two extra tabs — "Blog Posts" and "Money Pages" — paste the 4 questions and 4 URLs there; prompts to run the blog-writing / title-tag / meta-description tasks live in columns to the right of each week's row. Copy those prompts into your own doc/notes now — Matt is deleting the source pages after tonight.
- **Post-Rank-Week check**: open Google (incognito is fine) and search the exact question you answered in Monday's blog post; check whether you rank #1, page 1, or aren't indexed yet. If not indexed: sign up for Google Search Console and submit your sitemap to speed up discovery.
- **Money-page fix order (Matt's own priority stack when working a site)**: 1) title tag, 2) meta description, 3) H1 — keyword in all three — then wait 1–2 weeks to see if rankings move before touching on-page content further.
- **Checking whether a fix "worked"**: don't rely solely on incognito Google checks; refresh results inside a rank tracker (SEMrush/Ubersuggest) since those tools pull from a fresh crawl.
- **Handling an old page that outranks its replacement** (Carol's headshot-photographer case): don't delete either page. Re-add the old/combined page back into the site navigation, leave the newer split-out pages as they are, and make only small/minimal edits (e.g., a CTA tweak) to the page that's already ranking well.
- **Deciding what to fix on an old blog (150+ posts, photographer since 2014)**: don't attempt to rehabilitate all of them. Only chase "low-hanging fruit" — pages/posts already getting impressions or clicks but stuck on page 2 or lower on page 1. For posts that are de-indexed and getting no meaningful impressions: delete and either 404 or 301-redirect them (redirect if there's a relevant page to send that traffic to).
- **Scaling location pages for a multi-location/multi-division client** (Josh, print/signage, hundreds of locations): run keyword research across the entire location list first, rank opportunities by search volume vs. ease of ranking, then execute in priority order — Matt's agency paces even large clients at roughly 2 new pages/week rather than trying to do everything simultaneously.
- **Deciding "local vs. broader" for a growing service business** (Sarah, nanny agency, Cincinnati ~2M metro): check actual search behavior — people search "nannies in [city]," not "nannies in [state]." For a metro of that size, build suburb-level location pages within it before expanding to new cities; only expand geography once the existing metro's demand is closer to tapped out.
- **Using WordPress "project"/case-study pages that won't rank on their own** (landscaping example): build the actual location or service page as the thing meant to rank (talk about the local area — trees, landmarks, etc.) and link out to the relevant completed-project/case-study pages from within it. The project pages support the ranking page; they aren't expected to rank themselves.
- **Diagnosing a mismatched/unclear niche keyword** (Lori B, holiday decorating co.): Google broad candidate terms (e.g., "home decorator") and look at what category of business actually shows up. If the results are the wrong type of business (exterior lighting companies instead of interior holiday designers), that keyword is miscategorized for your niche — keep testing variations until the results show businesses that actually do what you do. Also usable: ask ChatGPT directly "who are my local competitors, this is what I do."
- **Reading a page audit's keyword-density flag** (Dara, Nigerian wedding planner): don't trust the tool's density verdict alone. Go look at what your actual ranking competitors do — count how many times they use the target keyword on their page — and match that usage rate rather than the tool's raw "high/low" density label.
- **Readability fixes**: keep paragraphs to roughly 2–3 sentences; vary sentence length rather than writing exclusively long sentences (this is what drives a Flesch/ease-of-reading score).
- **Heading structure fix**: exactly one H1 per page. For H2 count, look at how many H2s your top-ranking competitors use and use one more than them.
- **Getting real reviews without gaming**: hand the customer a business card with a QR code after service; offer something like 10% off their next service for leaving a review (don't specify "5-star" — just ask for "a review"). If offering nothing gets no reviews, that's useful internal data — adjust the incentive, but keep review volume arriving gradually/consistently rather than in a burst.
- **Audit-tool troubleshooting flow** (if audit.heytony.ca won't scan your site): (1) try a recrawl, (2) try re-adding your website, (3) try adding a different website (a friend's, or one you own) — if that one crawls fine and yours doesn't, the problem is specific to your site. Only after trying these, email support@heytony.ca (response may lag to Monday if it's near end-of-day).

## Rules / dos & don'ts (keep every threshold, number, specific)

- 30-day plan = **1 hour per week**, 4 weeks. "This is not a sprint, it is a marathon" — don't try to compress it into one sitting.
- "I'm not trying to get you guys to do everything. I'm trying to get you guys to make progress" — deliberately capped scope (5 red issues, not all of them; 4 blog posts, not more) to avoid overwhelm.
- **Stop reciprocal link exchanges between friends'/accountability-group sites immediately** ("private blog network") — Matt: "stop doing that right away." There's a thin technical line some advanced SEOs walk with this, but for most people it crosses into something Google penalizes.
- **Stop "review trains"** (community groups of 4-5 businesses trading review requests monthly) — reads as gaming the system to Google; can get those reviews taken down and can drag down other, legitimate reviews on the same profile as collateral.
- Never explicitly ask for a "**5-star** review" — just ask for "a review."
- Reviews arriving in a sudden cluster (e.g., 5 within 20 minutes) is a red flag to Google, especially if not all reviewers are local — Google can see reviewer IP/location and weights accordingly.
- Don't use a fake "moved from elsewhere" story to mask reviewer location for review-train participants — location authenticity matters; general rule: "if it feels sneaky, don't do it."
- Don't treat a page-audit tool's overall "site health %" as a real ranking-quality score — Matt: "it's just for grabbing on-page data... it's not meant to be used" as an overall grade (he flagged pageaudit.com's scoring/metrics need adjustment on his end).
- Over-optimization is real and penalized: a web-hosting company with thousands of backlinks was flagged as "over-optimized" in places — "more is obviously too much" past a point.
- Exactly **1 H1** per page; H2 count should be "one more than" what competitors use.
- Niche keyword density: match competitor usage rate rather than trusting an absolute density number (example threshold discussed: 10 uses of "wedding planner in Toronto" flagged as likely too many for a niche term; 2–3 mentions suggested as more appropriate).
- Paragraphs: **2–3 sentences**, mix short and long sentences.
- **Don't use Google Trends to judge whether a keyword has enough search volume** — Matt explicitly: "no, don't use that." Use a real keyword tool (he suggested a SEMrush 7-day free trial) instead.
- Keyword/competitive research tool pricing cited: **Ubersuggest ≈ $30–40/month** (cheapest); **SEMrush ≈ $130–140/month**. SEMrush and Ahrefs are the most accurate for volume/difficulty, though Matt notes those specific numbers are "made up" (estimated) on every platform to some degree — the "who's actually ranking for what" data is accurate across tools.
- Cached-data gotcha: Ubersuggest/SEMrush/Ahrefs-style tools show cached data (can be weeks or years stale) — click "update"/refresh to force current numbers.
- Agency's own cadence standard: **2 new local/blog pages per week** even for large multi-location clients — framed as "sniper rifle" precision over "shotgun" volume.
- Don't generalize page/site copy to a whole state/region if search behavior is hyper-local (e.g., "nannies in Cincinnati" vs. "nannies in Ohio") — write to the specific city/metro people actually search.

## Tradeoffs & nuance

- **Hire an expert vs. DIY**: fine to hire if you feel you can't be efficient alone — but "don't stop learning" either way. Matt's agency's best clients are the ones educated enough to understand *why* a tactic is used; uneducated clients ("why are you writing blog posts?") take more agency time to manage than the SEO work itself takes. An educated client can also audit a hired agency's actual output (internal linking present? heading tags structured? content copy-pasted from ChatGPT?) instead of trusting claims at face value.
- **Pacing vs. capability**: Josh's org could technically execute far more than 2 pages/week given its scale, but Matt's agency intentionally throttles to that pace across all clients — a deliberate choice to protect execution quality/consistency, not a hard SEO ceiling.
- **Local Service Ads vs. Google Ads**: no confident, data-backed answer given — explicitly framed as "my gut," with the caveat that Matt doesn't know the specific business/territory well enough to be definitive; positioned as something to A/B test rather than switch on faith.
- **Geographic expansion timing**: depends on the size of the existing addressable market — a ~2M metro (Cincinnati) should be mined further locally (suburb pages) before spending effort on new cities; a much smaller metro would presumably need to expand sooner, though Matt didn't state that threshold explicitly.
- **Keyword-to-category mismatch** requires empirical, iterative testing (Googling variations and eyeballing what type of business shows up) rather than assuming the obviously-worded term is the correct one to target.
- **Reviews**: incentivizing them is fine; it's the specific ask ("5-star" vs. "a review") and the arrival pattern (steady trickle vs. sudden cluster) that separates acceptable practice from something Google penalizes.
- **SEO vs. social media** (Ellen's dilemma): Matt frames SEO as the more sustainable, less "constant/nonstop" channel long-term, but with a much slower, quieter feedback loop (no likes/comments/DMs — just waiting, then results) versus social media's instant feedback. His actual answer was noncommittal — "either direction can be good as long as you stick with it and keep trying to improve" — not a recommendation to drop one for the other.
- **WordPress case-study/"project" pages**: valuable as internal-linking support content, but shouldn't be expected to rank on their own — the location/service page is the intended ranking target, and project pages reinforce rather than replace it.
- **Old high-performing pages after a redesign**: even when current best practice argues for splitting content into separate pages (per-service), an already-ranking combined page should be preserved and only lightly touched — protecting an existing win outweighs applying the "correct" structural pattern.
- **The 30-day plan's slow pace (1 hr/week) is itself a deliberate tradeoff** — members could likely finish faster, but pacing is designed to protect follow-through and prevent the overwhelm that causes people to quit by week two.
