# WDIR Replay 27.02.2026 — Knowledge Digest

## Tactics & step-by-step how-to
- **Reverse-engineering why a page won't rank (Melissa's "small business website design" page, UK)**: pull the exact-URL keyword report (page authority, referring domains), then compare against the top 3 ranking competitors on: exact-match keyword count vs. variation count, total word count, on-page structure/formatting, presence of stats/proof, CTA placement, FAQs, and internal linking. Matt's framing: "they're earning those spots because Google — they've done something that Google likes," so reverse-engineer the pattern rather than guess.
- Check keyword usage two ways: exact-match count AND total variation count (e.g. "small business" + "small businesses" combined) — a page can look under-optimized on exact-match while still being heavily optimized on variations.
- Find a competitor's site structure by pulling their XML sitemap (via `robots.txt` → sitemap index) — reveals blog post count, whether they run separate location/landing-page sitemaps, and (via post dates) how long they've been building topical authority.
- Check the oldest indexed blog post's publish date as a topical-authority proxy — a competitor with a 2016 post has years of accumulated signal a newer site can't shortcut.
- To find easy local wins: search the literal keyword phrase, open the ranking pages, and check word count/structure against your own page before deciding to rewrite anything.
- **Decision framework for a page that isn't ranking at all** (Melissa's case, given as literal next steps): (1) leave it as-is, (2) rewrite/restructure it now — risk: could lose an existing "near me" ranking — or (3) write more blog posts and internal-link them back to the page, then reassess after a set trial. Matt's recommended sequencing: do (3) first; only "take it apart" (rewrite) if there's no movement after **~4 weeks / a month**.
- To expand beyond a single well-ranked local town keyword (Melissa ranks #1 for "website design in Ludlow"), build individual location pages for each nearby town/city rather than trying to broaden the existing page.
- **Local SEO footer question (Varuna's client, doesn't take walk-in clients)**: if a client insists on a "clean" footer with no nav/NAP, still get location/contact info on the site — build a dedicated `/locations` page linking out to one sub-page per location (client has 4, one confirmed as San Diego), each sub-page written specifically for that location; at minimum link Contact Us from the footer.
- **Keyword-difficulty ladder**: when the obvious head term is too competitive (Varuna's "SEO agency in New Jersey"), go after a real variation people actually search (e.g. "SEO consultant in New Jersey") instead of abandoning the topic — same logic applied later to "how to file a mechanics lien" vs. "file a mechanics lien."
- **Checking if orphaned pages are indexed** (Jason's knowledge base, non-WordPress platform): check Google Search Console coverage directly; cross-check whether the pages are actually linked from the main site and whether they require login — Jason's were a mix of indexed/non-indexed specifically because they weren't linked site-wide and sat behind a login wall.
- **Deciding rewrite vs. leave-alone for existing content**: before committing to a rewrite, run the actual target phrase through keyword research first and look at what the SERP is really returning — Jason's "what blog software can I use" looks like a real customer question but the SERP intent is generic blogging platforms (Blogger/Tumblr), unrelated to his business, so it's not worth optimizing even though it "answers a real question."
- Given a large, hard-to-rewrite knowledge base: leave it alone as a support resource, and redirect effort into new blog posts that solve real problems for real people, with internal links from those posts into product/service pages.
- **Fixing a high-ranking, low-converting blog post** (Lux's "How to File a Mechanics Lien in California," #1 organic, lost featured snippet): (1) remove/shrink the "who wrote this and why" byline blurb sitting at the very top — not valuable real estate; (2) add a clickable shortcut/CTA higher on the page instead of only at the bottom (e.g., "we cover all 20 steps here — or we can just do it for you," "File your lien today — $X"); (3) check keyword intent labels (informational vs. commercial) before assuming the post itself should convert; (4) audit near-duplicate pages (their "Guide" vs. "FAQ" page cover the same topic) for possible cannibalization; (5) options once a page already ranks: full rewrite (risk losing rank, upside on conversion) vs. leave content as-is and just move the CTA button up / add an exit-intent or timed slide-out offering a free consult.
- **Live SERP-intent check example** (mechanics lien niche): "mechanics lien California" → dominated by ca.gov, an SC Law templates/forms page ("not a very good website" per Matt), Sacramento County Public Law Library; "how to file mechanics lien California" → informational intent, Lux's site ranks #2; "file mechanics lien in California" (drop "how to") → commercial intent ("user wants to investigate brands, services"), Lux ranks #4. Read the intent label the tool assigns before deciding what a page should be trying to do.
- **Blog structural fixes** (Nick's "Does Content Marketing Work?" post): move a vague newsletter opt-in ("best strategies, tools and behind-the-scenes insights") out of the top of the post into a **~30-second delayed popup or an exit-intent popup**; make every H2 a literal question and put the direct answer as plain paragraph text immediately under it (no "TL;DR," no heading literally called "Introduction"); collapse redundant closing sections (post had separate Key Takeaways / FAQ / Conclusion sections that all restated the same thing) into one; end with a plain CTA that doesn't announce itself as a CTA (e.g., "we can help you with your content marketing, contact us today").
- Audit heading structure by literally counting H2s and marking which ones add unique value vs. which are structural filler — Nick's post had 9 H2s, of which 4–5 didn't add anything.
- Before targeting a difficult head term for a new post (e.g., "does content marketing work"), check real search volume/difficulty in a keyword tool first; consider an easier adjacent angle (e.g., "how does content marketing work with SEO").
- Reddit and Quora are good for sourcing topic/angle ideas, but still verify volume/viability in an actual keyword tool before writing.
- **Google Merchant Center flags you can't directly satisfy** (Michael's bookstore): can't force a carrier (Canada Post) to ship faster and can't unilaterally change return policy against business reality — instead, benchmark what direct competitors actually charge/offer for shipping and returns and adjust real business terms to match (e.g., test raising product price to subsidize free shipping) rather than trying to game the feed.

## Rules / dos & don'ts
- Exact-match keyword count of **10x** on a page can trigger "over-optimized" flags (Semrush flagged Melissa for this); a top-3 competitor used the exact keyword **only once**, favoring a variation instead.
- More words is not automatically better: competitor page (#3 rank, "Wildflowers and Pixels") ran **~2,000 words**; Melissa's non-ranking page ran **~3,200 words**.
- High authority is not required to rank: a #1-ranking competitor for the harder variation keyword had just **1 referring domain** yet outranked Wix, Canva, GoDaddy, and Weebly.
- Don't use redirects to force a page into your sitemap/get it indexed — link directly to the real URL. Matt: "If Google's like why — you're linking to this page but then it's redirecting — why you still linking to this page?"
- AI/automation SEO platforms (named: **Search Atlas**): fine to use for the keyword-research portion only. Do not let a tool auto-publish content or auto-edit title tags/meta descriptions across a site without human review.
- A fully-automated AI SEO tool (unnamed, "purple background," used on TikTok by "Jake from Wizard Marketing," also used by Melissa on her own site last summer) drove fast growth that **collapsed completely around August**, forcing a restart from zero.
- Local SEO requires NAP (name, address, phone) and location signals to actually be present/crawlable on the site (e.g., in the footer) — Google "looks for that to determine if it's a local business or not."
- Blog posts are informational-intent by default and shouldn't be expected to convert like a service/money page: "the intent of a blog post isn't always to convert, and usually it's not. It's to build trust."
- H2 = the question; the paragraph directly beneath it = the direct answer. Never head a section "Introduction." Never stack Key Takeaways + FAQ + Conclusion as three separate closers that repeat each other.
- Don't dangle a generic newsletter/CTA blurb without saying what industry/value it covers; that ask belongs in a delayed or exit-intent popup, not primary page real estate.
- Google Merchant Center: don't lie on the feed to satisfy "increase shipping speed" / return-policy prompts — match real competitor terms instead.
- Internal linking is described as **"the biggest factor and the biggest differentiator"** (from Matt's own internal AI page-analysis tool run on the HeyTony site) between pages that rank/get traffic and ones that don't.

## Tradeoffs & nuance
- Word count and keyword density don't correlate with rank in either direction on their own: a shorter page using the exact keyword once outranked a longer page using it 10x — matching search intent and formatting mattered more than either variable.
- Referring domains/domain authority help but aren't gatekeeping: one #1-ranked competitor had a single referring domain and still beat Wix, Canva, GoDaddy, Weebly — likely on relevance, topical depth, and content age rather than raw authority.
- Rewriting a page that already ranks is a real gamble — you can gain conversions but lose the existing ranking (Melissa's "near me" position) — so the safer sequence is add supporting content/internal links first and only rewrite after a defined trial window if nothing moves.
- AI/automation tools get a qualified yes: useful as a research aid/co-pilot (keyword research, drafting), but Matt won't deploy them unsupervised for a real client — he's separately running his own experiment of a fully AI-built-and-run site with minimal human intervention specifically to test whether that can rank, but is explicit it isn't yet something he'd recommend for an actual business.
- A keyword that looks like a real customer question isn't automatically worth targeting — check what the SERP is actually serving for that literal phrase; if the ranking intent doesn't match your business (Jason's "what blog software can I use"), leave it alone regardless of internal demand for the content.
- A page can rank #1 and still barely convert (Lux's mechanics-lien post) — that's not automatically a problem with the page; it may mean the business needs a separate, higher-commercial-intent page rather than forcing an informational post to sell.
- Possible keyword cannibalization (Lux's Guide vs. FAQ pages) was flagged but explicitly left as "something you'll have to investigate," not a confirmed diagnosis.
- Local SEO best practice (full NAP/footer) can directly conflict with a client's design preference ("clean footer") — flagged as a real tension, not dismissed either way beyond "it should be on the site somewhere."
- Even a practicing SEO can neglect their own site relative to client work (Melissa's "cobbler's shoes" backstory) — expertise doesn't exempt anyone from the same mistakes reviewed on others' sites.
- Location-page expansion (Melissa, Varuna) is repeatedly framed as high-effort but high-payoff — never presented as a quick win.
