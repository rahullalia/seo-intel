# Choosing Strategic Colors for Your Website — Replay 14.01.2026

## Tactics & step-by-step how-to
- **Find your CTA color**: look at your site's existing dominant color on the color wheel, then pick the color on the *opposite* side (complementary) for your CTA button — this creates maximum contrast so the eye goes there first.
- **Dominant + accent ratio**: keep one color dominant (most of the page) and use the complementary color only as a small "pop" (CTA, accent). A 50/50 split of two complementary colors makes neither stand out — reserve contrast for the one thing you want noticed.
- **Target-audience-to-color mapping workflow**: before picking colors, define who you're speaking to, then choose the palette that matches the vibe that audience expects (demoed live by recoloring one donut-shop mockup for four different audiences — see Case Studies).
- **AI + Pinterest palette workflow** (no exact prompt text given, described as a process): (1) ask AI to help you figure out your target audience, (2) ask AI what "vibe" that audience is going for, (3) take that vibe phrase and search it directly in Pinterest (e.g., "health conscious earthy tones," "playful color palette") to pull real mood-board images/palettes.
- **Full-page screenshot for redesign reference**: use a browser plugin (unnamed) that captures a full-length screenshot of a site, then reference that image while redesigning — gives an overall view of the whole page at once instead of scrolling.
- **Elementor video background how-to**: select the whole section/container in Elementor, then paste a video link (Vimeo or YouTube) into the background-video setting.
- **Readability-overlay technique**: place a solid color overlay (e.g., blue) on top of a video/image background, underneath the text — this is what makes white text stand out and stay readable versus a raw photo/video background.
- **Gradient + multiply-blend legibility trick**: draw a rectangle over the photo, set it to a black-to-transparent gradient (both gradient stops black, one end's opacity dropped to transparent), turn off its border, then set its blend mode to **Multiply** — this deepens/darkens the photo underneath without fully hiding it, making overlaid text readable. (Multiply blend is available in effectively any design tool.)
- **Building "gold" as a color**: gold almost never works as one flat hex value — build it as a gradient using several different gold-adjacent tones. Keep the lightest stop from getting too close to white (unreadable text on top); darken any stop that reads too pale, and still add a dark overlay/gradient before placing white text on it.
- **Screenshot-a-video-frame hero trick**: when a full-screen background video isn't available/working, grab a still frame from the existing video, open it in Photoshop, remove the logo/text baked into the frame, and use that as a static full-bleed hero image instead of a small boxed video player.
- **Heading tag fix**: if a template skips levels (e.g., jumps from H2 straight to H6), the ideal fix is re-tagging the skipped text to H4; not critical to fix immediately as long as H1/H2/H3 are structured correctly.
- **CTA copywriting fix**: replace vague CTA labels ("Contact Us," "Book Today") with specific, action-led copy naming exactly what happens next (example given: "Start your corporate video today").
- **Button shape as brand signal**: match button corner radius to the logo's shape language — sharp/square corners for brands with sharp-lined logos (corporate/structured feel); rounded corners read as playful, which clashes on a corporate brand.
- **Font swap for brand consistency**: don't use a rounded/playful font on a corporate brand (looks like Comic Sans, "goofy") — inspect the brand's existing headline font and reuse it instead.
- **Hero-first prioritization**: put the majority of design effort into the hero/top section specifically, because audience attention drops the further down a page you go — fewer people ever see the bottom.
- Eyeball the page title/meta for a live site to guess its target keyword as a quick gut-check while reviewing (Matt did this live: "I'm guessing your target keyword is premier New York video and photo production company").

## Rules / dos & don'ts
- **Complementary colors** (opposite sides of the color wheel) = maximum contrast → use for anything that needs to stand out (CTAs).
- **Analogous colors** (adjacent on the wheel) = harmonious/cohesive/relaxing feel → use for backgrounds and sections that should feel unified.
- Never split a complementary pair 50/50 — always keep one dominant, one accent.
- Color-to-audience associations used repeatedly on the call:
  - Clean/soft **blues** → trust, professional/corporate feel (office workers, B2B buyers).
  - **Pinks and pastels** → playful, kids/birthday-party audiences.
  - **Muted, unsaturated greens and beiges** → organic, clean-ingredient, natural products.
  - **Bold lime green or red against a dark background** → energy, gym/fitness/bulk-up audiences.
  - **Gold against black** → rich/luxury feel (used for a yacht brand).
- Don't use neon/high-saturation colors under white text — flagged as "almost impossible to read" in a live comparison against a working teal CTA.
- Don't default background grays too dark — a lighter gray was praised as looking "strong" and clean; overly dark grays were called a common overcorrection.
- Avoid video backgrounds where possible — they slow down site load. If used, treat it as a real tradeoff: only keep the video if it demonstrably helps conversions/branding enough to justify the slower load; otherwise use a static image.
- Avoid WordPress **Revolution Slider** — flagged as a page-speed drag; replace with a single static image.
- Prefer **Vimeo** over YouTube for background/embedded video — gives a "nice clean player."
- Heading tags: never have more than one H1 per page, and never put an H2 before the H1 — that's what actually confuses Google. Skipping a level (H2 → H6) is a lesser issue, tolerable if H1/H2/H3 are otherwise correct.
- A dark footer functions as a visual "end of site" cue and adds a sense of stability/completeness.
- Full-bleed hero image or video beats a small boxed-in video for impact.
- A brand's logo is only a small portion of its overall brand — color and font choices carry most of the "who this speaks to" signal, and can be changed independently of the logo.

## Case studies / examples / results
- **Travel website mockup** (all-blue, analogous palette) — CTA button recolored live to orange (complementary to blue): "we can immediately see that this CTA button is standing out."
- **Green website mockup** (analogous greens) — CTA recolored to red (complementary); Matt noted the red-on-green combo also reads as "Christmas colors."
- **Purple website mockup** (analogous purples) — CTA recolored to orange/yellow (complementary): "that really stands out here."
- **Donut shop mockup, same product/logo, four target audiences shown via color alone**:
  - Corporate/office-worker buyers → soft, clean blues (professional feel).
  - Kids' birthday parties → pinks and pastel colors (playful).
  - Organic/clean-ingredient positioning → muted greens and beiges (unsaturated).
  - Gym-goer/bulk-up audience → bold lime green or red against a dark background (energy/vibrancy).
- **Teal vs. neon-green CTA test** (live in Adobe XD) — teal worked fine; neon green with white text on top was "almost impossible to read."
- **"Lux" (construction company site)** — dark blue primary + orange secondary/CTA praised as "a great choice," a genuine complementary pairing that "really speaks to this construction audience clearly." Feedback: several UI blocks used slightly different blues — recommended unifying them closer to the one dark blue (more analogous/consistent) rather than several near-miss blues competing.
- **Paul's website** — too many competing colors (orange, blue, yellow). Live fix: removed the yellow background, replaced with a soft blue. Result: "a lot easier on my eyes," and the CTA stood out more against the calmer background. Aside: cited the Simpsons trivia (Matt Groening chose yellow because it stands out while flipping channels) to explain why yellow is so attention-grabbing — and therefore risky as a large background color rather than a small accent.
- **Yacht/boat-charter site ("Escape from Reality")** — reviewed twice in the call:
  - First pass: gold + black color scheme praised ("gold stands out nice against black," "works really well for yachts"); video was boxed into a small square — recommended making it a full-screen hero instead; recommended adding a dark footer for visual "end of site" stability. Matt mentioned he's separately building his own yacht website using neutral tones instead of gold, for comparison.
  - Text-on-slider was unreadable ("this is hard to read") — recommended a gradient overlay; existing teal-blue overlay felt "a little too soft" against the site's corporate vibe. Recommended dropping the multi-slide slider entirely for one single hero slide, since the current slider text disappeared before there was time to read it. Praised the site's existing lighter background gray as looking "strong." Confirmed full-screen video was not available/working on their builder — reinforcing the static-image workaround.
  - Final live redesign: screenshotted a frame from their existing video, removed the baked-in logo in Photoshop, built a new hero around it; rejected "Escape from Reality" as the H1 ("not descriptive enough") and proposed something keyword-forward instead, e.g. "Premiere [Boat] Tours and Rentals"; tried **Avenir** (thin weight) for an elegant feel matching their existing font; rebuilt the gold color as a multi-tone gradient (darkened where it read too pale, added an overlay for text contrast).
- **Corporate video/photo production company site (New York)**, built on Elementor with a **Revolution Slider**:
  - Guessed target keyword from the page: "premier New York video and photo production company."
  - Full-screen video wasn't displaying/available correctly on the site.
  - A full-width layout bug (page content not spanning the full screen width, confirmed in both Safari and Chrome) came up live and was **not resolved** — Matt tried setting the background image to "cover" but couldn't diagnose the root cause on the call.
  - Heading structure: H1 → H2 → H3 → then "a bunch of H6," then back to H2. Verdict: not critical since H1/H2/H3 were correctly structured; the H6s were template defaults (likely used just to shrink font size, not for semantic reasons); real risk would be duplicate H1s or an H2 ahead of the H1.
  - Live hero redesign: swapped the site's rounded/playful headline font for their existing brand font (rounded read as "Comic Sans," "goofy," clashing with a corporate audience); added a black-to-transparent gradient behind the text set to Multiply blend; bolded supporting text; changed the headline to lead with credibility — "We are an award winning corporate videographer provider in New York"; changed the CTA from vague "Contact Us"/"Book Today" to "Start your corporate video today"; changed the CTA button from rounded to sharp/square corners to match the brand's sharp-lined logo; flagged **Revolution Slider** as a speed drag and recommended replacing it with a single static image.
- **A Wix-built site** — an attendee reported that a full-screen video player wasn't available on Wix; Matt said he'd need to check Wix specifically since his own demos run on Elementor/WordPress.
- **Matt's own agency rebrand** (~2 years ago) — kept the exact same logo, but swapped the color palette from pinks to blues/teals and changed the font: "even though I'm using the same logo," the site reads as a completely different vibe/audience fit purely from the color and font change.
- **Matt's own current branding rationale** — targets small businesses that have "felt burnt in the past by large agencies" but still want competent help; uses rounded fonts for a friendlier/more personal feel (vs. standard sans-serif), and blues/teals because those colors are associated with technology — fusing a "personal" vibe with a "tech" vibe.
- Aside: Matt notes his own book's cover also uses a complementary color scheme.

## Tradeoffs & nuance
- Video backgrounds are a genuine tradeoff, not a flat rule: they slow the site down, but if the video measurably helps conversions or branding, "it might be worth it" — the default lean is still toward skipping video when avoidable.
- Complementary-color contrast only works if the ratio stays unbalanced — the same two colors that make a CTA pop at a small percentage will cancel each other out at a 50/50 split.
- A color that's objectively "attention-grabbing" (yellow, per the Simpsons example) can still hurt a design if it's used as a dominant background rather than a small accent — the same property that makes it good for a CTA makes it bad as a base color.
- Heading-hierarchy purity matters less than heading-hierarchy correctness at the top: skipping a level (H2→H6) is a minor, low-priority fix, but duplicate H1s or an out-of-order H1/H2 is treated as a real SEO risk.
- Saturated "stand out" colors can undermine their own purpose if they kill text contrast (neon green + white text) — standing out and staying readable are separate goals that don't automatically align.
- A brand's logo and its color palette are decoupled: the same logo supported two entirely different audience-facing vibes on Matt's own site purely via a color/font change — meaning color strategy can be revisited without a logo redesign.
- Gold specifically resists a simple, one-color solution — it needs both a multi-tone gradient (to avoid reading as yellow/brown) and a legibility fix (avoiding light tones/adding an overlay), so it takes more layered handling than a single hex swap.
