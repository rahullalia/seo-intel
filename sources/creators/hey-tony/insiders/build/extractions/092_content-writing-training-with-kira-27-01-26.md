# Content Writing Training with Kira — 27.01.26 (Kira, HeyTony Senior SEO Specialist)

## Tactics & step-by-step how-to
- **AI keyword brainstorm workaround (no paid tool):** prompt an AI tool with something like *"give me some high traffic, low competition keywords"* — specifically framed as: *"here's some high traffic, low competition keywords for a web design brand, for a web design service that a web design service can write SEO blog posts about."* Purpose is to get into the customer's headspace for topic ideas, not to get accurate volume/competition data (see Rules — AI keyword numbers aren't trustworthy).
- **What/How/Where/Who prefix trick** for surfacing bottom-of-funnel question content: take your core service term and type it into a keyword/question tool prefixed each way — "what [service]," "how [service]," "where [service]," "who [service]" (demoed live with "web design"). Good starting point for bottom-funnel, but pair with the funnel-mapping tactic below to avoid staying stuck at bottom-funnel only.
- **Funnel-mapping tactic for topic ideation:** map content across TOFU/MOFU/BOFU (top/middle/bottom of funnel). Bottom-funnel = searcher already knows they need your exact service (e.g., "web design" — high intent, lower volume). Middle/top-funnel = searcher doesn't yet realize your service is the fix for their actual problem (e.g., "why aren't I getting business," "how do I get consistent leads," "why is X not working on Squarespace"). Most available traffic tends to sit in middle/top funnel, not bottom.
- **Client discovery question to find middle/top-funnel topics:** ask clients (or think through it yourself), *"If I could wave a magic wand and give you anything, what problem would I solve?"* The literal answer (e.g., "I want consistent leads") is the raw material for topic ideas — write content around the underlying problem, not just the literal service name.
- **Photographer positioning tactic** (told as a real prior client's approach): write content with nothing to do with the core service, aimed at people before they know they need it — e.g., a photographer's blog covered "how to find the best wedding venue in this area," so the person builds trust with the brand long before they're shopping for a photographer, then converts later.
- Fixing an "Answer the Public only gives me 2 questions" problem: swap which tab/section you pull from ("Questions" vs. "all ideas") and test both the literal bottom-funnel term and a broader/adjacent middle-funnel phrase — narrow terms like "website design" return far less than a phrase people would actually type when they don't yet know they need the service.
- **Blog-post-vs-FAQ overlap check:** before writing a blog post on a question already answered in an on-page FAQ, check whether the two would target the *same* keyword/topic on the *same* page-level intent. If the FAQ only gives a partial/brief answer and the full topic can support its own page, it's fine to make it a separate blog post — treat it as a gut check ("if you feel like this page literally sounds exactly like this other page, it's a signal to adjust"), not a hard rule.
- **Never rewrite/duplicate a blog post to refresh it — edit it in place.** If a post needs updating, go back into the CMS and edit the existing post directly (whatever its original publish date) rather than writing something new and publishing it as a second entry — protects against keyword cannibalization.
- **Blog posting-cadence permission:** unlike video (YouTube), blog posts can be edited after publishing with no real downside — so if you're stuck on perfectionism, just publish to keep a cadence going and refine later, rather than delaying to get every post "just right."
- **Prioritization tactic when overwhelmed by "everything matters" SEO advice:** first ask your own business a scoping question — "what are my priorities for the next 3–6 months?" (revenue/leads vs. authority-building vs. brand). Then ask a second question — "is my business hyperlocal / location-bound, or do I serve broadly (any state/any English-speaking region)?" The combination of those two answers determines which levers (high-traffic/low-competition keywords, Google Business Profile optimization, conversion-rate optimization, service-page narrowing) should be the actual near-term focus — there is no single universal rank-order across all businesses.
- For a business that already gets plenty of leads/calls but a lot of them are unqualified (wrong service, e.g., "not this kind of procedure"): stop chasing high-traffic/low-competition keywords (that's what's flooding you with the wrong calls) and instead narrow/optimize the specific service pages that describe exactly what you do and don't do.
- For a business that needs more leads and isn't hyperlocal: deprioritize Google Business Profile work and instead prioritize high-traffic/low-competition keyword content plus conversion-rate optimization (making the "next step" — booking a call — obvious in layout/design).
- Hyphen/spacing/pluralization decisions (e.g., "permanent makeup" vs. "permanent-makeup," "ebook" vs. "e-book," "flashcards" vs. "flash cards"): check what your actual competitors/consumers use and default toward matching consumer search/display behavior over strict grammatical correctness, per your own optimization-vs-human values line (see Rules).

## Rules / dos & don'ts
- AI-tool keyword suggestions (ChatGPT/Claude/etc.) are **not accurate for traffic/competition** — that data lives behind paid tools (SEMrush, Ubersuggest) and is gated; use AI output for directional/ideation purposes only, never as a substitute for real keyword-volume data.
- Ideal image density in a blog post: **one image roughly every 200–300 words** — stated explicitly as an unrealistic target for a solo operator/small team, not a hard requirement ("that's not like we can... each of us on this call has a full SEO team").
- AI-generated images are fine to use in blog posts for SEO ranking purposes — Kira has personally used them across "many, many" blog articles that ranked, and has not seen any indication Google penalizes AI imagery, drawing the parallel to how Google initially resisted then accepted AI-written content once "it just has to be helpful."
- Keyword cannibalization = **more than one page or blog post ranking/competing for the same keyword** — avoid it; check before publishing a near-duplicate topic.
- Don't blindly follow "it depends" as a final answer without first anchoring to your own goals — pin down (a) 3–6 month business priority and (b) local vs. broad service area before choosing SEO tactics to prioritize.
- Grammar/correctness vs. actual search behavior: match what your real audience searches/expects to see (even if not grammatically "correct") **when your goal is consumer trust and conversion**, but this is a values call, not a mandate — some clients (e.g., lawyers) will insist on being technically correct over matching sloppy search phrasing, because looking "right" matters more to their brand.
- Don't obsess over the literal hyphen/space/word-boundary version of a keyword when a tool only surfaces one form — Kira's stated position: if Google can't tell the difference between a hyphenated and non-hyphenated version of the same term, "we've got bigger problems" — pick the version that fits your values and move on.
- Never let overwhelm stop cadence — small business owners can't do every SEO best practice simultaneously; decide priorities deliberately and give yourself grace rather than freezing.

## Tradeoffs & nuance
- Optimization vs. human/authentic voice is a spectrum, not a binary — Kira frames nearly every stylistic SEO decision (hyphenation, grammatically "incorrect" popular phrasing, keyword-stuffed sentences like "how to run dishwasher") as a personal-values line each business owner sets for themselves, not a universal best practice.
- You can't cleanly A/B-test "optimized" vs. "human" versions of the same content by comparing two different blog posts — traffic differences between two posts are confounded by other variables (backlinks, topic demand, age), so within-post philosophy differences aren't reliably measurable that way.
- Broad, high-traffic/low-competition keyword strategy is good for businesses hungry for volume, but actively bad for highly specialized businesses (plastic surgery, law) where it generates unqualified calls that cost staff time — the "more traffic" lever isn't universally positive.
- Google Business Profile optimization is high-value for hyperlocal businesses but a lower priority for businesses that serve broadly/nationally — location-based tactics aren't one-size-fits-all.
- AI images and AI content both faced initial platform skepticism ("Google won't rank AI content") that resolved to "fine, as long as it's helpful" — treated as a precedent for how the same resolution will likely apply to open questions like AI-image penalties.
- Reposting/refreshing old content is legitimate, but the line between "updating an existing post" (safe) and "duplicating/copy-pasting as a new post" (cannibalization risk) matters and wasn't clearly known even by the attendee who'd done it (Samantha wasn't sure which she'd done).
- FAQ content and a full blog post can coexist on the same topic without being cannibalization, as long as the FAQ only partially covers the topic — the boundary is fuzzy and explicitly described as "a gut check," not a bright-line rule.
- Perfect execution (curated real photography, a full SEO team dividing images/content/posting) is acknowledged as the ideal, but explicitly not the bar small business owners should hold themselves to — priorities should be chosen deliberately per business goals/values rather than attempting all best practices at once.
