## Tactics & step-by-step how-to

- **Keyword selection for ecommerce/print-on-demand** (Jorg — 2-month-old print-on-demand business, Etsy + Shopify shops, ~5 blog posts):
  - Use a keyword tool's "include keywords" filter to surface niche variants instead of targeting a broad category term.
  - Reject "equestrian" as a target keyword; target "horse mug" instead — checked live at 2% difficulty with roughly 1,700 combined search volume across US/UK/Australia.
  - Other viable long-tail variants surfaced live: mugs with horses, horse coffee mugs, horse racing mug(s).
  - For the "funny t-shirt" niche, audience-qualified variants are easier than the broad term and still sizable: funny t-shirts for men (easier), funny t-shirts for women (a lot easier), funny t-shirts for adults, father t-shirts funny, funny cat t-shirts, funny christian t-shirts, funny golf t-shirts.
  - Don't target the homepage with the store's generic tagline keyword (e.g., "funny t-shirts, apparel, and drinkware") — nobody searches that phrase. Put target keywords on collection pages, product pages, and blog posts; the homepage just needs to make a visitor understand what the site is about once they land from those entry pages.
  - Only the newest ~2 weeks of products had keyword research done (via Rank Week); older/existing products still need it.

- **Homepage vs. local-page targeting** (contrast Jorg's ecommerce case with Anthony's local-service case):
  - Local/service businesses: the homepage should target "[service] + [city]" (Matt's example: a dentist in Hamilton should target "dentist in Hamilton" on the homepage).
  - Ecommerce/content sites: don't target the homepage; ranking should happen on lower pages (collections, products, blog posts).

- **Site diagnosis workflow, used repeatedly across all four site reviews:**
  1. Open the specific page in question (not just the homepage) and ask "why isn't this ranking."
  2. Check the title tag — does it describe what the business/page does and include the target keyword?
  3. Check that a meta description exists and is compelling/clickable.
  4. Check heading-tag order: must go H1 → H2 → H3 without skipping a level going down; skipping backward up the hierarchy (e.g., H5 back to H3, H2, or H4) is fine.
  5. Check that every body link and menu item is actually clickable/visible for a human — if you can't click it, Google likely can't parse it reliably either.
  6. Run a performance/PageSpeed-style audit for render-blocking resources and image/video/font payload size.

- **Tatiana's site (corporate video/photo production, NYC) — fixes walked through live:**
  - Kill the rotating image carousel as the hero; hovering over it froze the rotation, so most visitors (who don't hover) never see any single slide's headline. Replace with one static hero image (ideally the crew actually shooting) plus persistent, always-visible text describing the business.
  - Fix heading order: her page jumped H1 → H5, then H3 → H6 in places; a heading-2 line also ended in the vague phrase "…in New York and beyond" — tighten to something specific (a named region) instead of "beyond."
  - Make "Contact Us" a visually distinct, consistent CTA across every page (homepage and About page used the same low-contrast, unstyled treatment) — turn it into a real button, bolder/bigger text, or rewrite the copy to "Get a Free Quote" / "Book a Free Call."
  - Don't add client/company logos you don't have explicit rights to use (she'd already swapped to plain text names over trademark concern) — Matt declined to advise using logos outright and pointed her to a trademark lawyer instead.
  - Link testimonials/client list out to real, verifiable reviews (e.g., Google reviews) — because anyone can fabricate a "review" screenshot with ChatGPT now.
  - Don't autoplay a large, blurry/over-compressed hero video; swap for real photography of the crew shooting, and if keeping any video, keep the file small AND genuinely showcase-quality (with sound), not a slow-loading low-res clip.
  - Performance audit findings (homepage + photography subpage): render-blocking resources, custom web fonts (recommended switching to standard/system fonts), font-display issues, legacy JavaScript, forced reflow, poor image delivery (individual images cited at roughly 1MB, ~900KB, 700KB, 600KB), unused CSS/JS, and one page loading around 8MB of images total (traced mainly to the slider).
  - Fix via a WordPress caching/optimization plugin (WP Rocket — see Tools) rather than manually chasing each flagged item.

- **Anthony's site (Chickadee Dumpster Rental, Port St. Lucie FL) — local SEO fixes:**
  - Run a keyword tool on the region name ("Treasure Coast") with an "include keywords" filter for dumpster/bin/garbage terms to surface real local search terms and their difficulty scores.
  - Build one page per city actually searched (Port St. Lucie, Vero Beach) rather than a county-level rollup page — "Indian River [County] dumpster rental" showed negligible search volume, so skip the county page entirely.
  - Root-cause a page that isn't ranking despite being on-topic by checking whether on-page copy uses the exact phrasing/abbreviation people search: the site spelled out "Saint Lucie" everywhere while searchers type the abbreviated "St. Lucie" — fix by replacing "Saint" → "St." sitewide, including in navigation.
  - Simplify the service-area mega-menu: collapse the nested county-then-city dropdown into one flat list of location pages — the nested version was overflowing/unclickable (submenu items fell off-screen and couldn't be clicked).

- **Diana's site ("Tripod Coaching and Consulting") — fixes walked through live:**
  - On the Services page, found two H1 tags, with one hidden behind a colored bar via white-text-on-white-background (a classic cloaking trick) — remove the duplicate hidden H1 and instead set the real target keyword ("business operations" was the example) as the page's actual on-page focus keyword.
  - Fix the Services page's broken/keyword-less URL slug and rewrite its thin meta description.
  - Generate a unique custom header/banner image for every blog post via ChatGPT (or pull from Unsplash, or Adobe Stock + AI credits) — every post currently uses the same generic banner, making posts indistinguishable from one another.
  - Disable comments entirely; also check the rendered page source even after "disabling" comments in the CMS — her theme still exposed comment-count/comment-action/comments-feed markup that a spam bot was exploiting to post backlink spam despite no visible comment form.
  - Change the post-footer CTA from "leave us a comment" to a link/pointer to the Contact page.
  - Restyle links: underline them and use a distinctly darker/different color from body text (her links were barely distinguishable from surrounding text).
  - Explicitly label the in-post outline "Table of Contents" instead of leaving it as an unlabeled list of words.
  - Break up long blog text with visuals — demoed live by pasting the full post title + body into ChatGPT and generating a Canva-ready infographic/timeline in seconds (exact prompts under Tools).
  - Delete all 162 auto-generated WordPress tag pages (surfaced via a Search Console/Semrush crawl); most held only 1–2 posts each. Replace tags with a small number of consolidated category pages (Matt's own site: one "Facebook" category holds organic + ads + social posts together; one "SEO" category replaces separate "SEO," "local SEO," "marketing techniques," "local SEO for beginners" tags).
  - Keep crawl depth shallow — every post should be reachable within about one click from the main blog-roll page.
  - Consider adding a short, self-recorded video (e.g., filmed on Zoom) to a ranking post to try to also capture a video-result/SERP-feature slot, provided it adds value beyond reading the text verbatim.

- **Judging whether a change worked:** wait roughly 2–3 months after making an on-page change, using that window to keep publishing blog posts and internally linking to the changed page — framed as helping "the ecosystem," not passive waiting.

## Rules / dos & don'ts

- Never leave a title tag that doesn't describe the actual business/keyword — the first fix checked on every site reviewed.
- Never rely on hidden text (font color matching background color) to stuff a keyword — Google devalues/ignores it; called out as an old/early black-hat tactic.
- Heading tags must descend without skipping a level (H1→H2→H3); jumping backward up the hierarchy (e.g., H5 back to H3/H2/H4) is fine.
- Exception: using H1 tags purely for visual styling is acceptable ONLY when organic Google search isn't the page's traffic source (Matt's own Rank Week landing page draws traffic from Instagram/email, so heading hierarchy "doesn't matter in this case").
- Don't build a location/service page speculatively — build it only if keyword research shows real search volume for that specific term (skip a county page at ~0 volume; build the city page instead).
- Match the exact abbreviation/spelling searchers actually use (e.g., "St." not "Saint") — don't assume the grammatically "correct" spelled-out form is what ranks.
- Don't use company logos you don't have explicit rights to use — check the legal/trademark situation (consult a trademark lawyer) rather than guessing.
- Delete tag pages, always. Use category pages instead.
- Turn blog comments off — for a small-business blog the realistic outcome is spam/backlink-fishing, not real engagement.
- Homepage keyword targeting: yes for local/service businesses (city + service term); no for ecommerce/content sites (target lower pages instead).
- No keyword tool is both cheap and good: Ubersuggest is the cheap option but less accurate; Semrush/Ahrefs are more accurate but cost more.
- **Ahrefs Lite: $29/month, capped at 100 searches/month.**
- **WP Rocket: Matt first guessed "like a hundred bucks," then corrected to roughly $59/year** (he says it used to be a one-time lifetime license).
- Wait ~2–3 months after a specific on-page change before judging whether it worked.
- Commit at least ~6 months of consistent SEO work before expecting to see overall results.
- Design/contrast rule of thumb: "when in doubt," make it black.
- A CTA (e.g., "Contact Us") must be visually distinct from surrounding nav/body text and consistent site-wide, not identical across homepage/About/etc. with no emphasis.
- Social proof should link out to real, checkable reviews (Google reviews, etc.) rather than only static client-name/logo lists, since fake testimonial screenshots are now trivial to generate with AI.
- Avoid job-seeker-intent keyword variants when the goal is client acquisition — "business operations analyst" and "business operations specialist" read as job-search queries, while "business operations consultant" was flagged as the better client-facing term.

## Tradeoffs & nuance

- Comments cut both ways: they can add keyword-relevant text/variation and signal engagement (a possible ranking positive), but the realistic outcome on a small-business blog is spam — Matt's call is that it's not worth the risk, so disable them.
- Category/blog-roll pages are mostly built "for Google" (crawlability/structure), not for humans — most real visitors land directly on a specific post from search rather than browsing the roll page, so don't over-invest in making that page a destination in itself.
- H1-for-styling is normally an anti-pattern but Matt uses it deliberately on his own Rank Week page because all of its traffic is non-organic (Instagram/email) — the rule bends specifically when Google will never crawl the page as a search entry point.
- Whether Google indexes an H1 present on only one rotating carousel slide is genuinely uncertain — "it depends on what Google sees when they render the page" — framed as an inconsistent risk, not a guaranteed miss.
- Local page-building is a volume tradeoff: build for the exact geography/phrasing people type (city-level "St. Lucie," not county-level "Indian River County") rather than mirroring an internal org chart of service areas.
- Ubersuggest vs. Ahrefs/Semrush is a discovery-vs-accuracy tradeoff; Katie's workaround is to combine both rather than pick one — bulk-discover cheaply, then verify the shortlist on the pricier/more-accurate tool.
- Legal caution: Matt declined to give a definitive yes/no on using client logos without permission and deferred to a trademark lawyer rather than risk giving bad advice.
- Patience vs. impatience: Matt acknowledges a 2–3 month per-change wait and a 6-month overall commitment "might sound crazy," but frames the waiting period as productive (continued publishing + internal linking) rather than passive downtime.
