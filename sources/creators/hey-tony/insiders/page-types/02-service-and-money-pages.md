# Service pages and money pages

*Part of the HeyTony page-type research (`page-types/`). The rules every page inherits are in `00-rules-for-every-page.md`; the citation key, sources, and file map are in `README.md`.*

A money page is any page a visitor lands on when they are ready to buy or hire: service pages, product pages, collection pages, location pages, pricing pages. For a service business the service page is the money page ("Service page equals a money page"), and it is the page every blog post on the site exists to feed. Matt's architecture has two tiers: a services overview (parent or hub) page that lists every service and mostly exists for navigation, and one child page per service, each built around exactly one keyword, where the real SEO effort goes. This chapter covers the whole discipline: what counts as a money page, what goes on it, how long it should be, how much keyword it needs, how it links, how to audit and rewrite it, and when to leave it alone.

## What it is for and what it targets

- Definition: "A money page is any page a visitor lands on when they are ready to buy or hire." Product pages, service pages, collection pages, and location pages are all money pages and all get the same discipline; "location pages are money pages, product pages are money pages, services are money pages, collections are money pages." [playbook III 3.1; calls 002, 066, 075]
- The plainest version: "A money page is a page on your website that makes you money. It's a service page or a product page, a page that somebody goes to when they are looking to buy or to hire you." "Service page equals a money page." "Barbara, service page is 100% a money page." "All you need is one." [calls 075, 025]
- Money page is about intent, not checkout: "People are ready to spend money when they visit that page"; "a page that somebody has a high intent. They're coming to that page because they are looking for that service." It "should be able to convert or book," or at least link to a booking or contact action. [calls 073, 116, 002]
- Matt's email taxonomy: money pages are "services, service + city combos, industries served, product or pricing pages. Anything someone lands on ready to buy." Content pages are "blog posts, guides, FAQs, comparisons. Anything someone lands on while researching." Junk to ignore: "tag pages, author pages, pagination, /cart/, /privacy-policy/, media attachments." The money page to work on first is "the product or service page that should be making you the most money." [emails 2026-08-04, 2025-12-08]
- Not money pages: blog posts, contact pages, and booking or appointment pages ("those pages don't rank"). A third-party booking tool is not your money page even though your money page links to it: "The money page is the page that sends a visitor TO the intake form; the form itself is not the money page." "you have a service, sex therapy for couples... That is the money page. That's the page where they click on the link and then you make money"; linking its CTA to a contact or intake form "is the right way to do it." [playbook III 3.1; calls 074, 114]
- A money page can send the buyer off-site to pay and still be the money page: Maritza's book page (testimonials, pictures of people buying) sends buyers to Gumroad, and the Rank Week page on heytony.ca ranks number one and sends people to SamCart: "that's okay." [call 031]
- Examples Matt gives: dentist = dental implants or Invisalign page; realtor = "homes for sale in Scottsdale" or the buy / sell pages; plumber = the 24-hour emergency page; hot sauce company = ghost pepper or Carolina Reaper product pages or a collection; magician = one page per event type; hat store = baseball hats collection; personal trainer in Vancouver; car accident lawyer; garage door company = "garage door installation Scottsdale"; psychotherapist = couples counseling or PTSD therapy pages; law firm = one landing page per practice area; electrician = EV charger installation, ceiling fan, home renovation pages; telehealth GLP-1 = "your men's, women's and military pages are your money pages"; shop page, rental page, Airbnb reservation page. [playbook III 3.1; calls 075, 118, 073, 012, 025]
- If stuck, ask an LLM directly: "I am a realtor, what would be my money page?" [playbook III 3.1; Part VI 1.7]
- Leanne's framing: Matt's "money page" means the core offerings you make the most money from; she calls a single-offer page a "sales page" or "landing page," and the homepage can also be a money page. [call 028]
- Why the service page is worth the effort: "Service pages are actually easier to optimize than product or category pages (at least in my opinion). Why? Because when someone is searching for a service, they're already looking for information. They're problem-aware. And if you can answer their questions clearly and quickly, you're more likely to win the lead." [book ch. 40]
- The job of a transactional page: "The goal here isn't to educate or convince, it's to close. So when you're optimizing for transactional keywords, think clarity, confidence, and ease of use. Make it obvious what you're offering, how much it costs, and how they can get it. At this stage, people don't want to be sold to, they want a solution. Your job is to make sure they don't have to look anywhere else." Example transactional searches: "buy noise-canceling headphones," "book dentist appointment near me," "hire plumber." [book ch. 3]
- Write the page "for a customer who's comparing two or three local companies right now, not someone doing research." [email 2026-08-04]
- The learn-vs-hire test: "Anything that ends with a conclusion, anything that is informational is a blog post... Service pages should be what then: I know I have anxiety, I'm looking for a therapist, right? So anxiety therapy in Manhattan is what I would call that page." If someone would Google the topic for information, write a blog post; if they would Google it to hire someone, write a service page; same topic, different content, never blended. "the content is written differently depending on what the intent is": "how to do snow removal in a parking lot" is a post, wanting to hire someone is a service page, and a product page (snowblower) states specs ("great for up to 2 feet of snow, blows snow 50 feet") rather than arguing why you need one. [calls 070, 077; playbook II 2.3]
- Applied: "Ayahuasca couples retreat" (20 US searches a month, about 0 difficulty) "I wouldn't even do that as a blog post. I would do that as like a service page... hey, you can come do this retreat here"; "how much is an ayahuasca retreat" becomes a blog post. A money page assumes the visitor is past education and just needs to know you offer the solution and how to book. [call 088; playbook II 2.3]
- "You can't rank for a keyword if you don't have a page about that keyword." "The only way to rank is if you have a page about that specific topic." "Google wants to find a specific page... you won't be able to rank on Google if you don't have a page that is targeting that keyword." Put transactional terms on dedicated pages, not blog posts. [calls 087, 061, 119; playbook II 2.3]
- Match the page type Google already ranks. Google the exact phrase first and build the matching format: "If they're case studies, yours should be a case study. If it's a blog post yours should be a blog post. If it's a service or a money page, yours should be a service or money page." For "affordable 4D ultrasound Houston" the results were pages: "none of these are blog posts... If it's not a blog post, you shouldn't have a blog post. It's probably a service page." If the top results are platforms (Vimeo-style, marketplaces) for a local service business, "maybe this isn't the right keyword for you," even at 0 difficulty. "best video production company in New York" is a list intent (Clutch ranks), so a single "we are the best" page fights the wrong intent. If all results for "podcast production" are how-to guides, write the guide: "Don't be scared to show people how to do it themselves." [calls 112, 096, 113, 084; playbook II 2.2]
- Money Page Doctor flags this too: submit a blog post for a commercial keyword and it says "blog post detected... consider creating a dedicated service page for this commercial keyword." If a blog post is cannibalizing a money keyword, "maybe change that blog post around or consider removing it." [calls 074, 022; playbook III 4.4]
- Do not build a blog post as a stand-in for a future city service page and 301 it later: "Why are you doing extra work just to redirect later? That's going to confuse Google"; if Google shows service pages for that keyword a post "is going to be very difficult to rank." [call 065]
- A blog post and a page "look the exact same to Google... It's just content." Service pages usually chase harder keywords, which is why they rank slower. [call 108]
- One keyword per page, always, no exceptions. "Think of every page as a silo": people google "cosmetic dentistry St. Catharines," "dental implants," "dental veneers," "dentures," one at a time, "And that allows us to create a page to rank for that specific keyword or that main topic." "every page on your website should be answering one question or solving one problem." "Every page should go after one specific keyword." A keyword can be 3 to 4 words, one full question, or one tight cluster of variants, and "a keyword can be a whole sentence" ("custom home builder Burnett County"). "When you try to rank for several, you won't rank for any or you'll rank lower for all of them." Kira: a focus keyword is "like a band aid. You use it once, you don't reuse it." [course: every-page-1-main-topic; calls 052, 084, 075, 068, 107; playbook III 1.1]
- Word-order and plural variants are one topic, not several: "Chicago luxury newborn photographer" / "Chicago photographer newborn" / "Chicago newborn photography" is ONE page. One page can and does rank for many phrasings of the same intent (SEO in Hamilton, SEO agency Hamilton, SEO company in Hamilton, SEO services) "because people don't all type in the same version of that keyword." [playbook III 1.1; call 052]
- A second keyword means a second page. Nary's tool suggested "outsourced accounting services St. Petersburg Florida" (440 searches, KD 6%) and ChatGPT told her to optimize both on one page; Matt: "No, do another page." Woodchip boiler vs biomass boiler: one money page per term, "people are looking at one or the other," plus a blog post "X vs Y" explaining they are the same. [calls 048, 019]
- Cannibalization, Matt's explanation: Google wants the best user experience, and "if multiple results for a keyword are from the same website, how is that a good user experience?" "If you have the exact same keyword on two different pages, Google looks at them and goes, well, I don't know which page to rank. Maybe I'm not going to rank either of them." "these guys are trying to double dip. We're only going to show the one page... None of them are gonna rank." Do not have more than one page about a topic: "that's what we call cannibalizing." [calls 052, 025, 125; course: every-page-1-main-topic]
- How close is too close: "Chicago Newborn Photography" on the homepage and "Newborn Photography Chicago" on a service page are "still going after the same variation and the same premise of the keyword"; a homepage "Chicago newborn photographer" plus a service page "newborn photography" was "different enough." Near-duplicate service pages do not get indexed: Pavitra's "Interior Design Services" and "Full Service Interior Design Consultation" pages sat unindexed beside her "Interior Design Studio" page: "Google's probably looking at these pages and saying this is all the same thing... can you consolidate these pages?" "if you create pages that are similar, then they're going to compete for one another... and they're not going to rank." [calls 050, 043, 010]
- Blog topics must not reuse the money keyword. Kathy (website management services): blog about a different topic such as "website design in 2026" and link the words "website management" to the service page: "That's not cannibalizing, because the topic of that page is different than the topic of your money page." Only use the money keyword in a blog title if the post is genuinely about it ("how do you choose a website management service"). Med spa: the money page is "Hydrafacial in Scottsdale," the blogs cover pros and cons, health benefits, how long it takes, and can close with "if you're looking for Hydrafacial in Scottsdale, Arizona" plus the link. A deep-clean blog post plus a deep-clean service page is cannibalization. A blog post on "commercial demolition hauling" when a service page exists cannibalizes it and "people aren't looking for a blog post." [calls 009, 058, 116, 105]
- Comparison content does not cannibalize a single-service page: a Botox page is about Botox; "Botox versus Dysport versus..." is "a brand new comparison piece with a different intent, further down the funnel ("That's like the consideration page")" and belongs on its own page. A FAQ question on a treatment page ("how long does permanent makeup last") can also become a full blog post "as long as you're not keyword cannibalizing," i.e. the page's main keyword differs from the blog's. [calls 048, 092]
- Which service gets the homepage: for a local or service business the homepage targets [service] + [city], the single highest-revenue service becomes the homepage's keyword, and every other service gets its own page; a national or virtual business optimizes the homepage for the largest nearby metro and spins out location pages; if the homepage naturally outranks the intended money page, treat the homepage as the money page and point future links there. A cleaning company's main site targets the small suburb (Ancaster) while location pages target the bigger, more competitive cities; an IT company's main service pages target the main location ("Lubbock IT support"). [service-page-master; calls 069, 038]
- Pick one main city per service page instead of listing several: a residential pest control page whose H1 had no location ("Where?") and whose H2 named "Naples, Fort Myers and Orlando" ("You have to get a lot more specific than that") should become "Residential Pest Control Orlando," and then no separate Orlando page duplicating it is needed. [call 070]
- Splitting bundled services, the book version: "Each service gets its own page. Why? Because each page on your website should be about one specific topic." "If I were to throw all my services onto one massive page, what would the main keyword be? 'Digital marketing'? That's way too broad. And from an SEO standpoint, it's a wasted opportunity." [book ch. 40]
- Splitting bundled services, the call versions: pet sitting, overnight care and dog walking become three pages ("if somebody's coming for pet sitting, they don't care about the dog walking part"); "custom jewelry" becomes custom engagement ring, custom wedding ring, custom promise ring ("if you want to rank for something you need a page about that topic"); HVAC = furnace installation, furnace swap, furnace repair, AC install, AC swap, AC repair; plumber = "not just 'plumbing services'": drain repair, drain snaking, new pipes; electrician = panel replacements, ceiling fan changes, full home renovations, knob and tube removal; hair salon = hair dyeing, haircuts, male vs female haircuts; wedding photographer = elopement, couples shoots, engagement shoots; magician = "Magician Kent," "Wedding Magician Kent," "Corporate Magician Kent" (a wedding searcher landing on a page that also talks birthdays and corporate "doesn't really make sense for them"); psychotherapist = couples therapy, marriage counseling, PTSD therapy, CBT, anxiety therapy in Columbus ("there's going to be one that's easier and you'll start getting calls for those keywords"); string quartet = a page per ensemble (quartet, trio, duo, solo) and per occasion (weddings, corporate, bar mitzvahs), not a vague "packages and pricing" page; holiday decorator = per season and per service (design, installation and takedown, storage); bookkeeper = monthly bookkeeping, cleanup, catch-up, then per-industry pages ("bookkeeping for HVAC companies," landscaping, pest control, pool service, plumbers, general contractors); organizer = garage, bathroom, laundry room, kitchen under overview pages (commercial, residential, moving); mover = home furniture movers, appliance movers, apartment movers, "hire movers," even route pages (New York to Washington) if keyword research supports them; flight-compensation company = a page per airline ("Porter flight delay compensation"); cosmetician = a page per treatment; "Down Under" in a nav dropdown = separate Australia and New Zealand pages because Google does not know "down under" means those. [calls 020, 001, 116, 071, 118, 075, 025, 061, 042, 052, 068, 114, 072, 092, 088]
- Two adjacent offers, two pages: a portrait photographer who also sells oil portraits from photos gets "portrait photographer/photography Birmingham" and "oil painting portraits Birmingham" and upsells between them; do not mix wedding photography and portrait painting on one page. A photography brand adding video should "create those as individual pages rather than adding that onto the service there" (no domain change). A web development agency whose competitors rank for "web design" should "add on another service called Web Design" as its own page plus a post on the difference linking to both. [calls 075, 050, 106]
- Get more specific as you grow: a cosmetics manufacturer plans "hair care shampoos" rather than only "we make shampoo," to "start to get a little more specialized." A product sold under three bundles: "I would let keyword research dictate what I do"; if the keywords differ, keep three pages, if not, merge. A course sold under seven names: a short post comparing the names, and/or a money page per name that "explain them slightly differently... basically say it's the same core concept," starting with the easiest to rank. [calls 005, 116, 042]
- How many service pages: "You don't need 25 services"; three or four service pages is fine, and keep linking blogs to those rather than inventing "content pages." "If you only have one money page, add one. But you probably need more than one." "You don't need to make all your service pages and launch them all at once"; "You don't have to do this overnight." A long services list is "totally fine" for visitors and "helps with the SEO"; an auto shop listed 36 services, each with its own page. [calls 015, 012, 071, 061, 039]
- Overview (parent) page vs child pages: the overview "lists out all of our different services... more for navigation to get to the individual service pages. And that's when we can really go hard with our SEO for the individual service pages." Matt's own services page just lists the two services: "this page isn't meant to rank. These specific service pages are meant to rank"; make it "as minimal as possible" and link through to a page per service. Denroy: "Create a hub page that links to every single one of your service pages"; it becomes "your main hub page that internal links to all of the other pages," do not remove it, optimize it over time, keep adding links to new pages. A psychotherapy practice's "cluster or pillar is services": a Services page listing dating, pregnancy, anxiety, fertility, each clicking to its own page, instead of a huge dropdown. An HVAC site: "a button that says services, and then on that services page, you can have like furnace, ac, whatever, like different categories of them... And then you could have the different services for each of those." [calls 016, 052, 015, 068, 070, 001]
- Name pages with the words customers actually search. "Before I create a page, what is the purpose? Are people searching for that thing? If they are not searching for that keyword, I don't create the page unless it's like a landing page." Invented service names (Marketing Mountain, Lead Forge) are not searched; use generic descriptive names. Service-area sub-pages nobody searches (county pages for a dumpster company) should not exist: "I don't create a page on my website for SEO unless I know people are looking for it." [calls 105, 103]
- Avoid jargon and wrong-intent names: "help desk" and "managed IT services" lose to what the average owner types ("my computer is broken," "IT support," or niche to "virtual IT support"); "WordPress web design" returns WordPress itself; "Total Yellowstone" pulls the TV show (use "Yellowstone excursions"); "premier" is a word "nobody's Googling... They'll say the best or the top"; a tool's "cat fighting help" is awkward as an H1, so research the synonyms (cat behaviorist, cat trainer, cat coach) and pick one or two; Google preferred "web design" over "website design" in Hamilton and "that slight variation matters"; Kira tests variants in the keyword tool before committing ("we buy junk cars Dallas" 210/mo KD 9 beat "we buy junk cars Dallas Texas" 20/mo and "junk car buyer"). The nav label word "services" "isn't necessarily going to matter" for SEO, "it's more of like the content that's on the page" (a florist can label it "Occasions"). [calls 054, 021, 084, 031, 058, 118, 107, 042]
- Do keyword research for every service page: "you should do this for every single page on your site," finding the main keyword and secondary keywords; pick the keyword you want even if difficult (plumber in Hamilton, KD 60), then add variations as secondary keywords on the same page ("plumber in Hamilton, maybe plumbers in Hamilton, Ontario"). Book version: primary "plumber Detroit," secondaries "plumber in Detroit," "plumbing services Detroit MI," "local plumbing companies in Detroit": "we're going to sneak in through the back door and rank for the secondary keywords first." Shortcut: paste a competitor's service page into the keyword tool to see exactly what it ranks for. [course: finding-keywords; course: keyword-research; book ch. 36]
- Easier service keywords beat the city-wide head term: "dentist St. Catherine" is 1,000 searches and very hard; "emergency dentist" or "pediatric dentist" are "way easier to rank for"; "wisdom teeth removal" is 880 searches and easy: "I'd rather go for that easier keyword." "cyber security services" alone is too difficult; "SEO" is "an extremely difficult keyword," so optimize for "hire SEO expert in [City]" or "hire SEO services"; 90%-difficulty hosting terms should go narrower and local ("Texas website hosting"). "when you're trying to rank locally... start in a much smaller area, and then once you win that area, start branching outwards" (HeyTony: Hamilton, then Toronto, then Windsor). [course: finding-keywords; calls 038, 082, 097]
- A zero-volume or tiny keyword is still worth a page: "this could actually be a really good opportunity for us because there's no keyword difficulty... if that's a service that you offer, you can still rank for that." "commercial trash compactor NJ" at zero volume: "if one person is searching commercial trash compactor... is that not good?" "spider control Orlando" at about 20 searches and 0 to 2% difficulty: "this is the exact kind of keyword I would go after... If I get 20 customers a month, I'm going to be happy," and it needs zero backlinks. "brand storyteller staffing": "the people who are searching those keywords have a higher intent... Most people aren't going after keywords with very low volume because they're hogs." Per-industry bookkeeping pages: volume minimal or zero but intent high. "narcissistic abuse recovery program" (0 KD, about 150/mo) topped the results the day after a press release. "Catholic natural family planning online classes" "needs a dedicated page for this keyword." KD 20 to 30 is achievable but slower. [course: fixing-audit-issues-light-keyword-research; calls 021, 070, 029, 061, 009, 119, 056]
- Never put "near me" in your keyword, URL, or copy: "if you are local and you see near me in there, don't include near me in the keyword. That's not going to help you. That just looks spammy as hell." "That is the sign of somebody who doesn't know how to do SEO." Google knows where you and the searcher are; the keyword is service plus city ("dental implants Hamilton"). Never use "near me" as the audit focus keyword either. [course: finding-keywords; call 075; playbook III 4.2]
- Neighborhood and area names: the course says do not use neighborhood or area names like "Garden City Dental" or "Martindale Dental" as keywords; "choose keywords that make sense for your business, that aren't crazy difficult to rank for." But get as specific as the searches allow ("Is it Movers Leslieville, Movers Queen West"), and a service page about one specific DC neighborhood ("very unique, very useful, and very specific... a lot of value and research") hit the top of the AI Overview within two days. [course: finding-keywords; calls 025, 001]
- Qualifiers for remote or non-local clients: an India-based web designer targeting US/UK should say on the page that you speak English, support US clients, work the same hours, and go after long-tail keywords like "website designer in India who speaks English and works 9 to 5 Eastern." A homeschool math tutor should not go local at all: "Talk about the problem that people are having and the solution that you have" ("math tutoring for homeschool students," volume 30, difficulty 0). For an easy keyword in another language: "your location. Maybe you can mention that on the page or maybe not"; adding a city to a long keyword makes you more likely to rank there but no more likely elsewhere, "and we don't want to stuff keywords." Location in the H1 is fine even if you live outside the area you serve. Never mention a city you do not serve in title, meta or H1 (a wedding photographer's traffic came from Dallas). [calls 020, 070, 112, 061, 017]
- Only money and service pages need the customer's real language; run keyword research natively in that language, never translate. [playbook II 1.5]
- Offer only what you actually sell: Matt kept a website design page after stopping the service: "people go to your website, they call you and you're like, no, we actually don't do website design. Then they hang up and they go to the next search result. That's going to hurt your search results." Plenty of leads but the wrong fit (a surgeon called for procedures he does not do): stop chasing more high-traffic keywords and narrow the service pages to describe exactly what you do and do not do. [calls 117, 119; playbook II 2.6]
- Seasonal or event-driven money pages: "Six months out, that's when you want to start doing SEO for those pages" (a San Jose furniture rental page ahead of the Super Bowl). [call 114]
- AI search does not remove the need: "do we even need to focus as much on optimizing the website, on doing different page for different service and location?" "No, you still need to do that... it is doing multiple searches for a longer query... you still need to be giving Google that information." Bottom-of-funnel pages "where people are looking to convert and buy" remain important. [call 038]
- "How it works" style pages may carry a keyword ("working with Blue Collar"); "every page should have a keyword" except the about page. Collection pages can work like service pages for long-tail product keywords ("funny T shirts for women"). An affiliate who cannot sell on her own site can build comparison or "alternatives" pages as pages, placed "in the footer maybe." [calls 088, 103, 114]
- Revenue is capped by fulfillment capacity, not lead volume; past that point conversion rate and pricing are the levers, not more traffic. [playbook I 11; VI 5.1]

## What goes on it, top to bottom (the structure)

- Copy first, then layout; the message dictates the section structure. One section = one H2 = one topic, with alternating background tints so each reads as distinct. Most important messages near the top, because "the longer it is, the less likely people reach the bottom." [playbook III 3.3]
- The canonical service-business skeleton, in order: (1) Hero: H1 (focus keyword + city for a local page, plain language) + supporting subhead + one CTA button, all visible with zero scrolling, and a trust signal (stars, stat, logos) in or right below it. (2) Trust row: about 3 of years of experience, number of happy clients, 5-star rating, licensed/insured, awards, association logos. (3) About / intro: what you do, how, who you serve, ideally an audience-identifier layout (icon boxes per persona) so visitors self-identify. (4) Services / products overview: a brief intro to each, each linking to its own dedicated page. (5) Testimonials: pasted real reviews. (6) "How it works" / process: plain numbered steps of what happens after they book (defuses first-time-buyer anxiety). (7) FAQ tied to that page's focus keyword. (8) Final CTA at the very bottom. Build the body with lists, tables, and explicit numbered steps. [playbook III 3.3; service-page-master]
- The book's must-cover list for every service page, verbatim: "What the service is (Use plain English, not jargon.)"; "Who it's for (Speak directly to your ideal customer.)"; "What problems it solves"; "What your process looks like"; "What it costs (or a general price range) (If you're not ready to list prices, at least address the cost.)"; "How long it takes"; "What makes your approach unique"; "Common questions (Yes, add an FAQ section.)"; "Testimonials, before / after results, case studies, logos, reviews, etc."; "Call-to-action (Tell people what to do next. Book a call? Fill out a form? Don't leave them guessing.)" [book ch. 40]
- The book's real-world model ("air conditioner repair Hamilton"): the winners "had full-on pages specifically about air conditioner repair, optimized for the city I was in," with "Average wait times (helpful during a heatwave)," "Hourly rates and pricing info," "Common AC problems (like freezing units)," "DIY tips to try before calling," "Glowing customer reviews," "What to expect when the technician arrives," "Prep instructions and safety protocols." Closing rule: "So be specific. Be helpful. Be thorough. Your service page should answer every question a potential customer might have, before they ever pick up the phone or fill out a form. Because when someone is in need, the best page wins. Every time." [book ch. 40]
- The human checklist Matt uses before publishing: "Somebody comes to this page and they want to hire you or they want to buy your product... Probably price, how long does it take, or how long does it take to ship... what are applications that people can use this product in? What is it good for? What are the ingredients?" Al's Bin Rentals (his first client, ten-plus years old, still ranking) is the template in miniature: what we take, what we don't take, bin sizes, an "order disposal bin" button, phone number, rear doors, driveway friendly, flatbed services, Visa/MasterCard/cash: "What other information do you need to know?" Old-fashioned but "it's all user experience." The original brief for that client: "pictures of our bins and trucks. Oh, and diagrams showing the dimensions of each bin. And the types of things people can and can't put in them." [calls 117, 105; book Part I Prologue]
- Model pages on heytony.ca: the service page "explains the process, how it works, how you can hire me, what we can do for you... this is how we do it. You're going to call us, we're going to speak with you"; the website design page is "Website design that elevates your brand," a "get a free quote" button that brings them to the form, then "here's how we do website design... We're not educating, they're past the education point"; the local SEO page is "all about local SEO, how it works, you know, some case studies and stuff like that. And then, you know, we have FAQs on this page as well." The white fillings dental page is the course's model: "all this content is mentioning white fillings. We're not talking about dental implants... they have Q&As, they have FAQs there. Book an appointment, that's fine." [calls 011, 070; course: anchor-text; course: every-page-1-main-topic]
- Hero rules from reviews: before scrolling the visitor must know "what the hell am I buying... how many people in the group? What kind of tour is it?" (Yellowstone tour page). Put the big question customers ask right at the top: a member did this and "they're starting to go to the page." Put the contact options at the top, not the bottom; on a hire-me page "have a call to action. Like, get a quote, hire, contact... have that right up here." A discount at the top ("35% off sign up right now") is fine "But this page starts with. There's not a lot of text... give me more information." [calls 084, 014, 105, 063, 109]
- Trust at the top: a factual statement opening the page ("78% of UK small businesses are online and 83% say their website is a huge part of how they run their business") beat a member's page; claims should have something behind them (press release, award, reviews). For health pages, E-A-T on every page: "oh, we have Dr. So and so on board and they have been doing this for X amount of years. And here's a quote from them related to this piece of content," credentials under the quote, links to the doctor's LinkedIn, research, talks; quotes must be relevant to that page. [calls 081, 031, 054]

Checklists and FAQs must be unique to that page, not repeated across service pages. [call 061]
- The proof sections competitors win with: sections for websites they've designed, what it looks like, payment plan (50% deposit), what's included, industries they've designed for, how it works, get in touch, what clients say, plus links to blog posts and a book-consultation button "every other section." Wedding businesses should carry "a highlight of the best work in that category" on the money page and also link to case study pages; do both. - Where reviews go: on every money page, not only a reviews page: "I would have it on every money page, I would have some reviews." Two or three per page, mixed so the same text does not repeat on every page, pasted manually as text (Google cannot read a screenshot), with a link to the Google Business Profile or other legitimate source; blocks can be H2, H3 or plain paragraphs; testimonials "relevant to that service"; location-specific testimonials only on the matching location page; a "read all the reviews here" button going to Google. [calls 021, 016, 074; playbook III 3.4]
- Process section: numbered steps of what happens next; Leanne's version is "What to expect during your treatment" as a couple of paragraphs with an image; the book's model had "What to expect when the technician arrives." [playbook III 3.3; call 079; book ch. 40]
- Pricing: "Tell them your prices... if they're price shopping, they're going to price shop. And if you don't have any prices on your website, you're getting left out entirely." Use "starting at" plus a tier breakdown when price depends on scope. List ranges even as a premium provider; the valid exception is a luxury provider whose buyer decides through an in-person or call experience. A sales page can carry the price with the booking link beside it ("it costs $175... book a call here" with the Calendly link) because that "is a better user experience for a real person" than a contact form; AI will not read the Calendly, the reason is the user. Include a price range when the audit shows competitors have one. Caveat from the HVAC call: "It doesn't have to have the price on it necessarily on the service page"; "contact us for a free quote" is enough. Name the pricing page "Pricing," not "Investment." [calls 051, 052, 024, 001; playbook VI 5.1; III 3.4]
- FAQ placement: each service page "could have their own FAQs," tied to that page's focus keyword, shown as plain question-and-answer text. A long-tail question a money page cannot target directly can be captured with an FAQ section on the service page where the exact question appears (migraine coach). Yellowstone: FAQ-style answers on the page ("Am I guaranteed to see a wolf..."). The FAQ section is "the best chance" to link from a service page to a blog post, and a FAQ answer can deep-link with a # anchor to the spot on the money page. Do not push FAQs to the very bottom under everything (Matt had to scroll four times on Melissa's page and "your FAQs are pushed to the bottom"). [calls 004, 074, 084, 082, 043, 081, 105, 092; service-page-master]

[calls 013, 105, 116, 022; playbook III 3.4]
- The bottom of the page: a final CTA on every page ("It's always very important to have a final CTA basically on every page"), and on service or location pages "an extra section that is just there to internal link to any of these other services," e.g. heading "Are you looking for other roofing services?" relisting every service with a one-sentence description and a link. [calls 016, 082; playbook III 3.3]
- Overview (parent) page structure, Leanne's template: focus keyword "massage therapy services"; the same hero as every other page (H1 plus short description, white text on the dark background); one section per service with the service name, short copy, a custom checkmark bullet list in a brand colour (bold the points people resonate with), an image, and a CTA button; alternate image left/right and change background colour so sections read as different; reuse the same section layout rather than redesign each; CTA section at the bottom. It "quickly outlines each service and links to the individual page" and gives the clean /services/ then /services/deep-tissue-massage structure "really easy for Google to understand your site." [calls 079, 114, 075]
- Child (individual) service page structure, Leanne's deep tissue massage template: H1 "Winnipeg Deep Tissue Massage" with a descriptive line under it; "Who is deep tissue massage for" with six bullets turned into three icon boxes (chronic muscle tension, neck and shoulder tightness, lower back pain, desk-work posture); "Benefits of deep tissue massage" as a checklist beside a photo; "What to expect during your treatment"; closing CTA "Book a deep tissue massage in Winnipeg" with a filled primary button and an outline secondary (phone). Her logo design page, top to bottom: hero with H1 plus one CTA button; H2 "where Winnipeg logo design meets storytelling"; H2 with three H3 services in a three-column grid with icons; H2 "why does logo design matter more than you think" (text left, image right); H2 process section with H3s (image left, text right); about me (text and photo); a centered-text section for visual variety; FAQ accordion; closing CTA. "My rule of thumb when I'm designing, every H2 tag should be its own section." Leanne's Drive holds wireframes for a homepage, a service overview page and an individual service page (no product page wireframe yet) plus prompts for both page types. [calls 079, 115, 067, 016]
- What does not belong on a money page: deep education (a ceramic coating page's warranty explanations and "educational" content moved to blog posts and internally linked: "If I want ceramic coating, I probably already know what ceramic coating is. I don't need to be educated on it"); the basics for newcomers on a sales page (write a blog post with the basics and link to it, "Keep the sales page a sales page"); an infographic and article-style copy ("people don't want to learn about anxiety... people are looking for help with anxiety"); a "recent posts" widget (makes the page look like a blog post); filler sections like "Why choose our services, get started today"; a "who we work with" list (give it its own page); the same blocks repeated on every page ("the people you work with," "what to expect in your first call," a references list, a "written by" block: "There's so much content that is duplicated on this page"; "on your own website you don't want to repeat the same words on every page"); generic brand copy an unrelated business could reuse verbatim ("every woman is uniquely beautiful," "and more"); "AI blocks" (Jasmine was told by Claude to add them: "we haven't found that that does anything, at least not yet... write for humans first"). [calls 066, 024, 070, 105, 061, 010, 004, 022; playbook III 2.5]
- Top of page for humans, lower for Google: "a lot of what you're going to be doing, especially further down the page, is going to be more optimized for Google versus the top of the page. However, what you do at the top still heavily matters." Parents do not read much, Google does. [call 072]
- Page type signals: a service page should have a featured image set (HeyTony's services page was missing one) and its Open Graph type should not say "article"; fixing it requires manual schema saying "this page is actually a service page." Schema can tell search engines "is it a service page, is it a blog post, is it an article, is it a product page" and carry service descriptions. [course: open-graph-issues; course: schema-markup; book ch. 22]
- Geotagging photos on service pages: "You don't need to, but you can." [call 066]

## Layout and design

- "Copy first, then layout; the message dictates the section structure." Leanne's version of the same rule, stricter: "My rule of thumb when I'm designing, every H2 tag should be its own section." [playbook III 3.3; call 115]
- White space is not decoration, it reads as a quality signal: a wall of text with no white space reads as "broken," and a bloated "who we work with" list should move to its own page rather than crowd the money page. [call 061] A Wix service page needed roughly 50px margins, left-aligned text with a left-aligned button under it, a three-column layout, and adjusted line-height and letter-spacing before it read as clean. [call 055] A destination page whose copy ran edge to edge with no margin at all needed the same fix. [call 088]
- Give every page its own visual identity, not one template with the city or service swapped in: when every service and location page on a moving company's site used "different picture. Three or four paragraphs. Recent projects," visitors could not tell which page they were on, "it's hard as a user to really disseminate what page I'm on." Breadcrumbs, by contrast, were praised for doing that job well. [call 109]
- Never let styling hide text from the crawler: a "Services and industries" heading sat behind a colored bar the same shade as the page background on one consulting site, and a breadcrumb bar that disappeared on scroll hid text on another; both read as "very early days of SEO." The same objection applies beyond FAQ accordions, a "read more" dropdown burying ordinary service copy gets it too: "if this information is so important, why is it hidden in a box?" [calls 103, 109, 116]
- Icon-box sections (the audience-identifier layout already covered in the previous section) should not run generic stock icons: Leanne's set was bought as a stock icon pack and recolored to the brand's own color before use. [call 079]
- Landing and ad pages can run a flatter heading structure than a core money page; a core page carries the full tier, "for your core pages here, you would want to have your H1, your H2 and then your H2 [H3]." [call 016]
- On page builders without native anchor scrolling (Elementor, Divi, Showit, Squarespace), give a section a CSS ID and point a CTA button at it (like #contact) so it scrolls the visitor down the same page instead of loading a new one. [playbook IV Web Design]
- Long service lists belong in a mega menu, not a flat dropdown: keep the core few services visible in primary nav, push the long tail into an expandable menu with every item still linking to its own page, and collapse a long list of per-condition or per-item nav links into one "Services" hub that fans out from there. [playbook IV Web Design]

## Visuals

- Image density: roughly one image every 200 to 300 words, a minimum of 4 per page, ideally one per section, and every page needs at least a featured image. [service-page-master]
- Sourcing priority: your own real photos and screenshots first, AI-generated second, stock last, "everyone is a little on high alert on the internet, nervous about scams." The one flip: for charts and infographics specifically, AI-generated goes to the top of the list, because real, unfakeable artifacts (dashboard screenshots, client-site frames, annotated metrics) are the strongest imagery a service business has, no competitor can post the same one. [service-page-master]
- Photos should be specific to what they show, not one generic hero photo standing in for a whole catalog: a dumpster rental site made its pictures "very specific to each item," one photo per bin size. [call 096] The opposite failure showed up on a hauling company's site, blurry and cropped dumpster photos against a competitor's crisp, cohesive drawings, with the fix suggested as generating a clean labeled graphic with AI rather than shipping a bad photo. [call 105]
- File names carry their own signal, rename the file to something keyword-descriptive before upload rather than leaving a camera or CMS default in place. Alt text should identify the medium and describe the image for real, keyword worked in on at most half the images and never the identical phrase repeated across all of them; a plain oversized image with no alt text at all is a common, easy-to-miss fault on an otherwise strong page. [service-page-master; call 115]
- Format: WebP or AVIF at roughly 70% quality holds the same look at a fraction of the file size; compress anything over about 1MB, lazy-load everything below the fold, keep the hero image eager so it does not delay the first paint. [service-page-master]
- Video: never self-host, embed via YouTube or Vimeo (both still get indexed), and Google indexes one video per page. Video is not required to rank, weigh an autoplay hero video's page-speed and mobile cost against what it actually buys: "is that video appealing to you, or to a person looking to hire you?" [service-page-master]
- Never an auto-rotating carousel or slider hero, and never an autoplaying hero video: most visitors never see slide two, every visitor downloads every slide regardless, and a carousel can hide the page's own H1 behind it on hover. Replace with one static hero built around real people, plus persistent text. [service-page-master]
- Trust visuals: a hero star-rating build, five gold stars matched to Google's own color plus "Rated 5 stars on Google" hyperlinked to the real review source, placed once prominently rather than repeated down the page. Replace a live Google Maps iframe with a static screenshot linked to the real listing, the embed is a speed cost nobody actually interacts with. [service-page-master]
- Typography: one heading font plus one plain body font, two fonts is safe, past three slows the page; Google Fonts is the default safe source. Body text never below 16px; headings live as real text, never a graphic or an image standing in for one, since neither Google nor an AI crawler can read baked-in image text. [service-page-master]
- Color: pick complementary colors for CTA contrast and keep one dominant, using the complement only as a small pop, never split 50/50. Self-check contrast by mentally stripping the color and viewing the page in grayscale, if the text is not clearly readable that way contrast is too low, a check that catches failures automated tools miss. Match color to audience (clean blues read trust and B2B, pastels read playful and kid-friendly, muted greens and beiges read organic, bold on dark reads gym or fitness, gold on black reads luxury), and use corner radius as a brand dial, rounder for friendlier categories like massage or baby photography, sharper for serious categories like cybersecurity or B2B. [service-page-master]
- Geotagging photos is optional either way, "you don't need to, but you can," and no source spells out what it buys you if you do. [calls 066, 070]

## Length, keyword density, and on-page SEO

- Title tag: 50 to 60 characters, ideally under 55 (email checklist: 60 max), keyword at the very front, brand name last or dropped. Google can override it with its own text regardless of what you set. [service-page-master; email 2025-07-03; call 118] Location belongs in a local money page's title: "Should title tags have our location on your money pages? Yes." [call 058] Worked example: "Web design in Hamilton. High performance web design by Hey Tony." [call 117]
- Meta description: about 120 to 155 characters (email checklist: 120 to 156), unique per page, written as a sales pitch. Keyword position matters more than presence, one roofing page buried its keyword at the very end and lost the benefit: "keywords, especially in your meta description, have much more weight to Google when they are earlier." [call 082]
- URL and slug: short, keyword included, clean parent-to-child nesting (`/services/website-design`). Keep the slug short even when the H1 is long, H1 "Bookkeeping services in St. Petersburg, Florida for small businesses," URL `/services/bookkeeping`. [playbook III 2.3] No year, no "near me." Change a slug only if the page is not ranking AND has no traffic AND no backlinks, all three at once, every change forces a re-index and one member who swapped her URL mid-Rank-Week lost indexing for weeks. [calls 062, 063] A stuffed, near-me-laden URL got replaced with a clean `/landscape-lighting-montreal`. [call 109] A dynamic search-results URL (anything with a "?" or "=" after the last slash) rarely ranks, build a static page at a clean URL with the same content instead. [call 110]
- H1: exactly one, first heading in DOM order, exact keyword, no brand name, live text, never an image. Write it the way a person would actually say it, not "gobbledygook": "romantic storytelling wedding photography in Toronto" became "Timeless wedding photography in Toronto." [playbook IV Heading Structure] "Might not be sexy, but it should detail exactly what you do." [call 117] Two live misses: an H1 that never named what was being sold ("Total Yellowstone three day") [call 084], and one stuffed with the keyword twice in a single line ("Beaverton Kids Art Camp creative stuff...") trimmed down to just the keyword. [call 072]
- H2 and H3: aim for one more H2 than the top-ranking competitor, 5 to 10 is normal, never balloon toward a competitor's 50. [service-page-master; call 110] Floor: one H1, at least 2 to 3 H2s, H3s are optional and rarely worth chasing. [calls 118, 125] H2s must support the H1's specific topic, never a generic value-prop line, and duplicate H2s are a real fault. [playbook III 2.4; call 119]
- Word count has no fixed target, match the top 3, specifically the lowest-ranked of the three, or add roughly 100 to 200 words, then trim if you overshoot. [playbook III 4.4] Verify competitor counts by eye, tools misreport them (one page tool-read at 1,300 words was 174 by hand). [playbook III 4.3] Real range across live reviews: Matt's own SEO and web-design pages held #1 for years on 600 to 800 words [playbook I 3]; a bookkeeping page needed only about 800 to 1,000 because competitors sat between 500 and 1,100 [call 049]; a homeschool-tutoring competitor ranked a six-word keyword on 200 to 400 words [call 070]; a ceramic-coating page went from 9,000 to 10,000 words down to 2,500 to 3,000 and was still "too long" against 1,100 to 2,500-word competitors [calls 066, 065]. The floor most audit tools flag (under 100 words) is outdated, real floor is 500-plus, agency target 1,000 to 1,500. [playbook III 4.6]
- Keyword placement: work the keyword into the H1, title, meta, first 100 words, at least one H2, an FAQ, and at least one image alt and file name. [playbook III 2.6] Density has no fixed target either, match competitors "plus one": Matt's own page hit 5.04% density (35 uses in 695 words) against competitors at 1.76% and 0.9%, and fell from #1 to #9, proof that outranking your own competitors on density reads as over-optimization. [calls 118, 119] Local and low-difficulty keywords often need only 2 to 3 mentions, not 10 to 12, a page held top 3 for "mechanic" on 3 uses while all 3 competitors used it zero times. [call 030] Multi-part local keywords get counted piece by piece, not as one phrase ("bookkeeping services" and "St. Petersburg" matched separately). [call 049] The email checklist's floor version: keyword in title, meta, H1, one H2, and the content at least twice, plus the URL if possible, "you'll be better off than 90% of websites out there." [email 2025-06-19]
- Readability: 7th to 8th grade, paragraphs of 2 to 3 sentences, varied sentence length. [service-page-master] Yoast readability should read green, "If Yoast is not available then don't worry about this." [email 2025-07-03]

## CTAs and conversion

- One primary CTA per page, solid, bold, single high-contrast color, any secondary CTA (call vs form) visually recedes: "If people have too many choices, they sometimes just choose not to make any choice." A repeated live fix is 5 competing CTAs trimmed to one. [service-page-master]
- Don't stop at one CTA top and one bottom, add 2 to 3 more spaced through the body so a convinced reader can act mid-page: "we're starting to put more CTAs through the content of the page... they can immediately go contact you." [call 043]
- Name the specific action, never a bare "Learn more," "Contact us," or "Book today." A roofing page's generic "contact us" got flagged for the same reason a crawler cannot infer a destination the way a person can: "never use generic call to action text... This should always be replaced with whatever you do" (e.g. "contact a Vancouver roofer"). [call 082] On a ceramic-coating page, "See packages and pricing" became "Book an appointment," routed straight to the pricing page. [call 066]
- Buttons should open in the same tab, not a new one, a UX rule rather than an SEO one. [call 043]
- Phone numbers and emails must be tap-to-call and clickable everywhere, anchored at the bottom of the screen for a mobile-heavy audience. [service-page-master]
- A short five-question tick-box questionnaire that gives away a free resource still counts as a real CTA: "As long as you're getting their contact information. That's a lead magnet. That's fantastic." [call 022]
- Traffic fine but form-fills low is a CTA problem first, button color and contrast before copy. [service-page-master] The CTA is itself a ranking signal in Matt's model: if visitors won't click or fill the form, "that sends a signal to Google that this person doesn't actually want that thing. Therefore, this other page should rank." Let a deep-pocketed competitor run the CTA-copy tests, then do something similar, never copy flat out. [call 024]

## Links, indexing, and where it sits in the site

- Internal linking is Matt's number-one lever: "The more internal links you build to a page, the more Google will understand this page is important." His own SEO service page climbed from page two, or position 13 depending on the telling, to number one purely on internal links, content untouched, over roughly three to four years. [service-page-master; calls 097, 086, 112]
- The two-way rule: every new post links out to 3 to 5 other posts plus at least one money page, then go back into 3 older, already-indexed posts and add a link from each INTO the new page, since Google already trusts those old pages this can get a new page indexed within hours. [service-page-master]
- From the money page's own side, cap outbound links at 2 to 3 essential resources and never treat them as a feature: "why would you want to send people off of it?" [call 014] The reverse ratio, from each blog post, is one link into the money page plus 2 to 3 into other blog posts, so the money page keeps looking like the important one Google should stop at. [call 087]
- Vary anchor text across links pointing at the same money page, descriptive and destination-naming beats generic: "learn more" and "click here" help neither Google nor the user. [service-page-master; call 030]
- External links: 1 to 2 credible links per page, never a direct competitor, point them at your own legitimacy profiles (Chamber of Commerce, Trustpilot, Yelp, Glassdoor) or at the source behind a claim. [service-page-master; call 010] Some ranking money pages carry zero external links and still rank fine, check what the competitor's equivalent page actually does before assuming you need one. [playbook III 2.12]
- Crawl depth: keep every money page within 3 clicks of the homepage, click depth "seems to matter a lot more for getting service pages, product pages ranking higher." [service-page-master; call 097] A page stuck at "crawled, currently not indexed" needs more internal links pointed at it, not a rewrite. [call 110]
- Structure: a two-tier IA, one overview or hub page (not pushed for ranking) linking down to individual child pages that do the SEO work, clean parent-to-child URL nesting. Keep the hub page even after it stops being the growth driver, keep optimizing it and adding links to it over time. [service-page-master; call 015]
- Make the on-page business mention or address a clickable link to the Google Business Profile CID link wherever it appears naturally. [calls 082, 039]
- Backlinks rarely land on service or product pages directly, "most websites don't want to link to your service pages," they land on blog posts and get passed inward via internal links; one relevant link at the exact page can outperform hundreds of sitewide backlinks. [call 101; playbook V 1.1]
- Register the site with Search Console and submit the sitemap, "Your sitemap is how Google efficiently discovers and indexes your pages." [email 2025-06-26]

## How to build or fix one (the workflows)

- Audit first, always: copy the money-page audit spreadsheet, pick a page, paste the URL into pageaudit.com, and read only three things off it, your keyword usage, word count, and keyword density against competitors, ignoring the overall percentage entirely. [service-page-master] Google the exact keyword in incognito, skip ads, Reddit, Yelp, and directories, take the next 3 genuine ranking business sites and run each through the same tool. [call 025] Run pagespeed.web.dev on your URL, mobile and desktop, but treat speed as a later lever: "gotta fix your pages first." [call 113] When a page returns all "NA" (usually bot-blocked), fall back to the "PA" Chrome extension's Copy All Headings. [playbook III 4.2] Money Page Doctor automates this first pass in about 10 seconds and flags "keyword mismatch," "content is thin," or a "you're already ranking #1" banner, always confirm where you actually rank before acting on a flagged gap. [playbook III 4.4]
- Benchmark method: never a fixed number, always the lowest-ranked of the top 3, not #1, "if #3 ranks with 500 words, aim for ~600, not 3,000 because #1 has 3,000." [playbook III 4.4] Verify competitor numbers by eye, tools misreport them often. The rule across word count, keyword count, heading count, internal links, and backlinks alike is "same level plus one," match, then edge past by a small margin, never blow past by multiples. [call 119] Once on-page is clean, go deeper: who ranks #1 to #3, their referring-domain count, how often and where they use the keyword, whether their content is simply better, how many internal links point at their page. [playbook III 4.3]
- Fix priority order: title tag, then meta description, then H1, keyword front-loaded in all three, about 5 to 10 minutes of work, then wait 1 to 2 weeks before touching on-page content further. [service-page-master; call 071] Judge a title, meta, or H1 nudge after roughly 2 weeks to a month, judge a content rewrite after 2 to 3 months, and give any newly published or edited page about a month before touching it again. [service-page-master]
- Rewrite discipline: the two verbatim rewrite prompts (tags, then full content) and the internal-linking prompt already live in `../service-page-master.md` section 7, and are repeated as Matt sends them in `heytony-email-tips.md` sections 3d through 3f, use those rather than re-deriving them here. Never trust the AI's self-reported word or keyword count, verify in wordcounter.net, the same workflow has delivered 1,700 words against a 2,200 target and 13 mentions against a 20-to-45 target. [service-page-master] Copy the output to a blank document, confirm the keyword is present and front-loaded, strip AI formatting and "fluff," and rewrite any H2 that does not sound like a person wrote it, then re-run the audit tool on the new draft before publishing. [service-page-master; email 2025-12-07]
- The 30-day plan: each week, publish one blog post linking to 2 to 3 other posts plus one money page, fix that week's money page's title tag and meta description, fix its H1, and add 3 internal links into it from older pages. Over 30 days that is 4 money pages, 4 blog posts, roughly 20 fixed audit issues. "If you only have one money page, add one. But you probably need more than one" [call 071]; with 46 product pages, "don't worry about it, just do four." [call 020]
- Order of operations from zero: fix money pages first, as much as possible, but don't let that block ongoing blog publishing, "I wouldn't let fixing the money pages stop me from writing blog content." [call 114] Content existing and complete beats every other lever, "you can't optimize what doesn't exist." [playbook III 1.5]

## When NOT to touch it

- The core freeze: never edit a page ranking #1 to #3, or ranking for anything at all, even a keyword you didn't intend, even when it breaks every other rule in this chapter. "Don't edit any pages that are ranking, for the love of God." [playbook I 3, III 1.2, VI 4.1]
- Evidence Matt left alone on purpose: a probate page ranking #1 using "probate" 122 times with two duplicate H1s and hidden overlay text [playbook III 1.2; call 124]; a page ranking #2 on zero backlinks [playbook III 1.2]; a lithium-battery site ranking #1 to #2 for unrelated golf-course keywords, "don't touch those pages unless you're fine with losing that traffic" [call 065]; a Shopify page whose ranking text doesn't match brand guidelines, "I don't care if it doesn't match your brand guidelines." [call 069]
- The only carve-out is UX or CTA polish, one button, one sentence, a layout reflow, never a content or structural rewrite, and only if the page ranks but converts at zero. Wait for an actual rank drop before touching anything larger. [service-page-master; playbook III 1.2]
- "Set and forget" has an expiration date: a page can hold #1 for years on thin content, then lose it once competitors escalate, that drop, not before, is the trigger to redo the audit. [playbook I 3, III 1.2]
- If a page and a blog post both already rank for the same keyword and both convert, leave both alone. [playbook II 3.3, III 2.13]
- Not-yet-ranking pages get patience, not surgery: wait 3 months, then 6, keep internal-linking meanwhile, small edits only in between, "add a couple paragraphs or add like a couple FAQs. Don't make like a crazy, crazy switch." [call 022]
- Redesign scope follows ranking status: strong existing rankings mean preserve verbiage, keywords, and URLs and restructure visually only; weak or no rankings mean free to rewrite from scratch. Never strip the exact keyword a page already ranks for out of the title while "improving" it. [playbook III 2.1, 3.6]
- Not ranking is not the same as not working: a $5,000 to $6,000 Yellowstone tour page sells by phone, "just because this page isn't ranking doesn't mean it's not good." [call 084]

## Don'ts

- Don't edit a page already ranking #1 to #3, or ranking at all, even weakly on page 3. [service-page-master]
- Don't use an auto-rotating carousel or slider hero, and don't autoplay a hero video. [service-page-master]
- Don't hide FAQ or ordinary service copy behind an accordion, toggle, or "read more" dropdown, "why is it hidden in a box?" [service-page-master; call 116]
- Don't run multiple competing primary CTAs, and don't ship a bare "Learn more." [service-page-master]
- Don't put "near me" or a year in a URL or on-page copy. [service-page-master]
- Don't use a graphic or a styled div as the H1, it must be live text. [service-page-master]
- Don't hyperlink a direct competitor, ever, not even to "feature" them. [service-page-master; call 007]
- Don't use stock-looking imagery on a page meant to convey a real team. [service-page-master]
- Don't paste reviews as screenshots, Google cannot read image text, paste real text instead. [service-page-master]
- Don't chase 100% on an audit tool, or fix a page just because it trails #1 on one metric. [service-page-master]
- Don't trust an AI's self-reported word or keyword count, or its SEO opinions over the real competitor data, "ChatGPT will bullshit you." [service-page-master; playbook III 4.4]
- Don't copy a competitor's ranking "trick" (stuffing, hidden text, duplicate H1s) on the assumption it works, it may be ranking despite the violation, not because of it. [service-page-master]
- Don't keyword-stuff, density above your competitors reads as over-optimization, not effort. [service-page-master]
- Don't hide page text behind a colored bar, a collapsed menu, or a breadcrumb that vanishes on scroll, "that's like very early days of SEO." [calls 103, 109]
- Don't build a placeholder blog post today meaning to 301-redirect it to a real service page later, "Why are you doing extra work just to redirect later? That's going to confuse Google." [call 065]
- Don't invent a service-area or industry page nobody searches for, "I don't create a page on my website for SEO unless I know people are looking for it." [call 105]
- Don't keep a service page live for something you no longer offer, a caller who hangs up on a bait-and-switch "is going to hurt your search results." [calls 117, 119]
- Don't let a live reviews-widget plugin quietly inflate your real word count, Google only credits visible text and the widget can slow the page for nothing in return. [call 074]
- Don't change a ranking page's URL unless it fails all three tests at once, not ranking, no traffic, no backlinks. [service-page-master; calls 062, 063]

## Where the sources are thin or disagree

- Word count floors conflict with the calls' own live rulings: master and playbook both give a "real floor of 500-plus, agency target 1,000 to 1,500," yet live examples repeatedly rank far below that when competitors do (a homeschool-tutoring page at 200 to 400 words, Matt's own SEO and web-design pages at 600 to 800 for years). Treat the 500 to 1,500 range as a starting guess to verify against the actual SERP, never a rule on its own. [call 070; playbook I 3, III 4.6]
- FAQ schema is covered at length in `../service-page-master.md` (deprioritized, Google deprecated FAQ rich results in May 2026) but never comes up once across the calls or emails read for this chapter, which treat FAQs purely as visible on-page content. Not a contradiction, just a topic the coaching calls never touch directly. [service-page-master]
- Geotagging photos is mentioned twice, both times as fully optional, "you don't need to, but you can," with no source ever explaining what it would actually help with if you did. [calls 066, 070]
- Whether keyword stuffing draws a real penalty is unresolved: one thread says it usually doesn't, "they just won't index it or they won't rank it," and a deliberately stuffed test page "worked for a bit" before falling off, while the standing safety argument elsewhere is reputational and UX-based rather than a named penalty. Treat stuffing as a rankings and UX risk, not a guaranteed manual penalty, since no source claims Google issues one. [call 066]
- URL-change guidance has a stricter default (change only if not ranking AND no traffic AND no backlinks, all three) and one looser live comment on a specific page with no backlinks yet ("you'd be fine to change a URL and just do a redirect"). Use the three-part test as the standing rule, the looser comment reads as Matt reasoning through one specific case rather than a revision to it. [service-page-master; call 105]
