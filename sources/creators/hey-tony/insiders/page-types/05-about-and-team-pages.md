# About / team pages

*Part of the HeyTony page-type research (`page-types/`). The rules every page inherits are in `00-rules-for-every-page.md`; the citation key, sources, and file map are in `README.md`.*

In Matt's system the About page is a trust page, not a ranking page. It carries no dedicated keyword (it overlaps the homepage), it is not expected to rank for anything beyond your own name, and Matt himself does not have one (his bio sits on his homepage). Its job is to prove that a real person runs a real business, so visitors who arrive from money pages and blog posts feel safe enough to contact you. Team, provider, and author pages are folded in here because Matt treats them the same way: E-E-A-T support, not keyword targets.

## What it is for and what it targets

- The page is about the visitor's trust, not about you. Leanne: "when you're creating your about page, it isn't about us, but it's about gaining the trust of our customers... they're really looking to see if you're a real person, can they help you with what they're looking for, and if this is a good fit... it's really about like a trust page. And when people trust you, people will shop with you." The playbook condenses it to "a trust page, not a life story." [call 089; playbook IV Web Design]
- It is not a ranking target: "Your about page is never going to rank and your contact page is only going to rank if somebody says business name, contact or call... So don't worry about optimizing those pages." Jorg's framing on the same call: the about page is for trust, "you can go a little bit more maybe private about yourself"; Matt: "even if you have an about page, it doesn't mean that page needs to rank." [call 052]
- "No keyword needed" rule: Matt "I don't care if an about page ranks or not"; it needs no dedicated keyword because it overlaps the homepage. The about page is "a little bit more about me" and does not compete with the homepage for keywords. The playbook lists About with the homepage, contact, and blog category pages as pages that are not keyword-targeted, not expected to independently rank, and whose SEO gaps usually do not matter. [calls 088, 021; playbook III 3.2]
- Matt has no about page at all: "I put my bio basically on my homepage." Marisa designs hers in Canva then rebuilds it on the site and is "not really trying to rank for our contact page or for our about page." [calls 052, 021]
- If your about page happens to rank (Ariel's did, because it uses her keyword and has a lot of information): "don't mess with that." [call 052]
- A thin about page cannot rank for anything and competes with your homepage for your own name: "there doesn't look like to be enough content on here for this to rank for anything. And because of the nature of it, this would be competing with your homepage for your name." [call 097]
- It is a standard page in Leanne's default service-site page set (About alongside Services, Locations, Blog and Contact), and a member added one as a normal part of finishing her comedian site (menu: Home, About, Contact me, Buy my book, Services, Locations). A homepage plus an about page ("a couple of pages") is enough to submit the sitemap to Google on a new site. [calls 036, 019, 014, 066]
- "Meet the founder" sections on funnel landing pages (Leanne): "good for like service based businesses... if you have like a product, you don't necessarily need to have meet the founder type thing, but if you're doing a course or something like that, then this is important." [call 016]
- Team / provider pages: for a mental health practice with about 100 independent-contractor providers, "I wouldn't even try to optimize for those pages because unless people are looking them up specifically, their website is probably going to come up first. Right. Or their Google business profile." The only keyword such a page can own is the person's name plus "dash therapist" or similar; put the SEO effort into informational searches instead. [call 019]
- Directory expert profiles (profile title "Renata Clark" plus one specialism such as "identity architecture researcher" or "whatever coach"): "unless these people are like highly searched and they're not doing their own SEO, I don't think this is a good strategy in terms of getting new people to your site." Their value is that the experts can backlink to their own profile. [call 017]
- Health niche: sub-pages per team member (doctor, licensed therapist) are an E-A-T idea, but more important is quoting them with their credentials on every relevant page and linking to their LinkedIn or studies. [call 054]
- Author pages: blog posts should show the author with a link to an author page that "has your social media links, that has any certifications... any associations that you're a part of... anywhere that you've won awards"; this gives authority with Google by tying the site to your profiles. Matt on his own: "my author page isn't good, I need to fix it up." His unfinished author page ("just says Matt D... not formatted properly") was still getting 167 clicks; "I should probably fix that." [calls 038, 013]
- "Written by this person" / "clinically reviewed by" author boxes on health content: "I don't know if that really matters all that much. Like sure, if there's extra credentials and you could do that. Doesn't take you much more time." [call 005]
- In the topic-gap analysis, author pages are "junk" to ignore, alongside tag pages, pagination, /cart/ and /privacy-policy/. No email covers about or team pages. [email 2026-08-04]
- The site name (the brand Google shows) should be the business name, not the CEO's name; a developer had set it to the CEO. [call 013]
- The curriculum and the book have nothing specific on building an About or Team page; the book only lists /about/, /team/, /careers/, /press/ among normal subdirectories. [course: universal per-page rules; book ch. 6]

## What goes on it, top to bottom (the structure)

- Leanne's About-page build (Figma mock, Winnipeg massage therapist), in order: (1) an H1 hero band, "About your Winnipeg massage therapist", descriptive, keyword plus location, with one short supporting line (she cut the paragraph down to just "my approach to massage therapy"); (2) a centred H2 trust line, "Helping Winnipeg clients feel better with their bodies", better as "...with massage therapy" to hit the keyword; (3) a half-width, left-aligned text block plus an image ("a professional, comfortable, consent based massage therapy experience", which answers a real worry); (4) a reversed image/text block, "why I became a massage therapist in Winnipeg", which "humanizes you that like, hey, you are a real person. You're not just a stock photo template"; (5) a final CTA block with a button. [call 089]
- H1 line breaks: break the H1 where it reads naturally ("About your Winnipeg / massage therapist") but use a <br> inside one element, never two text elements, or you get two H1 tags; GoHighLevel users can force the break with container padding instead. "We don't need to be too showy about our H1 tag. We want it to be descriptive so people can understand what this page is about quickly. And also it helps Google understand." [call 089]
- The founder story is the core block: Leanne's sales-page advice (Maritza's book) was to tell your own story, "I've been there. I've been in your shoes. I've been nervous. And this is kind of like what I've learned as a stand up comic." Clients want to hear their own voice and personality in their content ("the first time that we hear our voice finally"); a homepage story builds emotional connection. [calls 028, 013, 016]
- A "my letter to you" style personal section gets a photo of yourself next to it, "showing who you are." [call 049]
- The winning cat-behavior competitor had certifications at the top of the page, "her story" as a section, and featured-in logos linked to the external sources. [call 034]
- Anya (experiential travel, Africa): move the founder photo and "about me" higher on the page, call it "meet Anya", add videos of her and of guests in the destinations: "show me that you're a real person... make me feel safe... your industry... people are spending a lot of money with you and they're worried about their safety." [call 088]
- An "Our clients" section listing big-name companies is a trust asset ("You've worked with some massive companies. I love that for you."); set the company names in a brand-like font when the logos cannot legally be used. [call 103]
- A behind-the-scenes section is good; also show finished products and more testimonials. [call 105]
- If you have a published book, keep it on the same website on its own page, link to it, link to the Amazon listing, and "really push the book... does a lot for SEO in terms of authority and being qualified as an expert". [calls 068, 067]
- Diana "reorganized the about page" after asking whether toggles belong there; no further detail was recorded. [call 032]
- The About section inside the homepage (the about job when there is no about page): an About Us section sits right below the hero, "introducing ourselves to them, explaining what we do, how we do it, who we serve"; Leanne's wireframe places "introducing ourselves" and "trust building elements" near the top, and the "introduce ourselves" H2 section does the about job (who we are, who we work with). The playbook version: what you do, how, who you serve, with an audience-identifier layout (icon boxes per persona) so visitors self-identify, plus its own CTA. Leanne's homepage About section is an H2 that includes the focus keyword, icons of the ideal audience, a CTA, then testimonials. [calls 028, 008, 079, 067; playbook III 3.3; playbook IV Web Design]
- Rename generic "About us" headings to carry the brand or keyword: "About Pets Gone Wild"; "Just more instances to put those keywords into the page." An "about her" H2 on an artist homepage is fine alongside keyword H2s; a comedian homepage includes "about you / about me" content. [calls 068, 106]
- The homepage about blurb must link deeper: "you do need to describe to people who you are. I would encourage you to have a button down here that goes to a page, that goes to the about page." The homepage carries the short blurb, the about page holds the detail; Marita's about section gets a "read more about" button. [calls 050, 055, 058]
- A long About block on a homepage should be broken into bullet points. [call 045]
- On a service page, Leanne adds an "about me" section partway down: text on one side, a real photo of herself on the other, alt text "Winnipeg logo designer Leanne Jones." A brand-story section on a product page is fine as is (it had been mistaken for testimonials). [calls 115, 097]
- A homepage that is essentially "about us and like short story" with few keyword-specific sentences is not a money page; money pages must be built separately. A realtor whose about section lives on the homepage treats that as his "go to page or sell page"; Elliot verified its indexing by URL inspection; no separate about page existed. [calls 116, 047]
- If the homepage and about page show what services you offer, visitors arriving from blog posts understand you are, for example, a sex therapist and are more likely to contact you. [call 074]

## Layout and design

- Keep the same colours and type styles as the homepage: "we don't want it to look like people are landing on a completely different website." Use the same H1 font and size on interior pages as on the homepage H1. The playbook: keep the exact same palette as the rest of the site. [call 089; playbook IV Web Design]
- At least one visual element per section. [call 089; playbook IV Web Design]
- Alternate the blocks: a half-width left-aligned text block with an image, then a reversed image/text block. Never right-align body text; minimize centered text. [call 089; playbook IV Web Design]
- Soften pure black text on pure white for long About copy. [call 045]

## Visuals

- Use a real photo of the practitioner or founder, not stock, when it is a real business; the photo is what "humanizes" the page ("you are a real person. You're not just a stock photo template"). Real photos of the actual team build more trust than stock or AI. [call 089; playbook IV Web Design; playbook IV Conversion elements]
- The photo must be sharp: a clear photo of the owner builds trust; a blurry headshot was the first thing Matt flagged on the cat-behavior site. [call 034]
- Real photos of yourselves and of you working belong throughout the site; photos of the owner and the shop on a money page are a unique advantage: "I love that there's pictures of you here, your shop. Like, nobody else can post that." Owners' and studio photos read as "this is them. This is actually them." [calls 060, 066, 100]
- Replace over-used stock humans: "I've seen this photo of this woman from unsplash a billion times. So replace that with a photo of you." Real photos "make you look more authentic and real"; "even using photos that you take from your phone are better than most stock photos because it really showcases who you are as a business." [calls 113, 111, 115]
- The book agrees: "Photographs are a great way to show that you're a real person behind a real business. Nothing builds trust like images of you, your team, your office, or your happy customers. Photos don't always need to be professionally taken; your phone camera can do the job just fine." Avoid the generic stock "same smiling woman in a white coat" every dentist uses; "use your own photos whenever possible." [book ch. 12]
- Putting your own name in image descriptions and captions on the about page is fine: "you're showing that you're a real person". [call 088]
- Video: the direction is "more authenticity in 2026... people don't want a faceless company"; show "Jim's on a roof today" style videos and real photos of the owner and crew. 30-second real clips of an actual team member on site beat faceless content; AI avatars kill trust (about 12 views capped versus about 1,000 to 2,000 once the real person appeared). [call 095; playbook II 4.6; playbook V 5]
- Headshot attachment pages: provider headshots in WordPress create their own attachment pages; disable them via Yoast > Advanced > Media pages (turn off "enable media pages" so attachment URLs redirect to the media itself). [call 013]

## Length, keyword density, and on-page SEO

- No word count is given anywhere for an about page. The only length guidance: Leanne's finished mock was keyword-correct ("massage therapist, Winnipeg massage therapist all in our headline tags") but "the only differences I would make to make this more SEO friendly would be to expand on this copy, it's pretty light. I would have more copy in here." [call 089]
- If you do put a keyword on it, Leanne's build uses keyword plus location in the H1 and H2 ("About your Winnipeg massage therapist"); Matt's rule is that no dedicated keyword is required. [calls 089, 088]
- E-E-A-T self-audit: after editing, paste the page to Claude or ChatGPT and ask it to score the page on experience, authority and trust and suggest specific improvements; "no one knows the industry like you do," show experience and expertise. [call 039; playbook II 4.3; playbook VI 1.7]
- LLMs want to see you as an expert across many places (guest posts, podcasts, mentions), and expertise and experience should come through in every piece of content. Articles written about you elsewhere should link to your site (unlinked mentions feed E-A-T, Jeremiah the retired dentist turned gutter contractor). Leanne's own about page is where her directory listing / backlink example lives. [calls 024, 054, 055]
- The book's E-E-A-T stance: "it's my personal stories, case studies, and real-world experience that make it unique and genuinely helpful. These elements not only make the content more engaging but also build trust, showing that I've actually lived what I'm teaching." [book ch. 2]
- The curriculum gives no about-page rules; only the universal rules apply (one H1 matching the page topic, title tag and meta description present, a featured image, no dead ends, and its general link minimums, see the disagreement note below). [course: universal per-page rules]

## CTAs and conversion

- End the page with a CTA block and button (Leanne's final section). [call 089]
- The homepage About section carries its own CTA; the homepage blurb's button is "read more about" into the about page. [call 067; calls 050, 055, 058; playbook III 3.3]
- Trust elements double as conversion elements: certifications at the top, featured-in logos, big-name client lists, behind-the-scenes, testimonials, and (for high-ticket or safety-sensitive services like Anya's travel business) videos of you and your customers. [calls 034, 103, 105, 088]

## Links, indexing, and where it sits in the site

- About and Contact pages are exempt from the external-linking minimum and from the one-external plus three-to-five-internal link rule: "Your contact page or your about page doesn't necessarily need" it. [playbook III 2.12; call 112]
- The about page is one of the pages a blog post can link to so the site is not linear: "this blog post can link to the about page, to the main blog page, to the services page". [call 073]
- Navigation: "About" can hold secondary items to shorten the menu (career / hiring link underneath About; blog under about or about under blog). Put employment / careers under an About dropdown rather than the top nav "unless you're really trying to push people applying." A med spa's "Meet the Team, partnerships, collabs, careers, policies" lived under an About hover menu; "most of that stuff, I would say can be in the footer" but keep the About item. The playbook: move non-essential nav items (store, careers, policies) into the footer or an About dropdown. [calls 070, 066, 096, 058; playbook IV Web Design]
- Do not nest blog posts under About: "about-us/news/the-roof-replacement-process" should be flattened; blog posts are "just individual pages" at the root. [call 101]
- Area-served pages hidden under About Us (Scott) was part of why service intent was not being met. [call 109]
- Consolidate cluttered navs: John's SmugMug photography nav had "purchasing, about the artist, contact, Instagram, Facebook"; keep only the top destinations. [call 008]
- Readable URLs: Angela's live site had "meet Angela or about us" pages with readable URLs; her temporary WordPress build showed code-number URLs instead, and "anybody should be able to tell what the page is about just by the URL." Alex's about page lives at a "meet the team" slug; an audit tool flagged it as broken though it loads (sent to Matt's developer). [calls 124, 088]

## Don'ts

- Don't optimize the about page for your main keyword or worry about whether it ranks. [calls 052, 088, 021]
- Don't make it a life story; it is a trust page. [playbook IV Web Design]
- Don't use a blurry headshot, over-used stock humans, or AI avatars. [calls 034, 113; playbook II 4.6]
- Don't let it look like a different website (different palette or H1 styling). [call 089]
- Don't create two H1s by splitting the heading into two text elements. [call 089]
- Don't repeat the same "people you work with" / "written by" blocks on every page (duplicate text), and don't put the author box at the top of a blog post: "Why do we care that this person wrote this post? Like, is that really that important right at the top?" Move it to the bottom and use the top real estate for the CTA. [calls 010, 081]
- Don't set the site name to the CEO's name. [call 013]
- Don't nest blog posts or area-served pages under About. [calls 101, 109]
- Don't try to rank roster pages for 100 contractors or directory expert profiles. [calls 019, 017]
- Don't touch an about page that is already ranking. [call 052]

## Where the sources are thin or disagree

- The curriculum, the book, and the emails have nothing on building an About or Team page; the playbook covers only palette, visuals, alignment, nav placement, and the "trust page" framing, and says so explicitly. Only one full build exists (Leanne, call 089). No title tag, meta description, word count, or image count is specified for an about page. [course: universal per-page rules; book ch. 6; playbook IV Web Design]
- Link minimums conflict: the curriculum's universal rules (at least one external link, 3 to 5 internal links) versus the playbook and call 112, which exempt About and Contact from those minimums. [course: universal per-page rules; playbook III 2.12; call 112]
- Keyword on the about page: Matt says no dedicated keyword is needed and he does not care whether it ranks; Leanne's build deliberately puts keyword plus location in the H1 and H2, and call 068 says to rename "About us" headings to carry the brand or keyword. Both positions are kept. [calls 088, 089, 068]
- Author pages and author boxes: call 038 says an author page with socials, certifications, associations and awards gives authority with Google; call 005 says "clinically reviewed by" boxes probably do not matter much; the email gap analysis classes author pages as "junk"; the playbook's trend note ("Who wrote this?") leans toward authorship mattering more. [calls 038, 005; email 2026-08-04; playbook II 4.6]
- Team pages: call 019 says do not optimize provider pages; call 054 floats per-member sub-pages as an E-A-T idea while ranking on-page credential quotes higher. [calls 019, 054]
- Diana's question about whether toggles belong on an about page was raised but the answer was not recorded. [call 032]
