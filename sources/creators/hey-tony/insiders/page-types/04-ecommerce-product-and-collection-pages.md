# Ecommerce pages: product pages and collection / category pages

*Part of the HeyTony page-type research (`page-types/`). The rules every page inherits are in `00-rules-for-every-page.md`; the citation key, sources, and file map are in `README.md`.*

Matt's ecommerce split: stop trying to rank the store homepage for one keyword; give each featured product a clean homepage section with descriptive text, and push the ranking work onto collection and product pages. The collection page takes the broad, competitive keyword ("paddle T-shirts", "tennis t-shirt"); the product page takes the specific, attribute-based one ("polyester paddle shirt", "18-karat gold engagement ring"). "Collection page is a money page. Product page is a money page," and Matt's emails treat product and collection pages "identically to service pages in every prompt." The recurring diagnosis is that collection pages are "the biggest missed opportunity when I look at a Shopify store" and "one of the most underutilized types of page when it comes to E commerce and ranking"; the book says the same: "Category pages are easily one of the most underutilized and under-optimized assets on most online stores ... Category pages can rank. And not just rank, they can rank really well." Terminology note: "a category page is WordPress. It's for blog posts... A collection page is on Shopify. That can be a collection of different products... You can rank, and you should rank your collection page, but not your category pages." [playbook VI 3.1; calls 041, 022, 116, 017, 001; email 2025-12-08; book ch. 39]

## Product pages: what goes on them, top to bottom

- Name the product what people search, and make that the H1 and title. Book rule 1, "Unique Product Names Are NOT Always Beneficial": "Have you ever heard of the Florpavia couch? No? Exactly. Neither have your potential customers." "The product title is usually the H1 tag and the title tag on your page. Both are essential for SEO. If your H1 says 'Florpavia' instead of 'Mid-Century Modern Sofa,' you're losing the game before it even starts." "So, yeah, maybe save the fancy names for your packaging. On your site? Call it what it is." The proposed fix for client John was to move the creative name "underneath in the description or subtitle"; he refused and cancelled six months later. [book ch. 38]
- The naming cases: a "cream beaver fur hat" under a made-up name became "beaver fur top hat" / "white beaver fur felt hat" (260/mo Canada, 880/mo US, easy); Irish-named candles became "Vanilla soy candle blend - [Irish name]" (~30/mo), named by the forward note, called a blend, other notes in the description, never four scent notes in the name; a generic "novelty bongs" listing became "octopus bong" and the store renamed across the catalogue; a heart-shaped vibrator is named "heart shaped vibrator" ("And then you can have like the actual name, whatever it is"); goat milk treats became "Goat milk cookie for dogs" with the flavour in brackets ("peanut butter flavored") and a statement of who it is for (dogs, cats), which the pages never said; "moisturizing lotion for face" "is what your product is now going to be called"; "horse mug" (1,700 searches, 2% difficulty) beats "equestrian"; "funny apres ski T shirts" not "1027" (an inside joke nobody searches); "single Mother's Day e-card"; worship flags: "whatever the biggest keyword is, that is the easiest rank for, that is the title of it. That's your heading", in the actual Shopify product title, not an alt title; Nikki's H1 "Stink File #1 scratch and sniff sticker bundle" became "Scratch and sniff sticker bundle - Stink File #1" (keyword first, brand convention second "because that's not important for SEO"). Put the made-up name after a dash, and get explicit client sign-off before overriding a preferred name. [calls 012, 013, 084, 118, 125, 078, 066, 103, 074, 119; playbook II 1.5, VI 3.3]
- "Always follow the keyword research and never guess. Never," even against your attachment to a name; "your job is not to train people ... use the terms that people actually use": a UK "yoga rug" seller puts "organic cotton yoga mat" in the H1 and can say "rug" in the description, and copies competitors' naming because "they've done the keyword research." [playbook II 1.5; call 019]
- Long-tail product naming when the head term is too hard: "navy leather ballet flats" with the keyword at the front of the product name and the shoe's own name at the back; ranking #1 for "pewter woven leather loafer" is worthless if no one searches it. Never name products with city names. [calls 058, 012]
- One product, one page: don't carry two names (woodchip boiler / biomass boiler) on one page, pick one and put the comparison in a blog; for synonyms (balcony / demi / half-cup bras) research which term people search, pick the winner, and optionally one page explaining "type one vs type two vs type three", not a page per synonym. [calls 019, 053]
- Novel product nobody searches: don't force a keyword nobody Googles; build content around the underlying problem ("nobody buys my book, what do I do"), write "[established competitor product] alternative / alternatives" pages listing your products, or "old thing versus new thing" comparisons and a "why I built blank" post; a competitor brand name works as a secondary keyword in body copy ("Compared to Suavecito pomade, our pomade is..."), never the primary. [playbook II 1.5, II 2.6, VI 3.3; calls 013, 048, 076]
- Above the fold, clean and conversion-focused: "Product photo(s); Product name (with the keyword!); Star rating (if you've got 'em); Price; 'Add to Cart' button", modelled on Amazon ("product name, star rating, price, and the 'Buy Now' button. Just below that is the About This Item and Product Details section"). Matt's wireframe: "pictures on the left, you have a short description with an add to the cart and like all that stuff on the right. And then you're gonna have a little bit of information underneath that. Underneath there you'll probably have reviews and then related products." Natalie's supplement page passed with "add to cart's nice and big" and subscribe-and-save present. [book ch. 38; calls 117, 041]
- Short description and bullets: "people just want to know, what the hell do you sell? What does this thing do right? What features do I get? Like, they want bullet points, they want easy to understand" (said of an ad copywriter's descriptions that were "overwritten in terms of poetry, but not well enough in terms of SEO"). Punchy copy that never repeats "beard" or "hair" will not rank; the category money keyword ("hair pomade", "best hair pomade for guys") appears a few times on the product page. [calls 012, 076]
- The description itself: book rule 2, "Product Descriptions Don't Have to Be Dull". The two mistakes: "Copy-pasting from the manufacturer. Google hates duplicate content. If your product description appears word-for-word on 50 other sites, guess what? You're not going to rank" and "Treating product descriptions like an afterthought. Throwing something together to fill the white space." The process: "Find the keyword for your product. Analyze your competitors. Write a helpful, original description that makes you the best result on the page." "Treat your product page like a blog post (sort of)": H2s for sections, internal links to related products or helpful blogs, FAQs, "Your own expertise and opinion." [book ch. 38]
- Below the fold: "Everything else can go underneath that. Most visitors may not scroll all the way down, but the ones who do are likely on the fence. Your helpful, well-structured content down there could be the thing that gets them to buy. And for search engines? All that additional context, keyword usage, and structure gives you a huge leg up." Extra SEO text sits under reviews and related products ("add it underneath reviews") or in a details box; text below "all of the important information that people need to buy, check out, look at the product" is fine. [book ch. 38; call 117]
- Headings: Shopify product pages usually have no place for extra headings, so add an FAQ block (theme block "collapsible row" / "collapsible content"), set the rows to heading types, and put keywords in them; or add an H2 inside the description that mirrors the title ("Series one bundle includes"), "it's fine to make your heading two tag the same as your heading one tag in some cases" (competitors' only H2s were "vendor unknown, customer reviews, quick links"). Natalie's page had 10 H2s with the keyword in only one: add it to one more ("Why is our 5-HTP plus formula different?" instead of "Why buy from us"), make related products H3 not H2, make "Directions" an H3 phrased with the keyword ("Our 5-HTP plus is formulated to exclude..."). [calls 041, 125]
- FAQs on product pages: yes, "as long as you can have different FAQs for each product"; identical care/cleaning/storage FAQs across products are a problem ("you don't want the same FAQs on every single page"). Write product-specific ones: "what carat is this ring? ... if you had that ring out and a customer was talking to you, what kind of questions would they ask about that specific piece"; worth the work "to sell more product". FAQs feed long-tail AI searches ("everyone's not typing in just four words anymore... what temperature they play"), remove purchase blockers, and are "good for the bots to know the differences between your product and others." Accordion FAQs with one-line answers should be expanded with the keyword, "Otherwise why are these in dropdowns? They could just be listed as is." [calls 020, 041, 097; playbook VI 3.2]
- Reviews: "Keep the reviews there, because that'll help you sell more. It shows that people are buying it"; "especially on E commerce sites, so on your products you should definitely be asking your customers to leave reviews once they've received the product", with "a system to monitor that", because "Google will look and say this product has you know, 5,000, you know, five star reviews. It must be a good product so we should show it on top." Link testimonial blocks to where the review lives (Google, Yelp, the product page), and make a shared review block page-specific ("Reviews of the Yellowstone Day tour"). [calls 116, 097; course: reviews; playbook VI 3.4]
- Related products: "on a product page it'll be like here's some related products like that keeps Google on your website, that keeps people on your website" (no dead ends); "You may also like" blocks (often four products) count as internal links toward the 10 to 20 per page ceiling; strip heading tags from "Related products", cart/quantity modules, and breadcrumbs. [course: dead-end-page-has-no-outgoing-links; call 007; playbook IV Heading Structure, IV Internal Linking]
- Links from a product page: "If you have blog posts talking about how to use that product or how to clean that product or how to care for that product, you can absolutely link to those from that product page"; a sizing sheet at the bottom is good but "it does seem kind of like a dead end", so add a shop now / buy now button beside it because "Users are at the end of the page. There's nothing left for them to do." [calls 122, 097]
- Quote forms on custom-product pages: keep the full Get a Quote page and add a short form (name, phone, email, short message) on each custom-product page; conversions signal to Google and it removes a click. [call 013; playbook III 3.4]
- Unique descriptions at scale: standard shared descriptions across 1,000 doormat designs: "They should be different. Cannibalizing myself. Okay."; 100 engagement ring designs get unique meta descriptions and headers ("18 karat gold engagement ring" vs "14 karat gold engagement ring"), each optimized around one long-tail keyword, tracked in an Excel sheet; vary wording per item, "otherwise Google just restricts when the T shirts are too similar"; 400 vendor-fed diamonds with only a spec table get researched names ("Canadian diamonds", "ethical diamonds", "engagement diamond"), each a different H1, the unique shape/carat/clarity specs being "enough to make that a unique keyword", the bigger keyword ("ethically sourced Canadian diamonds") on the collection, and a pace of 10 or 20 a week "so you learn before touching all 400." [calls 013, 015, 056, 010]
- Variants and duplicate SKUs: "Add canonical tags. If you have to keep duplicate or similar pages, you can tell search engines which version is the 'main' one... You'll see this often on Shopify product pages with small variations." Hundreds of near-identical size pages (steel corner guards, every size a page): keep the pages that rank and get traffic, "do keyword research to be like, what sizes are people looking up the most and make sure I have those specific pages", and consider a category page with a filter for the rest; duplicate collections with "-1" URLs (couch-cushion-covers-1) get deleted. [book ch. 25; calls 116, 101]
- Seasonal and out-of-stock products: "We always recommend to not disable those URLs"; hide the Add to Cart / Buy Now button or set inventory to zero and leave the page live, because taking it down sends people to 404s or redirects, Google "lowers the importance of that page," and on return Google must recrawl and re-find it; a "one and done" item can be turned off and 301-redirected (passes authority, removes nav clutter); a permanently discontinued service gets a 410. Theatre tickets for closed shows: keep the page if people still search it, say it is sold out, set inventory to zero in schema, offer sign-up for notifications, link to other shows. Sold real-estate listings are "a dead page... carrying down your site": archive (unpublish), keep a few as case studies. Long-unsold designs: "just make a sale category and put things on sale and just get rid of them." [calls 030, 088, 047, 013; playbook IV Migrations]
- Seasonal product and gift pages: "have a money page about holiday gift cards", one page each for Black Friday, Christmas, Valentine's Day ("If it's a product page, that's totally fine to say Black Friday"); out of season remove the menu / homepage link but keep the URL live, with blog posts ("best Valentine's Day flowers to get your fiance") linking to it year-round so Google still finds it. Annual pages keep one evergreen URL with no year; update title / meta / H1 each year (2026 to 2027). Start seasonal optimization about six months out (a florist was told on a mid-June call to start Valentine's Day now; write "gifts for a May birthday" months before the spike); Jill published a blog about a B2B product line before the product page existed and will launch the page with a press release and LinkedIn content. [calls 021, 027; playbook III 2.3, VI 3.4]
- Product schema: name ("black boots"), image URL, brand, a description containing the product keyword plus descriptive factors, price currency and price, pasted as JSON-LD into the page header; reviews and prices feed rich snippets (star ratings, pricing); Yoast's default schema is enough for most. [calls 039; course: schema-markup; book ch. 22; playbook IV Schema]

## Collection / category pages: what goes on them, top to bottom

- Grid first, always. "Put marketing/info copy BELOW the grid, never above it"; "If somebody's on this collection page they want to buy... Don't make them scroll down. I want to see the title" and the products; "I want to buy a tennis shirt. I don't want to read about tennis shirts"; a workbook store had "all of this information before people get to the products... Especially on mobile, I got to scroll past all this... It's like recipe websites where you have to keep scrolling to get to the ingredients... it annoys people. They leave", so move the text "down here" near the footer (Shopify allows it); a fly-net-hat page lost its "we're taking pre orders" block and "Sick of the great Aussie salute" copy above the products ("is this really adding value? ... are they going to be more likely to buy? Probably not"). The book's step 4: "Keep the product grid at the top where people expect it, and place all your optimized content below the product listings. Search engines will still crawl and index it." (A theme may block adding a heading above the category tiles.) [playbook III 3.3, VI 3.2; calls 066, 084, 094, 055; book ch. 39]
- The shape: "at the top you could have a sentence or two, and then obviously the products in that collection. Underneath that you can have paragraphs. You can almost write, like a blog post with pictures and all this kind of other stuff." A four-SKU collection: "you're almost going to write a mini blog post underneath those four skus" (how often people should eat it, etc.) plus FAQ questions, "products on top and then FAQ blog below", drafted with the Rank Week blog prompt ("I'm writing a collection page for ... I need to have some FAQ questions underneath it"). A collection that is "just pictures of products" needs, underneath, "almost like a blog post, but more information about those products." [calls 116, 017, 020]
- Kira's content list for a thin collection (a page can rank with "two sentences at the top", but enrich it): why wear a padel shirt, benefits, differences vs a pickleball or regular sports shirt, history of the sport, what to pair it with (shorts, sneakers, with credible external links to where to buy), and FAQs at the bottom under "Frequently asked questions: paddle shirts", "a sneaky little way of including the keyword"; "I always like to think about pages as, like, we want it to have as much information as a blog post." Leanne: a collection "could have more text here, and then at the bottom, we could have another section with like, more descriptive text." [calls 076, 100]
- The book's four steps: (1) keyword and competitive research (Semrush, Ubersuggest, "or even ChatGPT for ideas"), then check the top 3 ranking pages for structure and keywords; (2) "Write Content Like a Blog Post": keyword in the H1, H2s below the grid ("How to Choose the Best Drip Coffee Machine"), FAQs real customers ask, opinions, tips, buyer's guides; (3) internal links to related posts, products, or services ("Coffee Machine Maintenance Guide", "How to Brew the Perfect Cup"); (4) content below the grid. Payoff: "sell products AND bring in organic traffic." The default layout being improved: filters on the left (brand, price, star rating), a product grid on the right, "And that's where most people stop." A thumbnails-only page misses the chance to "Rank for big, valuable keywords; Keep users engaged; Funnel visitors into deeper product pages; Build topical authority." [book ch. 39]
- Question-style H2s under the grid, not a labelled FAQ: "I wouldn't do FAQs, no. I would just do like a heading two tag with the question, how long does a temporary tattoo last? And then" the answer, which carries the keyword ("our motivational temporary tattoos are designed to last three months"), written differently from the product pages to avoid duplication; the playbook's version: plain H2 questions answered in natural language that reincorporate the keyword ("How long does the temporary tattoo last?", "Can I shower with a temporary tattoo?"), built with Shopify's native collapsible rows, "most importantly, you're using the keyword a couple more times on the page." Answer the SERP's question on the page when the SERP shows product pages: "Where do you get fly net hats? You can get them right here. They're made in Australia..." [calls 119, 101; playbook VI 3.2]
- FAQs on collection pages first: broadest reach and far less labour than per product; "start with collection pages because doing every product is a lot of work"; never duplicate the same FAQ block across pages. [call 041; playbook VI 3.2]
- One keyword per collection, and split mixed ones. A collection covering tennis T-shirts, polos, and hoodies is "three keywords in one and confuses Google": make the parent "explore our tennis apparel collection(s)" and give T-shirts, polos, and hoodies their own collections; don't keep two collections for the same thing (tennis polo shirts vs performance tennis shirts), pick one. Book: instead of one generic "coffee machine" category, separate pages for drip coffee makers, espresso machines, French presses, stovetop coffee pots ("More pages. More keywords. More ranking opportunities"), "But don't go overboard. You don't want hundreds of nearly identical category pages." Workbooks: one page was mixing "Canadian curriculum press", "80 page expanded edition workbooks", math, reading, coding, money (with coding products not even present); build by subject and grade ("math workbooks" 140/mo, "grade 10 math workbook" 20/mo, very high intent, "pre kindergarten workbooks"), in parent language (ages not grades, "PDF" not "ebook"); "Let the keyword research dictate which pages you actually create." [calls 061, 084; book ch. 39]
- More split examples: a "Nutrition" tab ("is that dog food?") becomes "Nutrition supplements for dogs", "Nutrition supplements for cats", "Nutrition supplement bundles for pets"; "necklaces" stays and a new "Murano necklaces" collection is added if people search it; ski T-shirts get a collection per keyword ("T shirts for skiing", "apres ski T shirts"); long-tail buyer-phrase collections (funny T shirts for women / for adults / cat / Christian / golf); a "little black dress" collection named for the keyword; "swimwear" (better: cup-size swimwear) ranks on the swimwear category, not the homepage; "New this season" reads "New lingerie this season" and "Signature pieces" reads "Signature lingerie pieces"; a perfume store missing "mini perfumes" (18,000 volume, 28 difficulty): "I should make a page about mini perfumes." [calls 066, 062, 078, 103, 118, 055, 107]
- Portfolio of small transactional collections when no head term has volume: per zodiac sign, per birth month, "funny tennis shirts"; "horoscope candles" (~110/mo at ~19 KD in the playbook, 190/mo in the call), "Scorpio candle" zero difficulty: "If they're searching for Scorpio candle, there should be a Scorpio page"; multiple collections with similar stuff are fine, with one hub/master post linking to each, while blogs handle "best gifts for Scorpio birthday / girlfriend / mom". [playbook VI 3.3; call 087]
- Reinforce the collection keyword in every product title inside it: "canvas art prints" collection with every product name containing "canvas art print", the category title, and a little text underneath; product names on a Shopify collection should render as H2 / H3 tags (a theme that didn't needed code edits). [calls 055, 100, 076; playbook VI 3.1]
- Brand sale collections: a separate collection per brand ("collection / brand name sale") listing the discounted products, with "at least one or two paragraphs explaining what the brand is, that your products are on sale and something that differentiates you from that brand's actual website"; the brand always ranks #1 for its own name, so go all in on "sale", "cheap", "discounted" (whichever has most volume), build internal links, and a press release mentioning the sale can land you directly under the brand site. [call 030]
- Marketplace or high-turnover stores: "your money pages could be your category pages for brands or for different styles or different pieces of clothing, like different collections"; a product page that appears and "then it goes away. That is not going to work... because it takes too much time to optimize"; a client with out-of-stock products: "we optimize the category pages and... any kind of brand pages." [call 118]
- Shopify quirks to fix on collection pages: the H1 often sits in the logo or at the bottom while the top text is an H2 ("H1 tag always the very top"; "every single page heading one should always be the first heading on that page"); menu items rendered as H2 / H3 above the H1 get changed to divs or paragraphs in theme.liquid and restyled with CSS; stray H2s like "country, region or cart" live in theme code; the collection H1 must be an H1, not an H3, and cart / footer items must not be headings; duplicate H1s come from page-builder apps (GemPages), fixable in product.liquid / theme.liquid via Edit Code (ChatGPT or Shopify Sidekick can help find the code, Denroi is better when available; the workaround is a redundant second H1 containing the keyword plus escalation to Shopify support); one Shopify H1 sat on a "limited time offer" element far down the page and had to be moved to the top; Dawn-based themes append the store name to every title (find the toggle or the "[store name]" shortcode, flipping it changes every page); the URL-edit screen has a checkbox to auto-create a redirect; a URL slug that contradicts the H1 (polo-shirts slug, "tennis shirts" H1) means "people have two different intents", so change the slug or make a new page and return the old one to polo shirts; an SEO app's robots.txt was disallowing blogs, collections, and products (disable the app); dynamic URLs with "?" or "=" are inventory or search pages that will not rank. [calls 066, 097, 094, 064, 007, 068, 084, 055, 059; playbook IV Page-Builder, IV Heading Structure, IV Robots.txt, IV Canonicals]
- WordPress / WooCommerce quirks: a product's long description not rendering on the front end (audit tools see no text) is fixed in Appearance > theme file editor, product.php / single-product template and the description class, pasting a description in if the block is missing; a category page with three H1s including an empty one in the category-description code view; blog category pages with low word count are acceptable if you do not need them to rank (HeyTony's Facebook-ads work category reached via Work > filter; to improve, add words at the top: "here's some of our work that we've done on Facebook ads. Here's the strategy we take", and more items add words); category pages need no featured / OG images "because these aren't getting indexed anyways"; a work category's topic comes from the anchor text pointing to it ("work category, web design... now Search engines know that this is about web design"); category H2s change by themselves as content is added. [calls 072, 041; course: low-word-count-how-to-fix, open-graph-issues, internal-linking, spy-on-your-competition]

## Layout and design (both)

- Reference stores: Huckberry is Matt's and Leanne's reference "for just how to lay out your products" (and its journal); product sites reuse the homepage wireframe with product categories; a model store splits the shop dropdown into shopping sections and gives each homepage section one product with explanatory text ("This website could improve their SEO by having more text on their website"). [calls 008, 100; playbook IV Web Design]
- Ecommerce UX from the book: "there's a big green 'Add to Cart' button staring you right in the face. No scrolling. No searching. It's just there"; offer Apple Pay and Google Pay; abandoned-cart email ("Finish Checking Out" with a 10% coupon); instant confirmation and tracking; a delivery-window message; a post-purchase check-in / upsell email. Bad UX to avoid: an ad that lands on the homepage instead of the product; "20 near-identical products so the one from the ad can't be found"; no wallets; no follow-up. Ads should advertise one product and link to that product page (a bundle landing page is fine). [book ch. 21; call 016]
- Header size: a logo bar taking "one third of the page" vs HeyTony's at about one sixteenth: "what the fuck is your logo doing so big? ... Where's the value?" [call 084]
- Small interaction fixes: make horizontal product-scroller buttons bigger or bolder with right padding; light grey thin text on white is hard to read and "Google might be hitting you with accessibility on this one." [call 097]
- Accordions: supplementary technical detail (specs) in an accordion is fine; "some people saying that drop down menus don't work as well anymore because people are using it to hide a lot of words. So if you can make sure it's not in a dropdown menu, that's fine"; acceptable on technical product pages "if people are using it... as long as it's not, like, every piece of information you're trying to hide." [playbook III 2.10; calls 117, 116]
- The benchmark for "make me want to buy your thing": the Rank Week landing page, where you know immediately what it is, the dates, "reserve your spot" CTAs halfway down, a countdown timer, results shown. [call 084]
- Change one thing at a time: a BigCommerce-to-Shopify relaunch moved conversion from 0.3% to 1.2% and the owner only knows she "changed a little bit the way the sizes were laid out"; if you change everything at once you cannot tell what worked. [call 032]
- Product-discovery tools: a bra size guide should show products under the result, ask a qualifying question ("what type of bra are you looking for?") that leads to a collection, offer a "check out all our bras in 38B" filter button, save the size, and capture email. [call 033]
- No sticky header means the footer must carry navigation, "even something as simple as collection list" linking the main collections, because with nothing in the footer "there's no button for you just to click for them to continue to look around the site." [call 086]
- Platform: Shopify is "the best e commerce platform in the world" (blogs, "all the like schema, markup and all that stuff that Google is looking for"); "if you have a shop, like I would use Shopify, it makes it so much easier for people to buy"; Shopify "is kind of limited in terms of what you could do with design, but you could do this in Shopify for sure"; if a Wix site already outranks competitors keep it and add the shop on the same domain; WordPress has the most options, Wix and Squarespace slightly fewer "but you can still really rank those sites"; Square is very limited (no text over the hero, hover text is alt text not an H1, no true H1 field); Squarespace if few products; WooCommerce / BigCommerce fine but need a developer; SmugMug galleries canonicalize image URLs as duplicates; Ghost handles images and alt text well. Checkout can live off-site ("You don't have to take payment on your own website"). [calls 024, 043, 016, 017, 067, 022; playbook IV Page-Builder]

## Visuals (both)

- Real product photography, in context: a person using or wearing the item, with descriptive alt text; competitors show shirts in context (snow, ski cabin, snowplow in the background), "Google can now read what's in a picture" and realistic context helps conversion, "look at what these guys are doing that I'm not"; a tennis collection with "no photo of someone wearing a tennis shirt" needs one. [playbook VI 3.2; calls 078, 084]
- File names and alt text: upload "black-flame-paddle-shirt" not "1234.jpg" because Google reads the file name, people "shop with their eyes" and may be on the images tab, and keyword in file name + alt text + on page is "controlling every variable"; every engagement-ring shape thumbnail had alt text "diamond", so match alt text to the shape ("1 carat round brilliant diamond", "cushion cut diamond"), fill the empty ones, and rename and re-upload thumbnails with matching file names ("easier than fixing the code... probably more impact, double win"). Tools: Image Alt Text Viewer; On Page SEO AI. Alt text is "five or six words", should not be used to add words to the page, and on Shopify is edited in the image's alt field or by re-uploading. [calls 041, 117, 125; playbook III 2.8]
- Image specs and speed: a template wants 1200 x 1200 product images while Google pulls 138 px thumbnails and 640 px; convert JPEGs to WebP; Shopify speed checklist: CDN set up, images served as WebP, lazy loading enabled, CSS / JS deferred, delete old oversized images still on the server; "The best thing you can do is use next-gen image formats (like .webp)"; a Fiverr Shopify speed specialist (roughly $100) is "the only time you're going to hear me say, go to Fiverr." [calls 060, 007, 009; playbook IV Site Speed; book ch. 19]
- Product images as links: an image "showcasing a product that clicks through to its detail page" with descriptive alt text; a "my go to cleaning supplies" graphic that was only an image is "a lost opportunity for linking", it should go to a page where people can add everything to their cart. [book ch. 9; call 124]
- Video: brand pages can host supplemental product videos, but video without a keyword target "is not gonna do much." [call 056]

## Length, keyword density, and on-page SEO (both)

- Placement checklist (Matt Demers): keyword in the product name (usually the H1), SEO / meta title, meta description, URL slug, the first 100 words of the description, naturally throughout, at least one subheading, image alt text, and image file names; the playbook adds an FAQ. Keyword first in the title and meta: "The sooner that keyword appears in your title, in your meta description, in the content as a whole, the more important Google and crawl bots will view that keyword." [call 041, 007; playbook III 2.6]
- Tag fixes from the reviews: move "cute combat boots" to the beginning of the title (adjective "colorful" later unless it also has volume) and shorten the meta to "140, 145 characters just to ensure it doesn't get cut off"; a category SEO title that was just "Bracelets" becomes "authentic Murano glass bracelets", a 63-character meta is extended to about 140 to 155 characters (under 160), a slug typo ("brace") is fixed only with a 301 and "extreme caution" (she once "paid dearly" for a slug change); a title shortened to under 60 characters via ChatGPT with a meta keyword near the front ("Scratch and sniff stickers lovers will enjoy the Series 1 bundle featuring 338 scented designs..."); a sustainable-fashion title carries the differentiator with the lowest difficulty and enough volume (eco friendly, sustainable, organic cotton, Ecovero). [calls 097, 041, 125, 086]
- Shopify meta descriptions: never rely on the auto-pulled excerpt; one store's product metas were pulling the required EU representative / product-warning text, "if it's taking a snippet of the content, that's basically a tell that a custom meta description was never written"; write each one in the product or collection's "search engine listing" / "search engine snippet" box (title tag, URL handle, meta description) focused on the one keyword with volume, low competition, and conversion; "if you can put in the title tag and meta description, that's all you really need. Like, you don't need Yoast for that"; optimizing every page's title and meta at once is fine. Collections missing metas or with over-long excerpt metas get rewritten the same way. [calls 086, 116, 106; playbook III 2.2]
- Every product needs its own title, meta, H1, and headers; two pages sharing the same title, meta, or H1 means Google "may not show any of them." [call 015; course: duplicate-tags]
- Keyword density has no set number: "If they only mentioned it once, you likely will only need to do it twice. If they mention it 20 times, you'll likely have to do it 21... You don't want to over optimize your page by going 20 extra keywords above them. That's redundant." A collection had "leather ballet flats" 41 times (mostly via product names, fine) but "women's leather trainers" only 8 times in one middle paragraph: "your keyword density might not be high enough. Like, if you got rid of half of this stuff and kept four of the keywords, would your density be better?" Big brands' pages are simplistic, compare against them; a tool flagged Leanne's own product page "too high", same lesson as service pages: stuffing reads as gibberish. [calls 007, 058, 115]
- Word count: same rule as the homepage, compare to the top two or three ranking competitors; with 15 to 20 reviews on a product, "look at the word count of the actual, like, what your competitor is doing, minus, if they have reviews, try to match their word count, and then try to match their keyword density and just pretend like your reviews don't exist on that page"; one member's "600 words on the product page is gonna be maybe too much"; Jorg's ~1,000-word collection is "probably around the right range... you could have a hundred more words" against competitors showing 2,200 to 3,400 words that were not visible ("it could be in so many different places... a pop up... when you click on this, more stuff loads"); the #1 ski T-shirt competitor ranks with a plain "T shirts" collection, one H1, and barely any text ("our complete T shirt collection for women and men"), "this should be easy for you to rank." [calls 076, 116, 117, 066, 078]
- Over-optimization: a 4,000-word fly-net-hat category against page-one competitors at roughly 174 to 590 words was "just doing too much... over optimized": repeated variants (indigenous art fly net hat, mosquito net hat, protection print fly net hat) collapse to "fly net hat"; the FAQ accordion (roughly 1,000 to 1,750 words) was removed to draft ("get rid of all these FAQs", don't delete); two H1s (one was the cart) became one; then wait a month or two before more changes. Don't benchmark the #1 competitor if they have a ton of backlinks, "let's try to go for the second position or third position" (the ranking competitor had hidden H3 / H4 link text and no internal linking, so good SEO was not why it ranked). [call 094]
- Hidden words count against you: an audit showed 808 words but only 511 visible, the rest from the pop-up, rewards program, cookie notice, recommended products, cart drawer, and "content hidden that's in the Shopify files or a mobile render only version"; "These are hidden words on your page that if a human can't see it, Google's like, why are you doing this?" [call 125]
- Heading hygiene: one H1, first on the page, not the logo, cart, price, or quantity ("cart, price, quantity should not be in and around your heading one tag"); a GemPages product with two H1s (the short product title shown on the collection, and a padded custom one) keeps the abbreviated one as H1 and demotes the other to H2 or text (two headings back to back with no text between is fine; "Only heading one is limited to just one per page"); a theme coding product titles as H3 instead of H1 is fixed product by product; brand name is not needed in H2s on a non-homepage ("You will rank for your brand name no matter what you do"), and "Why Rainbow City? Colorful, cute combat boots are different" reads "a little bit keyword stuffy"; buttons can carry the exact keyword ("Shop vegan colorful cute combat boots"); Shopify "always messes up their H2s and H1s in their order of things" (fixable). [calls 125, 007, 012, 097, 106]
- Keyword selection numbers: to be worth the time, "if it's a product, probably no less than 30. If it's a collection, no less than 50" monthly searches (lower for a brand-new site); cluster variations (a microwavable heat cap list: 17 keywords, 690 volume, difficulty 14 to 17) turn a 70-volume keyword into a real target, used as concepts (product feature icons like "deep conditioning", "microwavable"), in FAQs, headings, and alt text variations, not jammed into one page; singular vs plural carry different difficulty ("tennis t shirt" difficulty 8 vs "tennis t shirts" 16 to 17, the singular won); "Tennis shirts for matches, practice and everyday wear" is too long, "funny tennis shirts" has zero competition and volume, "if they're looking for a funny tennis shirt, I don't care where they're wearing it"; "custom" packaging (300 pages) uses "custom" where searched but researches sub-intents (eco friendly, wholesale, sizes) with H2s like "custom pizza box designs include ... here's how we do it." [calls 041, 066, 017; playbook II 1.4, VI 3.1]
- Match the SERP's page type: Googling "ski T-shirt" returns collection pages (and Amazon collections), so "people are looking for a collection page and you're trying to optimize for a product page"; "Google wants to see a collection page. I'm going to optimize my collection page for that keyword"; "where do I get fly net hats" returns Amazon and product pages, so optimize the product / collection page, not a blog; check whether searchers want the net, not the hat; yarn stores with small needle selections are still competitors because their pages rank for the keyword. [calls 078, 024, 101, 094, 022]
- Amazon: "you can ignore Amazon because they're going to have shit SEO anyways. And they're only ranking because they have that product and they have so much authority. But we can actually outrank Amazon by having better content"; ranking on Amazon "doesn't necessarily mean those products will rank well on Google." [course: creating-a-blog-outline; book Introduction]
## CTAs and conversion (both)

- The Add to Cart is the CTA: big, visible without scrolling, with Apple Pay and Google Pay; then the follow-through (abandoned-cart email "You left something in your cart" or "Here's 10% off if you complete your order within 24 hours", confirmation and tracking, delivery window, post-purchase upsell); retarget product-page visitors who didn't buy. [book ch. 21, 47]
- Buttons with the exact keyword ("Shop vegan colorful cute combat boots") are "great" ("for buttons, you can just go for the exact keyword"); add a buy button beside any bottom-of-page element (sizing sheet) so the page does not dead-end. [call 097]
- FAQs convert: "people often have questions that stop them from purchasing... addressing them can help with conversion." [call 041]
- Blog links sell cheap products: Matt spent $30,000 after a blog post about "can you start a community on Slack" linked to a product page; "if you have internal links and you have products like under a hundred bucks, people are probably going to be a lot more likely to buy that or fill out the form." Ecommerce backdoor when product keywords are too hard (pre-workout powder): one or two question blogs a week ("why take pre workout", "can you take pre workout when you're pregnant") with "a pop up with like a discount code... that leads them to your product or you can mention your products inside your blog posts." Informational content (perimenopause and sexual health for a sex-toy store) is worth writing even if you can't beat Mayo Clinic: "if they can find the information on your site ... they're staying in your ecosystem." [course: the-power-of-blog-posts; calls 117, 013]
- Google Merchant Center flags: shipping speed and return policy are matched against competitors; either match them or change the fundamentals (raise the price and offer free shipping, "people like free shipping"). [call 081]
- Etsy sellers who aren't showing: "look at how long their descriptions are... what their products are called, how many images they have", and start your own website "because then you control that ecosystem"; treat Etsy and Shopify separately (backlinks to the Etsy shop help it rank; a press release can be titled toward Etsy, "funny tennis T shirts on Etsy"). [calls 118, 096, 029]

## Links, indexing, and site structure (both)

- Menu depth: "if you have to click more than two times to get to that page, it's probably hidden. Those products are hidden"; dropdown menus themselves don't hurt SEO; orphan products must be reachable from the homepage within their subcategory path, with the main categories listed on the homepage; six collections linked on the homepage is good, but take them out of carousels; a product flagged "not accessible" is orphaned or buried. [calls 013, 017, 010, 065]
- Orphans at scale: ~600 Shopify "orphan" flags traced to nofollow footer links (Command F "nofollow" in the page source; product links from collection and brand pages are fine); practical fix: pick the 10 most important pages and add one or two internal links to each from blogs or elsewhere, and turn off any nofollow toggle when creating links in Shopify or Wix. 118 orphans on another store: delete the "-1" duplicate collections; a keyword collection nobody links to ("red wine glasses") is pointless, "if it's a keyword, you can't hide that page... you need to link to those from somewhere", the menu or blog posts. [calls 044, 101]
- URL and hierarchy: "example.com/product/protein-milk/" with /product/ as the subdirectory; category subdirectories /collections/, /shop/, /store/, /products/; breadcrumbs like "Products > Dog > Collars > Small Collars" create "a logical internal link structure that signals hierarchy and relevance"; Shopify's flat collections / products structure changes URLs on migration, so "keep all the URLs and the content as similar as you possibly can", never re-change URLs to fake nesting ("a redirect to a redirect to the actual page, which is not ideal"), and ask linking sites to update redirected URLs. [book ch. 6, 9; call 049]
- Collection-page linking rules: never link the page's own keyword away: "that keyword doesn't get linked to any other pages. It only gets linked to this page... tennis t shirts means 10 pages on your website instead of just one"; linking "tennis polo shirts" or "tennis hoodies" to their own collections is fine; don't link "premium quality ski T shirts" to a blog about "what makes a good T shirt" (wrong signal); the product tiles already give a category page "lots of internal links to other pages"; external links can point to where to buy pairing items, never to another store in your niche. [calls 066, 078, 084, 086, 076]
- Blog posts feed product and collection pages: "if you offer products online or products, you're going to link to a product page and you're going to use the keyword to link to that page"; "best of" articles ("best paddle shirts for rain", "best paddle apparel for cold weather") list your products with an image and internal links using the product names, and mentioning competitor products honestly is fine ("Google views this as an honest interpretation"); linking many products from one post is fine if relevant (funny doormats for baseball fans); but no product gallery at the bottom of every post, "I would link to one product that is relevant to that blog"; every post carries 2+ links to service or product pages; focus links on best sellers and the collections that already convert. [course: the-power-of-blog-posts; calls 041, 020, 073, 086; email 2025-07-03]
- Where to start on a big catalogue: 2.2 million products, "just choose five. Choose five of your most popular ones" as the first money pages to link into; 3,000 product pages "may be too many product pages", one page at a time starting with the most important; with many dead or unindexed pages, focus on indexed, important product pages first (internal links and backlinks); a jewelry store audits collections and the top 10 products first. [calls 116, 118, 047, 020]
- Cannibalization rules: a "natural granola" collection and a "health benefits of natural granola" blog don't cannibalize (the blog targets the longer keyword); a blog on the same topic as the collection would; a blog outranking your own collection (silk nightwear, ranking 10 where competitors show product pages): first "adjust the blog post to feature some of your products", else scrap the post and optimize the collection, or redirect the post to the collection (losing some of its backlinks); when collection, product, and blog pages all target the same keywords, give each page one keyword, redoing collections first, then products, then blogs; a blog ranking for a brand name instead of the brand page can't be forced, "You have to hope that they click on a link. You can try doing a pop up if you want or put a link higher on that page"; product-comparison pages (Botox vs Dysport vs Xeomin) are valid consideration content, not cannibalization. [calls 012, 116, 009, 048; playbook II 3.1, III 2.13]
- Same products in several collections: fine when each collection has a genuinely different keyword and content (multiple horoscope collections), not when "all the collections are the exact same with just a different keyword." [calls 087, 101]
- Sale and seasonal categories: a sale category to clear long-unsold designs; brand sale collections; seasonal money pages kept live year-round with the nav link pulled out of season and blog links pointing in year-round. [calls 013, 030, 021; playbook VI 3.4]
- Indexing expectations: 722 indexed vs 10,800 not indexed after a migration is normal, the not-indexed set is mostly tag pages and categories that "won't be indexed"; don't keep pressing "start validation"; request indexing only for key pages to bump them up the queue; ignore dynamic URLs in GSC and keep them out of the sitemap. [call 035; playbook IV Canonicals]
- Tag pages and blog categories: WordPress tag pages are "dead pages... duplicate content from the blog post... no unique content", HeyTony never uses them; categories only, "10 categories max... Never set it to uncategorized", a post may sit in two relevant categories; blog category pages link from the homepage so every post is two clicks from home; thin tag archives can be kept out of the index with robots.txt. [calls 110, 010; course: uploading-publishing-your-content; book ch. 24, 27]
- The blog lives on the store's domain, on the native blog: "your blog should be on the same domain as your main site... all you need is one link from one domain... merge everything onto your Shopify blog"; Shopify's native blog needs no app (one column, picture, text, text, picture, text; pagination default 6 posts per page, set to ~30, then 60, 100 as it grows; every post needs a featured image); BigCommerce uses its built-in blog, not a third-party blog with "?" and "=" URLs on another domain ("it needs to be on the same domain and like an actual URL"); a multi-vendor marketplace must not let vendors publish their own posts on your domain ("spammers are going to get in there, sell your backlinks"). [calls 091, 075, 065, 024; playbook IV Page-Builder, VI 3.2]
- Off-site: if directories outrank you, get listed on them (free); a nationally-shipped brand with no storefront skips GBP and local directories (Yellow Pages) and copies competitor backlinks instead; print-on-demand stores struggle to enter AI Overviews, what helped Denroi's tests was many low-authority outside mentions describing the products (material, how it was printed, print quality) and linking to them; a whole site dedicated to one product (GutterSaver Pro) outranks competitors' single product pages and is being recommended by AI. [playbook VI 2.4, VI 3.4; calls 062, 030, 012]
- Blog-to-paid-product pipeline: publish the free, shorter version as a blog post and sell the long, detailed version as a standalone paid guide (Jackie's curly-hair guide). [playbook VI 3.4]

## Don'ts

- Don't put text above the product grid on a collection page; "Chat GPT will make shit up. It doesn't know the SEO best practices." [call 066]
- Don't invent product names; don't put the brand or inside-joke name first. [book ch. 38; call 125]
- Don't copy-paste manufacturer descriptions or share one description across designs. [book ch. 38; call 013]
- Don't reuse the same FAQ block across products or collections. [call 020; playbook VI 3.2]
- Don't link a collection's own keyword to other pages; don't mismatch anchor text and destination. [calls 066, 078]
- Don't keep two collections for the same keyword, and don't clone a collection with "-1" URLs. [calls 061, 101]
- Don't put "best" on your own collection page or the brand name in non-homepage H2s. [calls 066, 097]
- Don't hide words in pop-ups, drawers, or mobile-only renders, and don't push everything into accordions. [calls 125, 117]
- Don't 404 a product that might restock; don't put a year in an evergreen URL. [call 030; playbook III 2.3]
- Don't rely on the auto-generated meta description; don't let the theme append the store name to every title. [calls 086, 055]
- Don't use tag pages, third-party blog apps, or a blog on a separate domain. [calls 110, 065, 091]
- Don't over-optimize to 4,000 words when page one sits at 174 to 590; don't benchmark a backlink-heavy #1. [call 094]
- Don't change everything at once after a relaunch; don't change a ranking page's copy because a tool suggests copy that is "bad for our brand" ("if the page ranks leave it"). [calls 032, 069]
- Don't copy competitors' encyclopedic blog volume: "If they have 10 posts on the topic, you only need to necessarily maybe do one because nine of their 10 might be doing nothing." [call 069]
- Don't send ad traffic to the homepage; don't put a product gallery at the bottom of every blog post. [book ch. 21; call 073]
- Don't name products with city names. [call 012]
- Don't external-link to a competing store in your niche. [call 086]
