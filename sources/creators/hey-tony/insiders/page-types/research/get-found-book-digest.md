# Get Found book digest (Matt Diamante, 2025)

Source: "Get Found: The No-Fluff Guide to Ranking Higher and Becoming Your Customers' First Choice" by Matt Diamante (HeyTony), HeyTony Inc., 2025. 48 chapters in six parts: I On-Page SEO (ch. 1-14), II Off-Page SEO (ch. 15-18), III Technical SEO (ch. 19-28), IV SEO Tools (ch. 29-35), V How to Optimize Your Website (ch. 36-42), VI Maximizing Your Efforts (ch. 43-48).

Scope of this digest: everything the book says about how website pages should be built, per page type and per element. Every bullet is one concrete rule, number, threshold, step, or example from the book, with the chapter in brackets. Verbatim quotes are in quotation marks; where the book's own punctuation used a dash inside a quote, it has been replaced with a comma or colon so this file stays dash-clean. Where the book says nothing about a page type, the heading says so and lists the closest adjacent guidance.

Overarching rules the book applies to every page, stated once here and assumed under every heading below:

- "Each page on your site should focus on one specific topic" [ch. 4]; "Every page on your website should be unique and cover a specific topic. Not kinda-sorta different. Fully distinct." [ch. 25]
- Content is "everything on your website, from the words and images to the videos and links ... These elements should be present on every page of your website." [ch. 2]
- "Think of every page on your website, blogs included, as a door to your business. The more doors you have, the more opportunities people have to find you." [ch. 2]; the college professor's lesson: "more pages mean ranking for more keywords and, ultimately, more traffic" [ch. 4]
- Target keyword goes "in your title tag, meta description, H1 tag, at least one H2 tag, and naturally throughout the body of your page." [ch. 3]
- "The number one reason people fail to rank is that they're not even using their keyword on their page at all!" [ch. 3]
- Every page: 3 to 5 in-content internal links with descriptive anchor text, at least one to a product or service page [ch. 9]; at least one external link to a reputable source, inside the content only [ch. 10]; one image per roughly 200 words [ch. 12]; one H1, at least 2 H2s [ch. 7].
- "Aim to have slightly more content than the page currently ranking #1." [ch. 2]; "if your competitors are using the keyword 7 times, use it 8 times." [ch. 37]; "If their page has 1,000 words, write 1,100. If they use the keyword 5 times, aim for 6, not 16." [ch. 41]
- Write so "even a 12-year-old could understand it." "It's dumb not to dumb it down." [ch. 2]
- Google weighs E-E-A-T (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness); "Google wants your personal experience in your content. It makes it unique." [ch. 2]
- Patience: "You can do all of these optimizations and not see any shift for 6-12 months." [Part V intro]; HeyTony requires a 12-month minimum client commitment [Introduction].

## 1. Homepage

- URL: the home page is the one exception to "every page's URL should include the relevant keyword"; it "usually takes the form of a simple domain name (like website.com)." [ch. 6]
- Hero: "Please, I'm begging you, ditch the home page carousels (aka sliders). No one is sitting there waiting for your image to slide over to the next one, especially on mobile. Sliders just slow your site down. Replace the carousels with one clear image and a headline that actually grabs attention." [ch. 20]
- First mobile test question for the home page: "Is it immediately clear what your business does?" [ch. 20]
- "Your home page should link directly to your individual services or top-selling products. This puts the things people actually want to buy one click away from your home page." [ch. 27]
- "You should also link to your blog categories directly from the home page. Keep it simple, limit yourself to 10 categories max. (You'll never need more than that.) With this setup, every blog post on your site is just two clicks from your home page one click to a category, one click to the blog post." [ch. 27]
- "PRO tip: Add your most recent 5-10 blog posts directly to your home page. Most website platforms have a built-in tool or widget to automate this. Now your latest posts are also just one click away from the home page." [ch. 27]
- Matt's long-standing crawl-depth rule: "If a page matters, link to it from the home page or the main menu." [ch. 27]
- Reference site structure Matt built for HeyTony: "a proper home page, service pages, a contact page, and a content hub built around regular blog posts." [ch. 9] Charlie's relaunch: "We launched with optimized service pages, a solid home page, and a few strategic blog posts." [ch. 25]
- A one-page placeholder site is "absolutely terrible for SEO" [ch. 9]; one-page microsites with "No blog. No content strategy. Nothing to optimize other than ... that one page" are not recommended, backlinks were the only lever left [ch. 16].
- Video belongs on the home page too: "Whether it's your home page, a blog post, or a landing page, video can be a game-changer." Google indexes one video per page. [ch. 14]
- Don't send ad clicks for a specific product to the home page: "It takes you to the home page instead of to the product. Confusion sets in." [ch. 21]
- Do not submit the home page URL to Google Search Console on its own: "make sure not to submit your home page or blog posts individually; Google only wants the sitemap file." [ch. 23]
- Like every page, the home page should carry words, images, video, links [ch. 2], at least one external link inside the content [ch. 10], and 3-5 internal links [ch. 9].
- Brand: people find the home page via navigational/branded searches ("Facebook", "HeyTony SEO"); if your business name is "supergeneric or overlaps with other well-established companies or websites ... it might be worth considering a more unique name." [ch. 3]
- Main navigation and footer appear on every page including the home page; see section 15 for what goes in them.

## 2. Service pages and money pages (chapter 40 in full detail)

- One page per service: "Most service businesses offer more than one thing. Take my agency, HeyTony, for example. We offer website design, SEO, Meta ads, Google ads, and a few other things. And guess what? Each service gets its own page. Why? Because each page on your website should be about one specific topic." [ch. 40]
- Why not one big services page: "If I were to throw all my services onto one massive page, what would the main keyword be? 'Digital marketing'? That's way too broad. And from an SEO standpoint, it's a wasted opportunity." [ch. 40]
- "Service pages are actually easier to optimize than product or category pages (at least in my opinion). Why? Because when someone is searching for a service, they're already looking for information. They're problem-aware. And if you can answer their questions clearly and quickly, you're more likely to win the lead." [ch. 40]
- What every service page must cover, verbatim list [ch. 40]:
  - "What the service is (Use plain English, not jargon.)"
  - "Who it's for (Speak directly to your ideal customer.)"
  - "What problems it solves"
  - "What your process looks like"
  - "What it costs (or a general price range) (If you're not ready to list prices, at least address the cost.)"
  - "How long it takes"
  - "What makes your approach unique"
  - "Common questions (Yes, add an FAQ section.)"
  - "Testimonials, before / after results, case studies, logos, reviews, etc."
  - "Call-to-action (Tell people what to do next. Book a call? Fill out a form? Don't leave them guessing.)"
- "Treat your service page like a blog post (sort of)": use the target keyword in "The H1 tag (the title of the page)", "At least one H2 tag", "The URL", "The title tag and meta description", "The content (sprinkled naturally throughout)". [ch. 40]
- "Don't forget to include internal links to related blog posts, case studies, or even other services. This strengthens your topical relevance and improves your SEO." [ch. 40]
- Real-world model (search "air conditioner repair Hamilton"): the winning companies "didn't just say 'We fix heating and cooling problems.' They had full-on pages specifically about air conditioner repair, optimized for the city I was in." Their service pages included [ch. 40]:
  - "Average wait times (helpful during a heatwave)"
  - "Hourly rates and pricing info"
  - "Common AC problems (like freezing units)"
  - "DIY tips to try before calling"
  - "Glowing customer reviews"
  - "What to expect when the technician arrives"
  - "Prep instructions and safety protocols"
- Closing rule: "So be specific. Be helpful. Be thorough. Your service page should answer every question a potential customer might have, before they ever pick up the phone or fill out a form. Because when someone is in need, the best page wins. Every time." [ch. 40]
- Writing process: service pages use the same chapter 37 process (keyword, mini audit, outline from top 3-5 competitors, AI draft, add experience). When prompting the AI, "just replace 'article' with 'product page' or 'service page.'" [ch. 37]
- Transactional intent pages: "This is why your product and service pages are so important. This is why clear calls to action, great page structure, and fast-loading websites make a difference ... The goal here isn't to educate or convince, it's to close. So when you're optimizing for transactional keywords, think clarity, confidence, and ease of use. Make it obvious what you're offering, how much it costs, and how they can get it. At this stage, people don't want to be sold to, they want a solution. Your job is to make sure they don't have to look anywhere else." [ch. 3]
- Example of money/transactional searches to target: "buy noise-canceling headphones," "book dentist appointment near me," "hire plumber" [ch. 3]
- Keyword selection for a service page: primary keyword "plumber Detroit"; secondary options "plumber in Detroit", "plumbing services Detroit MI", "local plumbing companies in Detroit"; "we're going to sneak in through the back door and rank for the secondary keywords first." [ch. 36]
- Keyword research shortcut for a service page: paste a competitor's service page into your keyword tool to see the exact keywords it ranks for ("if you're a dentist and your competitor has a page about teeth whitening...") [ch. 36]
- Service pages get powered by the blog: every HeyTony blog post followed one rule, "Internally link to at least one service page using keyword-rich anchor text. That's it." A year later the service pages ranked #1 for almost all top keywords with no edits. [ch. 9] "Blogging also boosts the performance of the pages that really matter, like your product and service pages." [ch. 2]
- Of the 3-5 in-content internal links on any page, "At least one of those links should go to your product or service pages." [ch. 9]
- Navigation: "Your main navigation menu should feature your highest-priority pages, like your services, products, contact page, or blog." [ch. 9] Nav and footer should link "your main service or product page (the one that lists everything you offer)" [ch. 27]; the home page links directly to each individual service [ch. 27].
- URL subdirectory: /services/ (also /products/, /store/, /shop/) [ch. 6]; good URL example "example.com/website-design", bad "example.com/hamilton-website-designers-web-design-hamilton/" [ch. 6]
- Service pages are high-intent pages: "If someone visits a high-intent page on your website, like a service, product, or pricing page, but doesn't convert, that's your cue" to retarget them with ads that remind, offer a time-sensitive deal, show testimonials, encourage a booking [ch. 47]; email automation: "If they land on your service page but don't fill out your contact form, send a follow-up email: 'Need help deciding? Here's how we can make it easier...'" [ch. 47]
- Schema can carry "service descriptions" and clarifies that a page is a service page [ch. 22].
- Early example of a service site's content brief (bin rental client): "pictures of our bins and trucks. Oh, and diagrams showing the dimensions of each bin. And the types of things people can and can't put in them", built with on-page SEO to rank for "Rubbish Removal"; business grew from 11 bins to over 100 [Part I Prologue].
- Competitor monitoring: Watchdog flags when competitors are "building new service pages" or "Updating pricing" [ch. 34].

## 3. Location / city pages

- The book has no dedicated location-page chapter; everything below is the adjacent guidance it gives.
- Geotargeted keywords: "phrases like 'electrician Windsor,' 'pizza near me,' or 'window cleaning Dallas.' ... usually much easier to rank for because you're not competing against the entire internet, you're only competing locally ... These searches are often commercial or transactional." [ch. 3]
- Footer is where "location-specific content" lives alongside FAQs, policies, support pages [ch. 9].
- The winning service pages in the AC story were "optimized for the city I was in" (Hamilton), i.e. a service plus city page [ch. 40].
- Keyword guessing for a local service: "house painter Houston", "Houston painting services", "interior painters Houston", then validate in a keyword tool [ch. 36]; plumber Detroit primary/secondary set [ch. 36].
- Local ranking happens in two places: "The Map Pack, that box at the top with the map and 3 business listings" and "The organic results, the regular blue links underneath the map"; "Ideally, you want to show up in both." [ch. 18]
- Google Business Profile is "absolutely non-negotiable" for the Map Pack; once verified: "Add your services, pricing, and business description. Set your hours of operation. Include your phone number, website, and address. Upload a menu if you're a restaurant. Choose the most accurate business category." [ch. 18]
- "IMPORTANT: Your Name, Address, and Phone Number (NAP) must be identical across all platforms. No exceptions." [ch. 18]
- Citations/directories (Yelp, Yellow Pages, 411, Foursquare) display name, logo, category, address, phone, hours, website, description; they verify legitimacy, give a backlink, get used by real people, and Yelp reviews are a local ranking signal [ch. 18].
- Reviews: "Reviews alone won't shoot you to the top of the map pack, but without them you'll never rank." Strategy: "Just ask." Never buy fake reviews. [ch. 18]
- Mobile must-haves for local: "Can someone call you with one click? Can they find your contact page or checkout easily?" [ch. 20]
- Import the GBP into Bing Places for Business "in under a minute" [ch. 18, 35].
- Result example: Ahmed's clinic ranks for "doctor near me," "general practitioner Dubai," "medical care in Dubai" at ~8,000 visitors per month [Introduction].

## 4. Product pages (chapter 38 in full detail)

- "all you need to do is what was mentioned in the previous chapter" (the chapter 37 process) plus two product-specific rules [ch. 38].
- Rule 1, "Unique Product Names Are NOT Always Beneficial": "Have you ever heard of the Florpavia couch? No? Exactly. Neither have your potential customers." A stylized name hides the product from search; "mid-century modern sofa" is what people search. [ch. 38]
- Fix proposed to client John: "He could update the product titles to something people are actually searching for, and move his unique product name underneath in the description or subtitle. That way, he could keep the creative name and improve his visibility on Google." John refused, cancelled six months later. [ch. 38]
- "The product title is usually the H1 tag and the title tag on your page. Both are essential for SEO. If your H1 says 'Florpavia' instead of 'Mid-Century Modern Sofa,' you're losing the game before it even starts." [ch. 38]
- "So, yeah, maybe save the fancy names for your packaging. On your site? Call it what it is." [ch. 38]
- Rule 2, "Product Descriptions Don't Have to Be Dull": model Amazon. "At the very top of a product listing, you'll find the essentials: product name, star rating, price, and the 'Buy Now' button. Just below that is the About This Item and Product Details section. These parts don't just add context, they build trust, answer questions, and help customers feel confident clicking 'Add to Cart.'" [ch. 38]
- "Where most businesses go wrong" [ch. 38]:
  1. "Copy-pasting from the manufacturer. Google hates duplicate content. If your product description appears word-for-word on 50 other sites, guess what? You're not going to rank."
  2. "Treating product descriptions like an afterthought. Throwing something together to fill the white space to make your page look more complete."
- "How to Optimize Product Pages": "Find the keyword for your product. Analyze your competitors. Write a helpful, original description that makes you the best result on the page." [ch. 38]
- "Treat your product page like a blog post (sort of)": product pages can use "H2 tags for organizing sections", "Internal links to related products or helpful blogs", "FAQs for common customer questions", "Your own expertise and opinion". [ch. 38]
- Above the fold: "I'm not saying shove 1,000 words to the top of the page. The top section should be clean and conversion-focused: Product photo(s); Product name (with the keyword!); Star rating (if you've got 'em); Price; 'Add to Cart' button." [ch. 38]
- Below the fold: "Everything else can go underneath that. Most visitors may not scroll all the way down, but the ones who do are likely on the fence. Your helpful, well-structured content down there could be the thing that gets them to buy. And for search engines? All that additional context, keyword usage, and structure gives you a huge leg up." [ch. 38]
- Great e-commerce UX: "there's a big green 'Add to Cart' button staring you right in the face. No scrolling. No searching. It's just there." Offer Apple Pay and Google Pay; send an abandoned-cart email ("Finish Checking Out" with a 10% coupon); instant confirmation and tracking; delivery-window message; post-purchase check-in/upsell email. [ch. 21]
- Bad e-commerce UX to avoid: ad lands on home page instead of product; 20 near-identical products so the one from the ad can't be found; no Apple Pay/Google Pay; no follow-up or abandoned-cart reminder [ch. 21].
- Product images can be image links "showcasing a product that clicks through to its detail page"; give them descriptive alt text [ch. 9].
- Product URL pattern: "example.com/product/protein-milk/" where /product/ is the subdirectory [ch. 6].
- Product variants: "Add canonical tags. If you have to keep duplicate or similar pages, you can tell search engines which version is the 'main' one by using canonical tags. You'll see this often on Shopify product pages with small variations." [ch. 25]
- Schema for products: "product pricing, customer reviews" feed rich snippets showing "star ratings, pricing" [ch. 22].
- Retarget product-page visitors who didn't buy; abandoned checkout email: "You left something in your cart" or "Here's 10% off if you complete your order within 24 hours." [ch. 47]
- Platform speed note: on Shopify "The best thing you can do is use next-gen image formats (like .webp)"; hiring a Fiverr speed specialist is the only Fiverr use Matt endorses [ch. 19].
- Amazon ranking is a separate discipline: ranking on Amazon "doesn't necessarily mean those products will rank well on Google" [Introduction].

## 5. Category / collection pages (chapter 39 in full detail)

- "Category pages are easily one of the most underutilized and under-optimized assets on most online stores ... Category pages can rank. And not just rank, they can rank really well." [ch. 39]
- Default layout the book describes: "On the left, you've got filters like brand names, price ranges, star ratings, and other product attributes. On the right, you'll usually see a grid of products that fall under that category. And that's where most people stop." [ch. 39]
- "Category pages are SEO gold. They sit higher in the hierarchy than product pages and usually target shorter, broader keywords, meaning more search volume and more potential traffic. If someone searches 'best coffee machines,' they probably aren't looking for a specific model yet. They want options, comparisons, and guidance. That's exactly what your category page should give them." [ch. 39]
- A thumbnails-only category page misses the chance to: "Rank for big, valuable keywords; Keep users engaged; Funnel visitors into deeper product pages; Build topical authority." [ch. 39]
- H1 choice: "Instead of using a broad, generic keyword like 'coffee machines' as your H1 tag, you can be more strategic and specific. Use a keyword that people are actually searching for, something like 'drip coffee machines' or 'stovetop espresso makers.' These longer-tail variations usually have lower competition but still carry strong search volume." [ch. 39]
- Split mixed categories: "Instead of one generic 'coffee machine' category, create separate pages for: Drip coffee makers; Espresso machines; French presses; Stovetop coffee pots. Give each one its own optimized category page ... More pages. More keywords. More ranking opportunities." [ch. 39]
- "But don't go overboard. You don't want hundreds of nearly identical category pages that overwhelm users or dilute your SEO strategy. Keep it focused. Keep it useful." [ch. 39]
- "How to Optimize Category Pages", verbatim steps [ch. 39]:
  1. "Keyword & Competitive Research. Start with proper keyword research (using Semrush, Ubersuggest, or even ChatGPT for ideas) to identify the best keyword for each category. Then check the top 3 ranking pages to see how they've structured their content and what keywords they're using."
  2. "Write Content Like a Blog Post. Think of your category page as more than just a product grid. It's a page that can rank, so treat it like a blog post: Use your target keyword in the H1 tag. Add H2s below the product grid (e.g., 'How to Choose the Best Drip Coffee Machine'). Include FAQs that real customers ask. Share opinions, tips, or buyer's guides."
  3. "Use Internal Linking. Link out to related blog posts, products, or service pages. For example, link to your 'Coffee Machine Maintenance Guide' or 'How to Brew the Perfect Cup' blog posts."
  4. "Place the Content in the Right Spot. Don't make people scroll through a wall of text just to get to your products. Keep the product grid at the top where people expect it, and place all your optimized content below the product listings. Search engines will still crawl and index it. Users who scroll will find it helpful. And you'll keep your user experience clean and conversion-friendly."
- Payoff: "your category pages can do double-duty: sell products AND bring in organic traffic." [ch. 39]
- URL subdirectory options: /collections/, /shop/, /store/, /products/ [ch. 6].
- Breadcrumbs on category hierarchies: "Products > Dog > Collars > Small Collars" create "a logical internal link structure that signals hierarchy and relevance" [ch. 9].
- Blog category pages: link them from the home page, max 10 categories, so every post is two clicks from home [ch. 27].
- Related don't: robots.txt can keep thin "tag archives" out of the index [ch. 24].

## 6. About / team pages

- Nothing in the book specifically about building an About or Team page. Adjacent guidance:
- Subdirectories the book lists include /about/, /team/, /careers/, /press/ [ch. 6].
- "Photographs are a great way to show that you're a real person behind a real business. Nothing builds trust like images of you, your team, your office, or your happy customers. Photos don't always need to be professionally taken; your phone camera can do the job just fine." [ch. 12]

- E-E-A-T: "it's my personal stories, case studies, and real-world experience that make it unique and genuinely helpful. These elements not only make the content more engaging but also build trust, showing that I've actually lived what I'm teaching." [ch. 2]

## 7. Contact / quote / booking pages

- The contact page belongs in the main navigation: "Your main navigation menu should feature your highest-priority pages, like your services, products, contact page, or blog." [ch. 9]
- Nav and footer must include "a contact page (because people need a way to reach you)" [ch. 27].
- Mobile checklist items: "Can someone call you with one click? Can they find your contact page or checkout easily?" [ch. 20]
- CTA buttons ("Book a Call," "Schedule an Appointment," "Get a Free Quote") are conversion tools, not SEO links: "that kind of text is vague. It doesn't tell search engines anything specific about the page it links to ... Use buttons to guide users toward the next step in their journey, and support those buttons with clear text links nearby when it makes sense for SEO." [ch. 9]
- Service page CTA rule: "Tell people what to do next. Book a call? Fill out a form? Don't leave them guessing." [ch. 40]
- Free quotes count as a lead magnet ("Checklists, Cheatsheets, Ebooks, Discount codes, Free quotes, Digital products, Courses") [ch. 46]; sticky banners on product or service sites offer "something like '10% off your first order' or 'Get a free quote.'" [ch. 46]
- If someone lands on a service page "but don't fill out your contact form," trigger a follow-up email [ch. 47].
- Social "link in bio" can point to "your website, a contact page, or a lead magnet" [ch. 17].
- Subdirectory /contact/ [ch. 6]; Matt's rebuilt site structure explicitly includes "a contact page" [ch. 9].
- GBP reports "How many calls, messages, or bookings you're getting directly from Google" [ch. 35].

## 8. Pricing pages

- Nav/footer "can also include links to pricing, case studies, or anything else that directly supports conversions." [ch. 27]
- A pricing page is a high-intent page: "If someone visits a high-intent page on your website, like a service, product, or pricing page, but doesn't convert," retarget them [ch. 47].
- Pricing on service pages: "What it costs (or a general price range) (If you're not ready to list prices, at least address the cost.)" [ch. 40]; the chosen HVAC company listed "Hourly rates and pricing info" [ch. 40].
- Transactional pages: "Make it obvious what you're offering, how much it costs, and how they can get it." [ch. 3]
- "Spotify pricing" is the book's example of a navigational keyword, i.e. people search brand + pricing [ch. 3].
- Schema can expose "product pricing" in rich snippets [ch. 22]; GBP should include "services, pricing" [ch. 18].
- Competitors "Updating pricing" is one of the changes Watchdog tracks [ch. 34].

## 9. FAQ pages and FAQ blocks

- FAQ section on every service page: "Common questions (Yes, add an FAQ section.)" [ch. 40]
- FAQs on product pages: "FAQs for common customer questions" [ch. 38]
- FAQs on category pages: "Include FAQs that real customers ask." [ch. 39]
- A standalone FAQ page lives in the footer: "Your footer ... is where you can link to secondary, but still important, pages. Things like frequently asked questions (FAQs), policies, support pages, or location-specific content live here." [ch. 9]
- Question-shaped headings win snippets: "When your headings align with commonly searched questions or phrases, they can boost your chances of landing in rich snippets (aka rich results) or AI-generated overviews in search results. And that kind of visibility? It can be a game-changer, putting your page front and center, even above the traditional #1 spot." [ch. 7]
- Schema "Clarifies what kind of page it is: Whether it's a blog post, product page, service page, recipe, or FAQ"; rich snippets can show "FAQs" [ch. 22, Glossary].
- Where the questions come from: Google "People also ask" (click a question then close it to load more) [ch. 36]; AnswerThePublic (3 free searches per day) [ch. 36]; "questions you hear from your customers ... If a customer asks you a question in person, on social media, via email, or in a sales call, that's a topic worth writing about." [ch. 36]
- Marcus Sheridan's model: answer the questions customers actually ask ("How much does a fiberglass pool cost?", "Fiberglass vs. concrete pools, which is better?", "What are the problems with fiberglass pools?") [ch. 2].
- Record yourself answering "the questions your customers ask you all the time," transcribe, and turn into content [ch. 14].

## 10. Blog posts and content pages (chapter 37 and chapter 2 in full detail)

What a page's content must be (chapter 2):

- "High-quality content is well written, easy to understand, and effortless to skim, making it simple for users to find the information they need. It provides the most complete, in-depth coverage of a specific topic while incorporating personal experience and expertise. Most importantly, high-quality content doesn't just inform, it solves a problem." [ch. 2]
- "Your content should do one of two things: answer a question or solve a problem. Don't just fill space." [ch. 2]
- Helpful content = be the go-to person; example topic "The best grass for dogs" should cover pros and cons of grass types, which are safe to eat, how each handles urine and waste, which recover from digging, breeds with grass issues, drought-resistant grasses, preventing yellow spots [ch. 2].
- Don't cram it all into one piece: "a better approach is to mention related topics briefly and link to other content that dives deeper into those topics ... these practices are called topical mapping and internal linking." [ch. 2]
- Weave in personal experience: "Who would you trust more? Someone who just rewrites existing articles about dog-friendly grass OR Someone who's spent 20 years working with dogs and different types of grass" [ch. 2]
- "High-quality helpful content keeps users on your page longer, signaling to search engines that your information is valuable." "Longer content that really digs into a topic typically ranks higher." [ch. 2]
- Blogging cadence and payoff examples: Marcus Sheridan "pumping out 2-3 blog posts per week" for 18 months [ch. 2]; Amy's flower shop went from 1,000 to 9,000 monthly visitors in 8 months "primarily through one thing: blog posts" [ch. 2]; HeyTony published "several times per week" [ch. 9]; a client got "eight blog posts or page updates per month and building four backlinks" and went from 100 to 10,000 monthly visitors in six months [ch. 23]; "Every week. Not every post will get traffic or earn backlinks, but if you don't publish, you'll never know which one could." [ch. 16]
- "For most websites, blog posts are the primary driver of visitors. They're the front door." [ch. 2] Blogging builds topical authority and "boosts the performance of the pages that really matter, like your product and service pages." [ch. 2]
- Word count: "While longer content often performs better, there's no magic number ... I've seen pages with just 400 words outrank pages that are double or triple that length. My general rule? Aim to have slightly more content than the page currently ranking #1. There's no need to go overboard and tack on an extra thousand words, just focus on being a little more helpful and complete than your competition." [ch. 2]
- Readability: "It's dumb not to dumb it down." Use everyday language (the plastic surgeons dropped "subglandular placement" and "capsular contracture" for what patients search). Measure with Flesch Reading Ease / Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level; Yoast flags complexity before publishing. "The goal? Write so clearly that even a 12-year-old could understand it." [ch. 2]
- AI: "If you're asking ChatGPT to 'write a blog post about the benefits of a high-protein diet' and then copy-pasting the result straight onto your website, don't expect great rankings." Use AI "to brainstorm, structure, expand on your original ideas, or clarify your message" and weave in "real-world stories and insights." [ch. 2]

Keyword choice for posts (chapters 3 and 36):

- Informational keywords ("how to file taxes as a freelancer," "what is a root canal") are "where blog content becomes your secret weapon" [ch. 3]; commercial keywords ("best CRM for small businesses," "Nike vs. Adidas running shoes") suit "comparison posts, buyer's guides, and even blog posts answering 'which is better?' questions" [ch. 3].
- Favor evergreen topics ("how to change a tire," "how to write a business plan"); HeyTony's 2023 post "Facebook Reels Not Showing? Here's How To Fix It." has brought over 82,000 visitors. Keep evergreen posts updated "now and then." [ch. 3]
- Long-tail keywords (3+ words) also rank you for the short-tail terms inside them ("gluten-free sourdough garlic bread" covers "bread," "garlic bread," "sourdough") [ch. 3].
- Target low-volume keywords of "10 to 100 searches per month"; 2 posts per week at ~50 searches each = ~400 potential searches per month, ~4,800 monthly after 12 months; authority unlocks competitive terms "around the 6-9 month mark" (the avalanche technique) [ch. 36].
- "Pro tip: Some of these related keywords could be entire topics on their own, which you can elaborate in detail on separate pages or posts." [ch. 3]

The chapter 37 process, step by step:

- Mini content roadmap: reverse-engineer the #1 page. "We're going to analyze a few key elements of the top-ranking page, including: Keyword density; Word count; Where and how often they use the keyword; Page speed; Backlinks and referring domains." [ch. 37]
- Spreadsheet: "First column = Metric you're tracking; Second column = Your page; Third column = Top-ranking competitor's page" [ch. 37]
- Tools: "An SEO Chrome extension (like the HeyTony Page Audit Extension); A keyword tool like Semrush or Ubersuggest; HeyTony Content Analyzer; Google PageSpeed Insights (https://pagespeed.web.dev) for loading performance" [ch. 37]
- "If you don't have access to a paid SEO tool, you can skip backlinks and referring domains for now ... If your competitor has even a few backlinks, you'll need to work on getting some too." [ch. 37]
- How to find the metrics: "Keyword Usage: CMD+F (Mac) or CTRL+F (Windows), paste the keyword to see how many times they're using it on the page. Title Tag, Meta Description, H1, H2, word count: HeyTony Page Audit Extension. Keyword Usage: ... Content Analyzer (it's free) ... https://heytony.ca/content-analyzer/. Referring domains / backlinks: Semrush or Ubersuggest. Page Speed: PageSpeed Insights" [ch. 37]
- Creating an outline, verbatim steps [ch. 37]:
  1. "Open the top 3-5 ranking pages for the keyword you're targeting."
  2. "Copy all of their heading tags (H1s, H2s, H3s, etc.) into a blank document."
  3. "Do this for each of the 3-5 pages and combine them into one doc."
  4. "Remove anything irrelevant like 'Related Posts,' newsletter signup prompts, or off-topic headings."
  5. "Insert any relevant secondary keywords as H2s or H3s, but only if they make sense within the context."
  6. "Rewrite and reword the headings so they're not identical to the competition. You don't want Google flagging your content as duplicate."
- "What you're left with is a skeleton outline ... This should take you no more than 5-10 minutes. From here, all you have to do is fill in the blanks." [ch. 37]
- Drafting Step 1, gather competitor content: copy each of the top 3-5 pages (CMD+A / CTRL+A), paste each into ChatGPT and ask it to summarize ("we're just training the AI to understand what's already out there") [ch. 37]
- Step 2, paste your outline and prompt: "Write an article based on this outline." (swap "article" for "product page" or "service page"). "Important: Don't copy and paste this AI-generated draft directly to your website. It still needs your voice, your expertise, and your human touch." [ch. 37]
- Step 3, add experience: "Add real-world examples or case studies manually, or... Film yourself talking through the topic, share personal stories, or explain client wins. Then paste the transcript into the same AI conversation." Prompts: "Summarize this transcript and include my experiences in the final article." then "Write the article based on this outline. Make sure to include my personal stories, experiences, and case studies from the transcript." [ch. 37]
- Step 4, final touches, verbatim [ch. 37]:
  - "Make sure your H1 includes the target keyword."
  - "Ensure at least one H2 includes the keyword."
  - "Fact-check the content."
  - "Add images, internal links, and external sources where appropriate."
  - "Double-check your roadmap to make sure you're using the keyword enough times and in the right places. And don't overdo it; if your competitors are using the keyword 7 times, use it 8 times."
- Video-first drafting: record client/team calls (with permission) or talk to camera answering common customer questions; get a transcript (upload to YouTube, it auto-transcribes); feed to ChatGPT; then "enhance it by adding heading tags, images, and video"; embed the original video in the post ("a full-circle content strategy"); reuse the transcript for social hooks and captions [ch. 14].

Structure and layout of a post:

- One H1 (mirrors the title tag, keyword near the start, under 60 characters); at least 2 H2s with keyword/variation; H3s only if depth requires; sequential hierarchy [ch. 7]. If you need H4-H6, "it might be a sign that your content is getting too dense for one page. In that case, consider creating a separate piece of content." [ch. 7]
- Table of contents: bullets "as navigational elements by internally linking each point to a relevant section further down the page. You'll often see this in a table of contents located at the top of a blog post." [ch. 11]
- Break walls of text with bullets and numbered lists; numbered lists for step-by-step processes; listicle titles like "10 Must-Try Excursions in Barbados" or "5 Easy Weeknight Dinners You Can Make in Under 30 Minutes" are "simple, scannable, and incredibly effective" [ch. 11].
- Featured image: "This image usually appears at the top of the page, either just below or layered behind your H1 tag. It's also the image that shows up when someone shares your page on social media." A post about Gluten-Free Sourdough Garlic Bread should open with "a mouth-watering photo of that bread right at the top." [ch. 12]
- In-content images "sprinkled throughout ... each image should serve a purpose"; "Aim to include one image for every 200 words of content." [ch. 12] Custom graphics, screenshots (proof of results, step-by-step), charts, infographics [ch. 12].
- Embed one video where helpful; Google indexes one video per page [ch. 14].
- In-content lead magnet "between paragraphs can be very effective if it's related to the blog post or page"; a blog sidebar lead magnet is "Not very effective. People have developed banner blindness." [ch. 46]
- Internal links: 3-5 per post, descriptive anchors, at least one to a product/service page [ch. 9]; from each new post link to three other relevant pages and link to it from three existing pages [ch. 27] (or 2-3 out and update 2-3 older posts, "especially effective because those older posts are already indexed") [ch. 28].
- External links: at least one per post, in the content, to a study/report/tool that backs a claim; never with your target keyword as anchor text [ch. 10].
- URL: /blog/ subdirectory, e.g. "example.com/blog/health-benefits-of-protein-milk/" [ch. 6].
- Title tag can be more clickbaity for social than for search; Yoast lets you set a separate social title ("NASA will pay you $18,000 to stay in bed for 70 straight days" vs "NASA is looking for participants for their Bed Rest Studies") [ch. 4].
- After publishing: share on "platforms that support link sharing, like Facebook, Twitter, Reddit, and Pinterest." [ch. 17] Infographics are "highly shareable, especially on platforms like Pinterest, and can help drive additional traffic and even earn backlinks." [ch. 12]
- Uniqueness test between posts: "Can You Bring an Apple Watch in a Hot Tub?" and "Can You Bring an iPhone into a Hot Tub?" are fine (different products); "5 Top Restaurants in Hamilton" and "The 5 Best Restaurants in Hamilton, Ontario" are duplicates [ch. 25].
- Never delete a high-traffic post on a whim (see chapter 42 section); update it instead [ch. 42].
- Old content that gets emailed each year (a spring maintenance checklist post written years ago) still drives traffic and signals legitimacy [ch. 17].

## 11. Portfolio / gallery / case-study pages

- Case studies belong in nav/footer when they support conversions: "You can also include links to pricing, case studies, or anything else that directly supports conversions." [ch. 27]
- Service pages should show "Testimonials, before / after results, case studies, logos, reviews, etc." and internally link "to related blog posts, case studies, or even other services." [ch. 40]

- Don't bloat galleries: on Wix/Squarespace "Don't overload your pages with unnecessary videos, animations, or massive galleries." [ch. 19]
- Image rules apply (webp, under 250KB, 1600px full-width, 800px in-content, descriptive alt text) [ch. 12, 13].

## 12. Testimonials / reviews pages

- No dedicated reviews-page guidance. What the book says:
- Put reviews on the pages that sell: service pages list "Glowing customer reviews" and "Testimonials ... reviews" [ch. 40]; product pages show "Star rating (if you've got 'em)" in the top section [ch. 38].
- Schema can carry "customer reviews" and rich snippets "show star ratings" [ch. 22].
- Retargeting ads should "Share testimonials or social proof to reduce hesitation." [ch. 47]
- Collect reviews continuously and honestly: "Just ask ... Text them. Email them. Ask them while they're still smiling after a good experience." "don't buy fake reviews. That can tank your rankings or get you banned from Google altogether." [ch. 18]
- Photos of "your happy customers" build trust [ch. 12].

## 13. Legal pages (privacy, terms, cookies, disclaimers)

- Nothing in the book about writing legal pages. Placement only: "policies" and "support pages" go in the footer, not the main menu [ch. 9].
- Low-value/private pages can be kept out of the index via robots.txt ("tag archives, 'thank you' pages, checkout flows, etc."), but "Just leave it alone" unless you know what you're doing [ch. 24].
- Ownership note (not a page rule, but a site rule): "If you paid for a website, you should own that website" and host it on your own server [ch. 25].

## 14. Landing pages, thank-you, 404 pages, sitemap pages, any other page type

- Landing pages (funnel pages): "standalone pages designed specifically for marketing or ad campaigns. People don't stumble across them in search; they're sent via email, ads, or social media. Landing pages have one job: convert. Whether it's signing up for a webinar, downloading a guide, or making a purchase, they're laser-focused on a single call-to-action. That's why they typically don't include internal links or navigation, distractions kill conversions." [ch. 28]
- "Landing pages are orphan pages. And in this case, that's perfectly fine ... Landing pages may be terrible for search rankings, but they're amazing for driving leads and sales." [ch. 28]
- "If you're using landing pages, consider excluding them from your sitemap and disallowing them in your robots.txt file. You'll avoid confusing search engines and keep the focus where it belongs, on conversions." [ch. 28]
- Glossary: "Funnel pages: Web pages designed for each step of the funnel (landing, checkout, thank you)"; "Landing page: A focused page designed to capture leads or drive a single action." [Glossary]
- Video also works on landing pages [ch. 14].
- Thank-you pages and checkout flows: robots.txt can "Prevent indexing of low-value or private pages, like tag archives, 'thank you' pages, checkout flows, etc." [ch. 24]
- Checkout UX: Apple Pay and Google Pay, cart preserved, discount auto-applied, "Five seconds later: Purchase complete", instant confirmation email and tracking [ch. 21]; abandoned checkout email within 24 hours [ch. 47].
- 404 pages: "Landing on a 404 page when you're looking for something is super frustrating. It's a dead end, and users (and search engines) don't like dead ends." Fix causes (deleted/renamed pages without redirects; external pages removed) with 301 redirects, swaps, or removal [ch. 26]. "301 redirect: A permanent redirect from one URL to another, preserving SEO value." [Glossary]
- Sitemap page: lives at yoursite.com/sitemap.xml (Yoast: /sitemap_index.xml); auto-generated by WordPress, Shopify, Wix, Squarespace; Yoast builds "a clean, well-structured sitemap"; lists last-updated date, alternative language versions, page hierarchy; submit once in GSC under Indexing > Sitemaps; never submit individual URLs there [ch. 23, 29].
- Account/utility pages: subdirectories the book lists include /login/, /signup/, /account/, /dashboard/, /profile/ [ch. 6].
- Lead-magnet destination: social "link in bio" can send people to a lead magnet [ch. 17]; lead magnets are "Checklists, Cheatsheets, Ebooks, Discount codes, Free quotes, Digital products, Courses" and should cost nothing to fulfill [ch. 46].
- Microsites: one-page ad-adjacent microsites "I don't recommend this strategy" [ch. 16].
- Staging rule: don't create a Google Search Console account until the site is fully launched, or "Google will index a half-finished product" [ch. 29].
- Forum/user-generated pages: every forum post and reply is its own URL (the 400,000-backlink footer story), so a site-wide footer link multiplies into hundreds of thousands of backlinks from one domain [ch. 16].

## 15. Cross-page elements: header/navigation, footer, sidebars, pop-ups, email capture boxes, sticky bars, breadcrumbs

Header / main navigation:

- "Navigational links are the ones that appear in your main menu and footer, showing up on every page of your website. These links send a strong signal to Google (and your visitors) about which pages are the most important." [ch. 9]
- "Your main navigation menu should feature your highest-priority pages, like your services, products, contact page, or blog. These are the pages that drive the most action, so they deserve top placement." [ch. 9]
- HeyTony crawl-depth rule: "Start with your navigation and footer. The most important pages on your site should be linked there. This usually includes your main service or product page (the one that lists everything you offer), your blog (to give access to all your content), and a contact page (because people need a way to reach you). You can also include links to pricing, case studies, or anything else that directly supports conversions." [ch. 27]
- A lead magnet can sit in the nav: "a puppy training academy might have a tab that says 'New Owner Checklist.'" [ch. 46]
- Never put external links in the navigation menu or footer: external links go "Inside your content only, never in your navigation menu or footer." [ch. 10]
- Don't hide the blog: "'Can we link to the blog but hide it so people can't find it?' If that's you, don't." [ch. 27]
- Glossary: "Header: The top section of a web page, usually with logo and navigation." [Glossary]

Footer:

- "Your footer ... is where you can link to secondary, but still important, pages. Things like frequently asked questions (FAQs), policies, support pages, or location-specific content live here. It's your chance to keep key pages accessible without crowding the top of your site." [ch. 9]
- Footer must also carry the essentials from the crawl-depth rule (main service/product page, blog, contact) [ch. 27].
- Footer credit links replicate on every page; a "Website built by HeyTony" footer credit on a client's forum site produced ~400,000 backlinks from one domain and a 61% spam score; Google appears to count only "the first 5 to 10" links from one domain [ch. 16].
- Glossary: "Footer: The bottom section of a web page, usually with links and contact info." [Glossary]

Breadcrumbs:

- "Breadcrumbs are those little navigation trails you often see at the top of a page, just under the main menu, which show users exactly where they are on your site and how they got there. Breadcrumbs are kind of like a GPS for your website." Example: "Products > Dog > Collars > Small Collars". "From an SEO perspective, they also create a logical internal link structure that signals hierarchy and relevance to search engines." [ch. 9]

Sidebars:

- "Blog sidebar. Not very effective. People have developed banner blindness." [ch. 46]

Pop-ups:

- "Popups. Exit intent popups are most effective. Can appear from bottom left or right without blocking content. Only appear when a user is about to leave the website." [ch. 46]

Email capture boxes and lead magnets:

- "Your first thought might be to add a widget that says 'Subscribe to Newsletter' on your website. Unfortunately, that doesn't work anymore. You need to give people a good reason to give you their email address." [ch. 46]
- Placement list, verbatim [ch. 46]: "1. Blog sidebar. Not very effective. People have developed banner blindness. 2. In content. Placing your lead magnet signup between paragraphs can be very effective if it's related to the blog post or page. 3. Navigation menu. You can sometimes find lead magnets directly in a website's navigation menu. For example, a puppy training academy might have a tab that says 'New Owner Checklist.' 4. Popups. Exit intent popups are most effective. Can appear from bottom left or right without blocking content. Only appear when a user is about to leave the website. 4. Sticky banners. Often seen on product or service sites. Offer something like '10% off your first order' or 'Get a free quote.' Warning: Do NOT have both top and bottom sticky banners at once." (The book numbers both pop-ups and sticky banners as item 4.)
- Lead magnet rule: "give away what other people charge for"; but "do not work for free or get into a discount race to the bottom." [ch. 46]

Sticky bars:

- "Sticky banners. Often seen on product or service sites. Offer something like '10% off your first order' or 'Get a free quote.' Warning: Do NOT have both top and bottom sticky banners at once." [ch. 46]

Other repeated elements:

- Buttons are CTAs ("Book a Call," "Schedule an Appointment," "Get a Free Quote"), not keyword links; pair them with descriptive text links nearby [ch. 9].
- Home page carousels/sliders: remove, replace with one clear image and a headline [ch. 20].
- Recent-posts widget on the home page (5-10 posts) [ch. 27].
- Featured image field on every page/post; it doubles as the social share image [ch. 12].
- Table of contents at the top of long posts [ch. 11].
- Plugins: "too many plugins can seriously slow down your site. Go through your plugins and remove anything you're not using." A plugin injecting 2,000 hidden words on every page caused a duplicate-content stall for five months [ch. 19, 25].
- Canva templates: "If you rely too heavily on Canva's default templates without customizing them, your content is going to look like everyone else's." [ch. 33]

## 16. Per-element rules for every page

### Title tags

- "Think of a title tag as the headline of your web page. It's the first thing people see when your site shows up in Google search results. (It's the clickable blue text ...)" Also shown on social shares and in browser tabs. [ch. 4]
- Character count: "Aim for around 60 characters (that's roughly 8-15 words, depending on how big those words are). Why 60? Because Google typically cuts off anything longer than that." [ch. 4]
- Keyword placement: "Always try to place your main keyword (the term you're trying to rank for) as close to the start of your title tag as possible." [ch. 4]
- Uniqueness: "Every title tag on your website should be unique ... Using the same title tag on multiple pages is a big no-no ... That can lead to both pages underperforming." [ch. 4]
- Tone: "Title tags for social media can be a bit more clickbaity than SEO title tags, which are typically more factual. If you use a WordPress plugin like Yoast, you can actually have two title tags: one for search and a different one that appears on social media." [ch. 4]
- Why it ranks you: "A compelling, keyword-rich title tag attracts more clicks. And here's the magic: The more people click your link (instead of your competitors'), the stronger the signal you send to Google that your content is valuable." [ch. 4]
- "Your title tag is what screams, 'Choose me instead of the other person!'" [ch. 4]
- Product pages: "The product title is usually the H1 tag and the title tag on your page." Use the searched name, not the invented one. [ch. 38]
- Service pages: keyword in "The title tag and meta description" [ch. 40].
- Pull competitors' title tags with the HeyTony Page Audit Extension during the mini audit [ch. 37]; Yoast lets you "easily update your title tags and meta descriptions" [ch. 32]; BrightLocal flags missing title tags [ch. 35].

### Meta descriptions

- "The meta description is the short snippet of text that appears directly below the title tag in search results ... Think of it as your second (and final) chance to convince someone that your link is the best one on the page." [ch. 5]
- Length: "You've got between 80 characters and about 156 characters." [ch. 5]
- Content: "Just clearly tell people exactly what they can expect on your page." Example for "The Best Candle Wax for Mosquitoes": "Mosquitoes hate this candle wax. Here are the waxes that work best and why these candles are a must-have for outdoor nights." [ch. 5]
- Keyword: "it helps to include your primary keyword near the beginning of your meta description. However, don't stress too much about using the exact keyword every single time. Google doesn't directly factor meta description keywords into rankings." [ch. 5]
- "Google sometimes ignores your carefully crafted meta description altogether, opting instead to create its own based on what it finds on your page." [ch. 5]
- Uniqueness: "If you reuse the same meta description across multiple pages, you risk confusing your visitors and sending mixed signals to Google. In fact, search engines may ignore one of your pages altogether, thinking it's a duplicate." [ch. 5]
- Also appears on Facebook, LinkedIn, Twitter shares [ch. 5]. Higher CTR from a good description "helps your page rank higher" [ch. 5].

### URLs

- "URLs, also known as links, slugs, or permalinks ... every page on your website needs a unique URL" [ch. 6]
- Length: browsers allow up to 2,048 characters "But just because you can make it that long, doesn't mean you should!" Shorter URLs are "Easier for users to understand and remember; More search engine friendly; Less likely to break when shared; Just plain nicer to look at" [ch. 6]
- Keywords: "Every page's URL should include the relevant keyword, except for your home page ... For example, if you have a page about chocolate chip cookies, your URL could be something like: yourdomain.com/chocolate-chip-cookies/" [ch. 6]
- Don't stuff: "yourdomain.com/chocolate-chip-cookies-cookies-with-chocolate-chips-the-best-chocolate-chip-cookies" is keyword stuffing; "Keyword stuffing doesn't work. In fact, it can actually hurt your rankings." [ch. 6]
- "Not-So-Good URL: example.com/hamilton-website-designers-web-design-hamilton/ ... Good URL: example.com/website-design ... short, descriptive, and includes the necessary keyword." [ch. 6]
- Subdirectories: "Think of subdirectories like folders ... example.com/product/protein-milk/ ... example.com/blog/health-benefits-of-protein-milk/ ... One is for buying something and the other is for learning something." [ch. 6]
- Common subdirectories listed: /products/, /services/, /store/, /shop/, /collections/, /blog/, /articles/, /resources/, /guides/, /news/, /about/, /team/, /careers/, /contact/, /press/, /login/, /signup/, /account/, /dashboard/, /profile/ [ch. 6]
- "The only real guideline I'll give you is this: Use subdirectories to clearly describe the section of your website they belong to ... don't use subdirectories as a way to cram in extra keywords." [ch. 6]
- Changing or deleting a URL without a 301 redirect creates broken links [ch. 26]; when merging pages "use a 301 redirect from the page that's not ranking" [ch. 25]; during the Charlie rebuild they "Preserved old high-ranking URLs and content" [ch. 25].
- "Keyword stuffing in the URLs" was one of the "classic outdated tactics" found on Charlie's old site [ch. 25].

### Headings H1-H6

- "Heading tags are one of the most valuable, and yet most underused and misused, elements in SEO ... Without proper headings, your page becomes harder to read, harder to skim, and harder to understand." [ch. 7]
- H1: "Every page on your website should have one, and only one, H1 tag. It often mirrors your title tag ... usually the biggest, boldest text on the page ... If you have more than one H1 tag, it may confuse search engines." Best practices: "Only use ONE H1 tag per page. Try to keep them under 60 characters. Use your keyword toward the beginning." [ch. 7]
- H2: "a good guideline is to include at least 2 per page. Each H2 should introduce a key section of your content and ideally include a variation or subtopic related to the keyword in your H1 tag." Best practices: "You can and should use multiple H2 tags on every page. No strict character limit, but keep them concise. Use the keywords you're trying to rank for somewhere in your H2 tags; they don't necessarily need to be at the beginning." [ch. 7]
- H3: "H3 tags support your H2s ... Unlike H1s and H2s, H3 tags don't need to include your main keyword or a variation, they're more about structure than SEO." Best practices: "You don't necessarily need to use H3 tags. No character limit, but keep them concise! You don't necessarily need to include keywords you're trying to rank for. Your H3 tags support what your H2 tags are talking about." [ch. 7]
- H4-H6: "less common ... not as essential as your H1 through H3 tags when it comes to SEO impact." "pro tip: If you find yourself needing to go that deep into subtopics, it might be a sign that your content is getting too dense for one page. In that case, consider creating a separate piece of content." [ch. 7]
- Hierarchy: "Always use them in sequential order ... jumping straight from an H1 to an H3 ... you're skipping important context ... That said, you can move backwards ... if you're in the middle of a section using H3 tags and start a brand-new topic, it's perfectly fine to go back to an H2." [ch. 7]
- Question-style headings raise the chance of rich snippets and AI overviews [ch. 7].
- Outline method: copy competitor H1/H2/H3s, combine, strip "Related Posts"/newsletter headings, add secondary keywords as H2/H3, reword so they are not identical [ch. 37]. Final check: H1 has the keyword, at least one H2 has it [ch. 37].
- Category page: keyword in H1; H2s below the product grid [ch. 39]. Product page: product name with keyword is the H1 [ch. 38]; "H2 tags for organizing sections" [ch. 38]. Service page: keyword in H1 and at least one H2 [ch. 40].
- BrightLocal flags missing H1 and H2 tags [ch. 35].

### Word count and content length

- "Aim to have slightly more content than the page currently ranking #1 ... no need to go overboard and tack on an extra thousand words" [ch. 2]; "I've seen pages with just 400 words outrank pages that are double or triple that length." [ch. 2]
- "If their page has 1,000 words, write 1,100." [ch. 41]
- Word count is one of the five mini-roadmap metrics; read it off the competitor with the Page Audit Extension [ch. 37].
- Too thin is a reason Google refuses to index: "The content is too thin (not helpful or too short)." [ch. 41]
- Don't dump length above the fold on product pages: "I'm not saying shove 1,000 words to the top of the page." [ch. 38]
- QA: "If we publish 1,500 words, we double-check to see that the word count on the page matches the word count in the document where we write the content." (a plugin had injected 2,000 hidden words per page) [ch. 25]
- "Thin content with absurdly high keyword density" was an outdated tactic flagged in an audit [ch. 25].
- A free manual audit assumes "one page per day and spending around an hour per page" for a site under 100 pages, about 3 months [ch. 30].

### Keyword density and placement

- Definition: "how often you use your focus keyword ... compared to the total word count on your page." "There's no magic number or perfect percentage, but here's a general rule: Don't overdo it ... That's called keyword stuffing, and Google hates it." [ch. 3]
- Quick trick: "Go to Google and search for the keyword you're targeting. Open up the top three results. Check how many words each page has ... hit CMD+F (or CTRL+F on Windows) and type in your keyword ... if the #1 result has 1,000 words and uses the keyword 10 times, their keyword density is 1% (10 ÷ 1,000). Your goal? Aim for a slightly higher keyword density, maybe use the keyword one or two extra times." [ch. 3]
- "if your competitors are using the keyword 7 times, use it 8 times." [ch. 37]; "If they use the keyword 5 times, aim for 6, not 16." [ch. 41]
- Hidden text (keyword in background color) "does NOT work anymore... so don't even think about it!" [ch. 3]
- Placement: "include your target keyword in your title tag, meta description, H1 tag, at least one H2 tag, and naturally throughout the body of your page." [ch. 3] Plus the URL [ch. 6, 40] and alt text on 1-2 images [ch. 13].
- Variations: write naturally and you'll use them ("gluten and dairy free garlic bread", "gluten free cheesy garlic bread", "how to make gluten free garlic bread"); variations help you rank for more searches [ch. 3].
- Choose a primary keyword plus easier secondary keywords; the page can win the secondaries first [ch. 36].
- "Keyword research is the single most important step in the SEO process." Validate every keyword's volume and difficulty in Semrush or Ubersuggest; ChatGPT is for brainstorming only [ch. 31, 36].
- Keyword research methods: competitor domain analysis, competitor page analysis, keyword gap, guessing; free: ChatGPT ("What keywords could this page be optimized for?"), People also ask, AnswerThePublic, customer questions [ch. 36].

### Readability

- "It's dumb not to dumb it down." "The goal? Write so clearly that even a 12-year-old could understand it." [ch. 2]
- Metric: Flesch Reading Ease / Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (e.g. Grade 8); Yoast scores readability and flags complexity before publishing [ch. 2].
- Example: "She exhibited a jubilant demeanor." vs "She looked really happy." [ch. 2]
- Use the words people search, not jargon (plastic surgeon example) [ch. 2]; "What the service is (Use plain English, not jargon.)" [ch. 40]
- Bullets and lists "can positively impact your site's readability score. This, in turn, can influence your search rankings." [ch. 11]
- Images, headings, bullets, video all break up text and aid skimming [ch. 7, 11, 12, 14].
- "A huge part of good SEO is making content so clear and simple that a 12-year-old could understand it." [Introduction]

### Bullets and lists

- "Simple, bullet points and numbered lists ... They break down information into bite-sized, easy-to-digest chunks ... long blocks of text: They're intimidating." [ch. 11]
- Use bullets to highlight key takeaways; example list of grocery-saving tips (plan meals, make a list, don't shop hungry, buy in bulk, compare unit prices, loyalty cards, store brands) [ch. 11].
- Use numbered lists for ordered steps; example five-step budget grocery trip (set budget, check pantry, plan around sales, write detailed list by category, stick to it) [ch. 11].
- Bullets as navigation: "internally linking each point to a relevant section further down the page ... a table of contents located at the top of a blog post." [ch. 11]
- When to use: "If you notice a big chunk of text with several paragraphs and nothing breaking it up, that's a good sign it might be time to add a list ... ask yourself: Would this section be easier to understand if I pulled out the key points?" [ch. 11]
- Not every page needs them: "There's no hard rule, you'll need to use your best judgment." [ch. 11]
- Graphics can "replace bullet points and numbered lists when you want to make your content more engaging" [ch. 12].

### Images (format, size, file names)

- Why: images increase time on page, boost conversion rates, build trust, make content easier to read [ch. 12].
- Frequency: "Aim to include one image for every 200 words of content. Don't treat this as a strict rule, but avoid publishing pages that are all text and no visuals." [ch. 12]
- Format: "Use .webp whenever possible ... If .webp isn't an option, .jpg is your next best bet. Avoid .pngs unless absolutely necessary. They're higher quality, but the large file sizes can slow down your site." [ch. 12] "Think of .webp like a smarter .jpg." [ch. 19]
- Platform caveat: "Squarespace is the only major website builder that doesn't support .webp. So if that's your platform, you'll want to stick with compressed .jpg or .png files." Canva can't export .webp; convert with a free online tool or Photoshop [ch. 12].
- Pixel sizing: "Full-width images (banners, hero sections): 1600px wide max. Half-width images (side-by-side with text): 800px wide max. In-content blog images (within paragraphs): 800px wide max." [ch. 12]
- File size: "Try to keep your images under 250KB each. With .webp, you can often get file sizes under 100KB, even for large images." [ch. 12] A 10MB image can shrink "down to 100KB or less without losing quality" [ch. 19].
- File names: nothing in the book about image file names.
- Custom over stock: "custom visuals, like branded graphics, screenshots, or photos you've taken yourself, make a much bigger impact ... Custom visuals show your audience (and Google) that you know your stuff." Use Canva or ChatGPT to make graphics. [ch. 12]
- "Warning: You can avoid any copyright infringement by using royalty-free, custom, or paid-for stock images." [ch. 12]
- Types to mix: photographs (you, team, office, customers; phone camera is fine), graphics and illustrations, infographics (shareable, earn backlinks), screenshots (how-tos, proof), charts and graphs (data, trends, evidence) [ch. 12].
- Placements: featured image (top, below or behind the H1, used for social shares) and in-content images (each must serve a purpose, not decoration) [ch. 12].
- Image links: weaker than text links but fine for "showcasing a product that clicks through to its detail page"; give them descriptive alt text [ch. 9].
- Speed: "One of the biggest culprits for slow sites? Giant image files. I've seen 10MB images being used." Optimize images on every platform; it is the main lever on Wix, Squarespace, Shopify [ch. 19].
- Ad/hero: one clear image plus headline instead of a slider [ch. 20].
- Glossary: ".jpg: best for photos. .png: supports transparency, often used for logos or graphics. .webp: smaller file sizes than .jpg / .png." [Glossary]

### Alt text

- Two purposes: "It describes the image to search engines. It provides context to users relying on screen readers." Shows when an image fails to load. [ch. 13]
- "some people try to use that field as a place to sneak in extra keywords. Don't do that. It's called keyword stuffing ... they'll ignore and maybe even penalize you for it." [ch. 13]
- "treat alt text like a quick, helpful explanation of what the image actually shows. If you can include a relevant keyword naturally, great, but make clarity the priority." [ch. 13]
- Alt text drives Google Image Search traffic [ch. 13].
- Crafting rule: "Clearly identify the medium (photo, illustration, graphic). Describe what's in the image. Include relevant details that might help with the search." [ch. 13]
- Example: "Bad alt text: 'pancakes,' too vague and ineffective. Better alt text: 'stack of blueberry pancakes in a kitchen,' provides more detail and context. Best alt text: 'illustration of a stack of blueberry pancakes in a kitchen,' specifies the medium and gives complete context." [ch. 13]
- "Pro tip: Add your focus keyword as alt text in 1-2 images when possible." [ch. 13]
- Image links need descriptive alt text so search engines understand the link [ch. 9].

### Video

- "unlike other on-page elements, it's not required, but when done well, it can be incredibly powerful ... video has one major advantage none of those other elements do: It keeps people on your website longer." [ch. 14]
- Embed, never upload: "Embedding a video means taking a small piece of code from YouTube (or another video hosting platform) and adding it to your website ... uploading the video directly to your website. And I'll say this clearly, don't do it." Uploading eats bandwidth, slows load, hurts mobile and rankings. Use "YouTube, Vimeo, or Loom." [ch. 14]
- "Google indexes one video per page. That means even if it's an embedded YouTube video, Google can pull that video into search results, giving that page an extra chance to show up." [ch. 14]
- You may embed other creators' videos if they help the user, but that helps their video rank, not yours; "whenever possible, it's better to include your own video." [ch. 14]
- Production bar: "Clear visuals and clear audio. That's all your viewer really needs." Phone on a tripod is enough; Loom screen recordings for digital topics [ch. 14].
- Works on home pages, blog posts, landing pages [ch. 14].
- Don't overload pages with unnecessary videos or animations (speed) [ch. 19].
- Video-to-blog workflow: record, transcribe via YouTube, draft with AI, add headings/images, embed the video back into the post [ch. 14].

### Anchor text

- "Anchor text is the clickable text on a website. It's usually blue and underlined ... used to give users and search engines context about the page it's linking to." [ch. 8]
- "should always be descriptive and relevant to the page you're linking to." Avoid "click here," "learn more," "read more." Example: link "how to choose the right running shoes," not "read more." [ch. 8]
- Length: "keep your anchor text between 1 and 5 words." [ch. 8]
- "Be specific, not vague ... If your anchor text says 'chocolate cake recipe,' it better link to a page with an actual chocolate cake recipe." [ch. 8]
- "Don't link full paragraphs or sentences ... Just link the relevant words that describe where the user is going." [ch. 8]
- Internal links: "If you use a relevant keyword as your anchor text (ideally one the linked page is targeting), you're helping Google understand and rank that page better." [ch. 9]; "Internally link to at least one service page using keyword-rich anchor text." [ch. 9]
- External links: "Avoid using your target keyword as anchor text ... If you're trying to rank for 'National Flower of Canada,' don't link to a Wikipedia article using that exact phrase ... use neutral anchor text like 'source' or 'according to Wikipedia.'" [ch. 10]
- Button text ("Book a Call") is vague by nature; treat buttons as CTAs, not keyword links [ch. 9].

### Internal links

- Definition and value: "Internal linking is when you link from one page on your website to another page on your site ... one of the most overlooked, underutilized, and ridiculously simple SEO tactics." [ch. 9]
- Four types: "1. Text links (anchor text)" (most effective), "2. Image links" (need alt text), "3. Buttons" (CTAs, not SEO), "4. Breadcrumbs (navigation paths)" [ch. 9]
- Where: navigational links (menu and footer, on every page, signal importance) and in-content contextual links (e.g. link "website design" in a post to the website design service page) [ch. 9].
- Quantity: "Try to include 3 to 5 internal links on every page (in content), using descriptive keywords as your anchor text ... At least one of those links should go to your product or service pages." [ch. 9]
- "Think of your website as a spiderweb. With each internal link, you're adding another strand." [ch. 9]
- Publishing rule: "Every time we publish a new page or blog post, we link from it to three other relevant pages on the website, and we also link to it from three other existing pages." [ch. 27]
- Orphan-fix version: "link out to 2-3 other relevant pages on your site. Then go back and update 2-3 older posts to link to the new content ... those older posts are already indexed." [ch. 28]
- Crawl depth: most pages reachable "within three clicks from the home page"; "If it takes five or six clicks to access a page, Google assumes it's not that important." [ch. 27]
- Category pages link to related blog posts, products, service pages [ch. 39]; product pages link to related products or helpful blogs [ch. 38]; service pages link to related posts, case studies, other services [ch. 40].
- Unindexed page: "Internally link to that page from other pages on your site that are indexed (especially from pages that get traffic)." [ch. 41]
- Over-optimization: "If their site has a few internal links, add a few more, not 100." [ch. 41]
- Landing pages deliberately have no internal links or navigation [ch. 28].
- Deleting a well-linked page loses the link equity it passed to money pages [ch. 42].
- Watchdog can tell you when competitors "add internal links" [ch. 34].

### External links

- "External links are links that lead from your website to another website. Think of them as references that support your information, data, or claims ... Every single page on your website should link to at least one external source." [ch. 10]
- Where: "Inside your content only, never in your navigation menu or footer. At least once on every page, especially when referencing facts, stats, or tools." [ch. 10]
- "Only link to reputable, relevant sources. Make sure the site is trustworthy, up-to-date, and closely related to the topic." [ch. 10]
- "Don't link to competitors or duplicate content. Never link to another page covering the exact same topic as you; it can send traffic away and weaken your own content's authority." [ch. 10]
- "Avoid using your target keyword as anchor text" for external links [ch. 10].
- Maintenance: if an external link 404s, "find a new, trustworthy source to replace it. If you can't? Just remove the link entirely." Or write your own version of the lost resource [ch. 26].
- Link out only where it helps; if you cite a stat, you must link the study [ch. 16].

### Schema

- "Schema markup, or structured data markup, is behind-the-scenes code you add to your website to help search engines better understand your content." [ch. 22]
- What it does: "Gives search engines extra context: ... business hours, address, logo, service descriptions, product pricing, customer reviews ... Clarifies what kind of page it is: Whether it's a blog post, product page, service page, recipe, or FAQ ... Can lead to rich snippets: ... star ratings, pricing, event info, etc. Schema doesn't guarantee them, but it significantly boosts your chances." [ch. 22]
- How: "Don't worry about learning how to write Schema code manually. You can easily generate it with ChatGPT or a schema markup tool (that's what I do). To do this, paste your page URL or content into ChatGPT and ask it to write you schema markup for that page." [ch. 22]
- Reality check: "Rich snippets are starting to take a backseat in some cases ... Google can still feature your content in a rich snippet even if you don't have Schema markup on the page ... That said, adding Schema still gives you a better shot at standing out, so it's worth doing whenever you can." [ch. 22]

### Site speed and Core Web Vitals

- "even a 1-second delay feels like an eternity" and sends people back to Google [ch. 19]; slow sites raise bounce rate, which hurts rankings [ch. 19].
- Mobile-first indexing since 2016: Google tests the mobile version "using slower connection speeds. So if your site loads quickly on a low-end phone with bad signal, you're winning." [ch. 19]
- Speed is an amplifier, not a guarantee: "If speed alone guaranteed rankings, SEO would be a breeze." [ch. 19]
- Levers: "optimizing images, leveraging browser caching, reducing server response time, and cutting down on heavy scripts or plugins." [ch. 19]
- WordPress: remove unused plugins, consolidate; "install a plugin like WP Rocket"; better hosting ("Basic hosting on places like GoDaddy, Bluehost, and others might save you money upfront, but it's going to cost you in SEO later." Matt uses CrocWeb); next-gen images; CDN ("Faster load times, Reduced bandwidth costs, More reliable uptime, Extra layer of security") [ch. 19].
- Wix and Squarespace: "Optimize your images. Don't overload your pages with unnecessary videos, animations, or massive galleries." [ch. 19]
- Shopify: use .webp; a Fiverr speed specialist is acceptable [ch. 19].
- Core Web Vitals: LCP "Your main content should load within 2.5 seconds ... I would even argue that your main content should load faster than that."; INP "your site should respond in less than 200 milliseconds"; CLS "Your layout should stay steady while the page loads." [ch. 21]
- Measure with Google PageSpeed Insights (https://pagespeed.web.dev) as part of the mini audit [ch. 37].
- Embedded (not uploaded) video protects speed [ch. 14]; sliders slow the site [ch. 20]; images under 250KB [ch. 12].

### Mobile/responsive

- "Over 60% of internet traffic comes from mobile devices"; "A responsive website automatically adjusts to fit any screen size." Builders bake it in, but templates "still need a little fine-tuning" and desktop edits can break mobile [ch. 20].
- Test: "Pick up your phone and visit your website. (Or better yet, hand your phone to a parent or grandparent and watch what happens!)" Checklist, verbatim [ch. 20]:
  - "Is it immediately clear what your business does?"
  - "Is the text easy to read?"
  - "Does anything look cut off or go off the screen?"
  - "Does it load quickly?"
  - "Are the buttons big enough to tap easily?"
  - "Can someone call you with one click?"
  - "Can they find your contact page or checkout easily?"
- "go check out the top-ranked competitors in your area. How does their mobile experience compare to yours? Be brutally honest." [ch. 20]
- "A note for any web designers reading this: Make mobile experience your top priority. Desktop traffic is shrinking." [ch. 20]
- Ditch home page carousels/sliders [ch. 20].
- Non-responsive design raises bounce rates and slips rankings [ch. 20].

### Duplicate content

- Charlie's site stalled five months after a rebuild because "A plugin was injecting over 2,000 extra words onto every page... even when it wasn't activated," so "Search engines saw hidden content on every page" and "Every page looked nearly identical to Google's crawler." Rankings rose within months of removing it. [ch. 25]
- "Take duplicate content seriously. Like, really seriously. Even copying a few sentences across pages can confuse search engines or tank your rankings." [ch. 25]
- "Every page on your website should be unique and cover a specific topic. Not kinda-sorta different. Fully distinct." [ch. 25]
- Examples: fine: "Can You Bring an Apple Watch in a Hot Tub?" / "Can You Bring an iPhone into a Hot Tub?"; duplicate: "5 Top Restaurants in Hamilton" / "The 5 Best Restaurants in Hamilton, Ontario" [ch. 25]
- Consequence: Google "might: Choose one and ignore the others. Or worse, ignore all of them." Keyword cannibalization splits authority. Check in Google Search Console which pages rank for the same term. [ch. 25]
- Four fixes, verbatim [ch. 25]:
  1. "Merge content. If the pages are similar and neither one is ranking well, combine them into a single, stronger page. Delete any duplicate information. Don't do this if one of the pages is already ranking high; you might lose that spot. Instead, use a 301 redirect from the page that's not ranking."
  2. "Use 301 redirects. If you delete one version of a page, make sure to redirect it to the version you're keeping."
  3. "Update the content. Instead of merging or deleting, just make one of the pages different! Focus it on a new angle, a different subtopic, or a related search term."
  4. "Add canonical tags. If you have to keep duplicate or similar pages, you can tell search engines which version is the 'main' one by using canonical tags. You'll see this often on Shopify product pages with small variations."
- Unique title tags and meta descriptions on every page [ch. 4, 5]; reword headings borrowed from competitors [ch. 37]; never copy manufacturer product descriptions [ch. 38]; don't link externally to pages on your exact topic [ch. 10].
- Duplicate content is one of two reasons Google gives for not indexing a page [ch. 41].

### Broken links, crawl depth, orphan pages

- Broken links (404s) come from "You (or someone on your team) deleted a page or changed its URL, and forgot to set up a redirect" or "An external site updated or removed the page you were linking to." [ch. 26]
- Impact: poor UX, higher bounce, "Too many broken links tell search engines that your site might be outdated or neglected." [ch. 26]
- Find them with Ahrefs, Semrush, or the HeyTony Site Audit Tool; strategies, verbatim [ch. 26]: "1. Run Regular Site Audits ... 2. Swap or Remove. If you're linking to an external site that's now a 404, try to find a new, trustworthy source to replace it. If you can't? Just remove the link entirely ... 3. Turn Broken Links Into Backlinks. Find broken links on other websites you'd love a backlink from, and reach out. Offer your content as a replacement ... 4. Create Your Own Version. If a valuable external resource is gone, consider writing your own post on the same topic."
- Crawl depth: "how many clicks it takes to get to any given page from your home page ... Websites with shallow crawl depth, meaning most pages are reachable within three clicks from the home page, tend to rank better." [ch. 27]
- HeyTony crawl-depth rules: nav and footer carry main service/product page, blog, contact, plus pricing/case studies; home page links to each service or top product; home page links to blog categories (max 10); recent 5-10 posts on home page; every new page links to three pages and gets linked from three [ch. 27].
- Orphan pages: "a page that isn't linked to from any other page on your site ... users can't navigate to it, and search engines have a hard time discovering it." Find with an audit tool (https://getfoundbook.com/audit); fix by linking to them (2-3 out, update 2-3 older posts) [ch. 28].
- Exception: landing pages are orphan pages on purpose; exclude from sitemap and disallow in robots.txt [ch. 28].
- Robots.txt can steer crawlers away from low-value pages and hide orphan pages, but "99.99% of people will never need to touch their robots.txt file ... Just leave it alone." [ch. 24]
- Sitemap: submit once in GSC; auto-updates on publish; never submit individual URLs [ch. 23, 29].

### What to do if a page is not ranking (chapter 41)

- "1. Revisit Your Mini Audit. Head back to Chapter 37 and redo your mini audit ... If you've genuinely followed your roadmap and your content is more helpful, more detailed, and better optimized but you're still not ranking, ask yourself: Have you waited long enough? Search engines take time. Sometimes it takes 3-6 months, and in rare cases, even longer. I once had a page that took over a year to climb the rankings." [ch. 41]
- "2. Check If the Page Is Indexed. ... Open Google Search Console, paste the exact URL of your page into the search bar at the top, and hit 'Enter'." If not indexed: "Build backlinks to that page ... Internally link to that page from other pages on your site that are indexed (especially from pages that get traffic). Check Google's reasoning. If they give you a reason for not indexing the page, it's usually one of two things: 1. The content is duplicate. 2. The content is too thin (not helpful or too short)." [ch. 41]
- "3. Check for Over-Optimization. Yes, there's such a thing as too much SEO. The goal isn't to blow your competitors out of the water, it's to slightly outdo them. If their page has 1,000 words, write 1,100. If they use the keyword 5 times, aim for 6, not 16. If their site has a few internal links, add a few more, not 100. Too much optimization can trigger red flags." [ch. 41]
- "4. You Might Need More Authority. ... Keep blogging. Keep earning backlinks. Keep internally linking to the page." [ch. 41]
- "You Can't Win Them All ... Focus on growing your overall traffic. As long as your site is consistently gaining visitors, you're moving in the right direction." [ch. 41]
- If traffic drops after optimizing: "give it a few weeks. Rankings can fluctuate right after making changes." Then: "1. Roll the page back to the previous version (check your revision history). 2. Monitor the results. 3. If it rebounds, great! If not, try optimizing again, but this time, make small, incremental tweaks instead of overhauling everything at once." "Sometimes all it takes is a single well-placed keyword or a better headline to get back on track." [ch. 41]
- Related: "Usually, we start seeing SEO movement by month three, even if it's small." [ch. 25] If nothing by month five, dig into the code for hidden/duplicate content [ch. 25].

### The SEO horror story lesson (chapter 42)

- A CEO deleted the company's highest-traffic blog post because he "didn't think the content topic was close enough to their product (it was), and he wanted something that would drive instant conversions (impossible)." [ch. 42]
- Consequences of deleting a high-traffic page, verbatim [ch. 42]:
  1. "Lose Overall Traffic: This will likely impact their domain authority (or credibility score)."
  2. "Decrease Credibility: Their credibility going down will make it harder to rank for any keywords, especially the 'money' keywords."
  3. "Lose Topical Authority: Search engines will have a harder time seeing them as a topical authority."
  4. "Lose Link Equity: That blog post was linking to other blog posts on their site as well as to some of their main service pages. Since this page had a lot of credibility, it was 'sharing' some of that credibility with the pages it was linking to."
  5. "Lose Backlinks: Removing that URL got rid of any backlinks it had. All of the sites linking to that page (if they're doing SEO) will be notified that they're linking to a broken link ... They will remove those links and may even replace them with competitor links."
  6. "Miss Future Opportunities: Since that blog post was a helpful piece of content and it no longer exists, the client is missing out on future organic backlinking opportunities."
- "Checklist Before Removing a Page", verbatim [ch. 42]:
  1. "Backlinks Check: Does this page have backlinks? If it does, and they are good backlinks that you want to keep, don't remove the page. Instead, update the content."
  2. "Traffic Analysis: If the page does not have good backlinks, check if it's getting traffic organically. If it is, you may want to keep that page. Feel free to make some minor edits as that could impact its search rankings."
  3. "Final Warning: If you really don't want that page on your site despite it having good backlinks and / or getting a significant amount of traffic, know that it may make it harder for you to rank and you may even lose rankings across your entire site."
- Lesson: traffic pages feed money pages; update rather than delete; redirect (301) anything you must remove [ch. 25, 26, 42].

### Don'ts

- Don't leave your keyword off the page ("the number one reason people fail to rank") [ch. 3].
- Don't keyword-stuff anywhere: body, URL, subdirectories, alt text; don't hide keywords in background-colored text [ch. 3, 6, 13].
- Don't over-optimize: 1,100 not 2,000 words; 6 uses not 16; a few more internal links, not 100 [ch. 41].
- Don't reuse title tags or meta descriptions across pages [ch. 4, 5].
- Don't use more than one H1; don't jump from H1 to H3 [ch. 7].
- Don't use "click here," "learn more," "read more" anchors; don't link whole sentences or paragraphs [ch. 8].
- Don't put external links in the nav or footer; don't link out to competitors or to a page on your exact topic; don't use your target keyword as external anchor text [ch. 10].
- Don't publish walls of text with no bullets or images [ch. 11, 12].
- Don't use .png unless necessary; don't upload 10MB images; keep under 250KB [ch. 12, 19].
- Don't use the same stock photo every competitor uses [ch. 12].
- Don't upload video to your own server; embed instead [ch. 14].
- Don't copy-paste AI output straight to the site; add your experience and fact-check [ch. 2, 14, 33, 37].
- Don't copy manufacturer product descriptions; don't use invented product names as H1/title tag [ch. 38].
- Don't stop a category page at the product grid; don't create hundreds of near-identical category pages [ch. 39].
- Don't put all services on one page [ch. 40].
- Don't shove 1,000 words above the fold on a product page; don't make people scroll through text to reach the product grid [ch. 38, 39].
- Don't use home page carousels/sliders [ch. 20].
- Don't hide the blog from visitors [ch. 27]; don't bury pages more than three clicks deep [ch. 27]; don't leave orphan pages (except landing pages) [ch. 28].
- Don't have top and bottom sticky banners at the same time; don't rely on a plain "Subscribe to Newsletter" widget or a blog sidebar box [ch. 46].
- Don't delete a page with backlinks or traffic; update it, or 301 it if it must go [ch. 42, 25].
- Don't change or delete URLs without a 301 redirect [ch. 26].
- Don't touch robots.txt unless you know what you're doing; don't submit individual URLs to GSC; don't create the GSC account before the site is finished [ch. 24, 23, 29].
- Don't let plugins inject hidden content; verify published word count equals drafted word count [ch. 25].
- Don't send paid-ad clicks for a product to the home page [ch. 21].
- Don't overload Wix/Squarespace pages with videos, animations, galleries [ch. 19].
- Don't buy fake reviews, PBN links, link-farm links, or reciprocal links for rankings; don't use the Disavow tool unless Google says you have a manual action [ch. 16, 18].
- Don't chase time-sensitive keywords as a core strategy; favor evergreen [ch. 3].
- Don't give your business a supergeneric name if you can help it [ch. 3].
- Don't expect results in under 6-12 months; don't cut SEO when times are tight [Introduction, Part V intro].
